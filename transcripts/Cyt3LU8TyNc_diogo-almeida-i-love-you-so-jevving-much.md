## Key claims (synthesis — see raw transcript below)

1. **The "two cults" / jaggedness problem.** One cult reads benchmarks and has been
   announcing AGI-three-months-away for two years; the other looks at the real world
   and sees it essentially unchanged. Almeida's position is that *both* are looking at
   the same facts: AI is genuinely transformative **and** currently underwhelming. He
   asks for an Occam's-razor explanation of jaggedness — superhuman on millennium-prize
   math, useless at automating a drive-through.
2. **His own diagnosis: RLHF is part of the problem.** "I co-created RLHF and I do
   believe RLHF is part of the problem." The HF in RLHF literally put human-in-the-loop
   optimisation into every model, so ~100% of current LLMs are optimised for
   *assistance* (pleasing a human reader) rather than *automation* (making the decision
   and getting out of the way). Consequence: models are "too good to be true" at
   assistance and untrustworthy for background cognitive work. He argues automation is
   the larger prize.
3. **The "bitterest lesson" ladder.** Loose paraphrase of Sutton: scaling/compute beats
   algorithms → **data always matters more than compute** → and the *bitterest* lesson is
   **doing the right task**. Evidence: pretraining had to discover the right objective
   (next-token prediction beat masked language modelling not on the objective's own
   merits but on what it enabled); and in the original RLHF paper a GPT-2-sized model
   with the *right data/task* reached roughly "real GPT-9" instruction-following quality
   — a jump no amount of compute on the wrong task would buy (he estimates GPT-7–9 class
   would be needed to match a 2018 GPT-2 at instruction following without that data).
   Jev is presented as the same move applied to decision-making.
4. **Reliability, not speed/cost/interface, is why people love Jev.** "You get Einstein
   95% of the time but Mr. Bean 5% of the time — and if you can't tell which is which,
   how are you going to automate anything?" Jev's pitch is extreme narrow reliability so
   the intelligence is usable *every time*. He notes this does not show up on benchmarks
   and that the developer "ChatGPT moment" for Jev happened because a model that did not
   overpromise was itself the surprise. He explicitly invites others to "make sick
   models, please" even if they are not TypeSafe's.
5. **Jev as a fourth logic gate.** Software was built from three semantic primitives
   (greater-than / less-than / equal). Machine-native intelligence is "conceptually
   adding a fourth semantic logic gate" — programming with common sense, intention and
   human understanding. Analogy: the blue LED did not add 50% more use cases to red and
   green, it unlocked white light and a lighting revolution. So the capability gain from
   a semantic gate will be far larger than "33% more expressive software."
6. **"Software research" as the next direction.** A call for an early-internet-style
   exploration explosion of what software can do — real software engineering rather than
   vibe coding: decomposition, verifiable stages and real encapsulation, explicit state
   management "instead of assuming everything is global state within a KV cache." He says
   TypeSafe has raised money and wants to fund this but lacks the operational capacity.
7. **Concrete targets he lists:** the tyranny of the KV cache (why is memory O(n); why
   can't full coding-agent history always be included and map-reduced over); shared
   state across parallel subagents instead of each rediscovering the codebase;
   non-naive routing in subagents; principal–agent problems in society (we can only
   encode the letter, not the spirit, so fairness forces letter-over-spirit and creates
   loopholes/pay-to-win); real-time intelligence security (detecting the OpenAI/Hugging
   Face sandbox intrusion was *easier* than performing it — it failed on fast/cheap/
   reliable, not on intelligence); semantic dependent typing (run a Jev-like model in the
   linter to uphold semantic properties); new interfaces / "do what I mean" returning via
   agent software; generative UIs (teased, deferred to other talks); stopping slop.
8. **Root cause of AI slop.** Slop exists because AI is unreliable → it cannot be used
   for decisions that cost the deployer anything → so costs get externalised onto
   readers, which is where the asymmetric advantage of bad actors comes from. Reliability
   is therefore the anti-slop lever.
9. **Closing.** Thanks to "early believers"; reiterates his long-standing anti-benchmark
   stance ("a lot of people are screaming that benchmarks suck — they hurt the people who
   do not have time for anything other than benchmarks most"); ambition restated as making
   machine-native intelligence "as reliable as a fourth logic gate," which he calls a very
   high bar with a lot of work left.

## Caveats

- This is Almeida's **founder-side** account. The standing objections are not addressed
  in the transcript and remain open on the wiki: [[entities/sean-goedecke]]'s "no
  substantial technical moat" argument (the speed is reproducible by plugging any
  mid-sized model into a single-token inference stack; "can't hallucinate" is a semantic
  dodge on a constrained output space), and the independent Parallel Web Systems
  evaluation (comparable NDCG@10 on reranking, losses on large-label-set topic
  classification and suspected out-of-distribution freshness classification). See
  [[entities/typesafe-ai]].
- The "GPT-2 with RLHF data ≈ real GPT-9" figure is Almeida's own retrospective framing
  of the 2022 RLHF paper, not a community-accepted benchmark claim.
- He states "we have raised a bunch of money" without naming an amount, investors or a
  valuation.

My top-3 picks of AI news this week.

Daniela Amodei, co-founder and president of Anthropic / Annie Noelker via WIRED / The Signal NewsletterAnthropic1. Anthropic’s Office PartyAnthropic released faster, cheaper top models, put Claude into your video calls, and let teams build spreadsheets and whiteboards with it.

Fermat in 30 hours: Claude Fable 5.2 runs up to 30% faster than 5.1 and costs $8 per million input tokens and $40 output. Anthropic says it formalised the proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem in 30 hours.

Claude takes the call: Claude Meetings joins Google Meet calls as a visible participant, writes the notes and action items, and builds docs while you talk. It’s in beta on Team and Enterprise plans.

Spreadsheets and apps: Teams can now build spreadsheets and whiteboards with Claude, and partners like Adobe and Atlassian can embed their apps directly inside it.

Alex’s take: Anthropic continues to push the frontier, one model and application at a time. Fable 5.2 is an absolute powerhouse for your most challenging long-horizon knowledge work tasks, and Claude Meetings has made AI-in-meeting startups question their moat entirely. Meetings and Sheets have been Google and Microsoft’s playground for decades, however Claude is now starting to edge into their domain. If Claude drops an email client and meeting infrastructure, I’d definitely situate myself entirely on their stack, let alone the thousands of businesses that already rely on Anthropic’s intelligence. Simple is better, especially when that means having everything in one place.

Google opened up its strongest model, used it to prove the code behind Signal’s (the messaging app) encryption, and released a DNA model that reads each type of cell.

Carbon unlocked: Google’s strongest model, Gemini 4 Carbon, is now open to paying developers, businesses and AI Ultra subscribers, two weeks after a launch for cyber defenders only. Its introductory price doubles after 30 November.

Proving Signal’s maths: Google researchers used Carbon to write millions of lines of Lean, a language for checkable proofs, and proved key security properties of Signal’s encryption code in weeks. Google says this usually takes expert teams years.

A DNA model for each cell: AlphaGenome 2 predicts how DNA changes affect a chosen cell type, and finds nearly twice as many gene-changing variants as the first version. It’s for research use only.

Alex’s take: Google’s new models have been very strong on the benchmarks, however, I just can’t bring myself to using them in my day to day work. The “vibe” and “feel” of Anthropic’s models very much make you feel at home in comparison to a more sanitised feeling from the likes of Google and OpenAI. Don’t get me wrong, both earn their place in my stack: take, for instance, Gemini Live, where I can point my phone’s camera at something in front of me and I get step-by-step instructions on what to do next. Or OpenAI’s Image model, generating things which Claude does not specialise in. But for a daily driver LLM, there’s a reason I still pick Opus 5.5 on High reasoning mode, every single time.

OpenAI let its agents pay for things, let brands set up shop inside ChatGPT, and folded its robotics team into its main research group.

A wallet for your agent: ChatGPT Wallet lets OpenAI’s always-on agents, called dots, buy things for you. You approve each purchase with Face ID or a fingerprint, and the shop never sees your real card number. It’s for US Pro users only.

Brands move in: Businesses can now claim their profile in ChatGPT and, in the US, launch a Business Agent that answers shoppers’ questions. Brands can also pay to sponsor that agent as an ad.

Robots go general: OpenAI broke up its stand-alone robotics team and moved its researchers under research chief Mark Chen. It says its general models can now run robots directly.

Alex’s take: The part I’m paying close attention to is the idea of sponsored agents. Brands can now pay to put their own agent in front of you in ChatGPT. When a brand comes up in a ChatGPT answer, you can now tap through to its profile and, if it has a Business Agent, start a separate chat to ask it about its products. Brands can also pay for a ChatGPT ad that leads to that agent, and those ads sit at the bottom of an answer when what you’re asking about matches something they sell. For now, Business Agents are only in the US, but we must remember why we want to use AI in the first place—to be maximally truth-seeking. How can it be if what it serves you is paid for? ChatGPT does label its ads as sponsored, just like Google, but a label only helps when you’re the one reading it. Once an agent shops for you on your behalf, you may never see the ads, or the options it passed over.

Loading...Content I EnjoyedAnthropic’s seven co-founders / IPO roadshow video via AnthropicAnthropic’s 42-minute pitch to Wall StreetOn 10 November, Anthropic started its roadshow, which you can watch here.

The tour is where a company sells its shares to big investors before it lists on the public markets. It plans to sell 825 million shares at $70 to $78 each, and I thought it would be worthwhile to dive into the numbers.

Over the last six quarters, Anthropic says revenue grew more than 45x while its computing costs grew 10x. Its gross margin (how much of every $1 of sales it keeps after paying to deliver it) is over 85% so far this year, up from 29% a year earlier. It also claims net revenue retention of about 900%, which means a customer who spent $1 a year ago now spends about $9. Even among its ten biggest customers, “it’s still less than 1% of their total expense line.”

What I really want to talk about is Anthropic’s compute dependencies. So far, it seems to me that Anthropic has run an infra-light model, renting most of its computing power rather than owning it. Compared to hyperscalers like SpaceX, Google, Meta and Microsoft, Anthropic’s models are head and shoulders above the rest, yet it doesn’t own the entire stack, so it can’t lean on economies of scale up and down it. How they’ve managed to pull this off amazes me.

That makes its SpaceX deal a massive risk factor. Anthropic’s filing says it could pay SpaceX up to $120.5 billion for computing power through 2029, about $3.1 billion a month once it’s all switched on. But after early 2027, either side can walk away on 90 days’ notice. Across all its suppliers, Anthropic has signed about $901 billion of infrastructure commitments.

So will we see Anthropic build data centres and hardware of its own? The roadshow suggests it has already started. Anthropic now leases chips and runs them in data centres built just for it, which it calls “Anthropic Direct”, and expects that to make up a growing share of its computing over the coming years.

Patrick Collison, co-founder of Stripe / Phillip Faraone/Getty ImagesNobody can describe the good AI future yetPatrick Collison, who co-founded the payments company Stripe, posted a thought last weekend that has stuck with me. We can easily picture what could go wrong with AI, he wrote, but the good version is strangely blurry.

Cured diseases and more stuff for everyone sound great, but he calls them “just ingredients”. What he wants is the recipe, and he asks some rather thought-provoking questions which nobody has yet answered:

Work: Do people still work? If they do, why, and what do they add?

Scarcity: If they don’t, how do we share things out, and what is still scarce?

Control: How many AIs will there be for each human, and are humans still in charge?

Speed: Nobody in 1700 could picture 1900 either, but that change came slowly enough to fix mistakes along the way. This one may not.

I think this week gave us a perfect example. Anthropic’s roadshow promised to end cancer within a decade, which is a wonderful ingredient. But it said nothing about what a nurse, a lawyer or a newsletter writer does on a Tuesday in 2035. My guess is that the answer has to be built from the bottom up, job by job, by people who actually do the work.

My suggestion: write one paragraph describing a good day in your job ten years from now, with AI doing most of today’s tasks. If you can’t picture it, list everything you did last week and cross out what AI could already do. What ends up being left is the work to hone in on and prioritise getting better at. Bonus points if it’s stuff you actually enjoy.

Loading...Quote to ShareOn 9 November, Jim Fan, Nvidia’s director of robotics, opened his keynote at the Conference on Robotic Learning (CoRL), in Austin. He described how roboticists reacted when OpenAI’s GPT-6 Astra turned out to be good at controlling robots, walking through five stages of grief:

“Depression with a wine bottle. My PhD is now a joke in the system prompt.”

Watch the clip (from CoRL’s livestream, which Jim Fan shared on X).

I think the reason this joke landed so well is that it’s actually true for a lot of people in that room. Years of specialist work is now something a general model can do with the right instructions.

But the point that Fan makes, which I also believe to be true, is that neither Astra nor Nvidia’s own models can do the job alone.

He uses the idea of System 1 and System 2, which the psychologist Daniel Kahneman made famous in Thinking, Fast and Slow. System 2 is slow, careful thinking, like planning a route on a map. System 1 is the fast reflex that catches a glass before it hits the floor.

In Fan’s setup, a big model like Astra plays System 2 and works out what a messy scene means. Nvidia’s new GR00T 2 plays System 1, running on the robot itself and handling the physics, “free of the tyranny of conference Wi-Fi.” Together they solved the Tower of Hanoi puzzle without any training on it, and Fan says GR00T 2’s weights will be open-sourced in December.

Ultimately, the specialist’s moat has moved. I think we’ve seen this in a lot of knowledge work as a whole, where a skill you might be “proud of”, say financial modelling, creating pivot tables, or compiling a compelling set of slides, all of a sudden becomes second nature to AI models today, all of which they can now do in minutes.

So it’s worth asking which part of your job is the reflex and which part is the judgement—something that only you can do. This means the connecting, the triangulating, the reasoning, the analysis, the context where LLMs fall short and only the human (currently) has the oversight to see.

“What is going to happen to Jensen Huang?”

On Friday I wrote up the whole saga of Jensen Huang’s exit from Nvidia, and asked whether the board would bring him back. A reader (who I think is brilliant) replied to tell me I was “looking down the wrong end of the telescope”. Everyone is asking what happens to Nvidia, he said. The better question is what a wealthy 63-year-old genius with decades ahead of him does next.

I thought it was an excellent lens, so let’s explore what that might look like.

On Friday, Brad Gerstner of Altimeter and Gavin Baker of Atreides, who hold about $2.13 billion of Nvidia shares between them, sent the board a letter asking for a slow, deliberate search that leaves “the potential return of Jensen Huang” on the table. Baker backs the petition to reinstate Jensen right away, though the letter itself ultimately concedes the final call does in fact belong to the board.

Reuters has also reported that Jensen allegedly misled Nvidia’s general counsel about his relationship with Alice Huang, which makes a return much harder. And three House committees have summoned him to a closed-door interview on 1 December.

Jensen owns about 3.6% of Nvidia, knows every chip buyer on earth, and lives in California, where non-compete clauses generally can’t stop him from starting a rival.

I actually think an interesting path starts with Elon Musk. Zero Hedge on X asked him to hire Jensen, to which Musk replied: “That would be an honor.” As of Thursday, traders on Kalshi put the odds of Jensen joining a Musk company at 27%.

Assuming Nvidia doesn’t take Jensen back, I see a great outcome as Jensen running Terafab, Musk’s semiconductor fabrication plant and chip factory valued at $25 billion. Then, there could be an angle here to open-source Grok (SpaceXAI’s AI model), which would place an awful lot of pressure on the frontier labs, leading to better outcomes for SpaceX given they’re not direct competitors and instead are the ones focusing on the lower layers of the stack (energy, chips, infrastructure) which the other players rely on.

As Elon often says, “the most entertaining outcome is the most likely.” I’d love to see it, but there’s an obvious catch. Jensen built Nvidia on designing chips, while companies like TSMC make them, therefore having him run the factory isn’t an obvious fit.

What’s more, SpaceX does a lot of work for the US government, which may be the hardest employer of all to join while Congress is asking about a suspected spy.

Yet everything hangs on one thing, which everyone’s still waiting for an answer on. Whether any secrets did actually reach Beijing.

If they did, no government will let him near advanced chips again. If they didn’t, investors will be queuing to fund whatever he builds next.

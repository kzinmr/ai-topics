# Position: Stop Anthropomorphizing Intermediate Tokens as Reasoning/Thinking Traces!

*Subbarao Kambhampati, Karthik Valmeekam, Siddhant Bhambri, Vardhan Palod, Lucas Saldyt, Kaya Stechly, Soumya Rani Samineni, Durgesh Kalwar, Upasana Biswas — arXiv:2504.09762, submitted 14 April 2025*

## Core claim

Intermediate token generation (ITG) — where a model produces output before the solution — has become a standard method to improve LLM performance on reasoning tasks. These intermediate tokens have been called "reasoning traces" or "thinking traces," implicitly anthropomorphizing them and implying they resemble steps a human would take solving a problem, providing an interpretable window into the model's "thinking."

**Position**: this anthropomorphization is not a harmless metaphor — it is dangerous. It confuses the nature of these models and how to use them effectively, and leads to questionable research. The authors call on the community to stop anthropomorphizing intermediate tokens.

## Key arguments

1. **No causal theory exists.** There is no theory drawing causal connections between the semantics of intermediate tokens and the final solution — beyond the basic understanding that intermediate tokens change the conditional distribution of solution tokens. Any interpretable meaning in the traces may be a fortuitous coincidence of rationales being present in training data, not the model performing internal computations corresponding to those statements. The correlation may be **spurious rather than causal**.

2. **Empirical evidence against user-facing semantics.** The authors survey recent literature showing that intermediate-token semantics correlate with performance only weakly or spuriously, and that forcing traces into natural-language format does not improve — and can degrade — solution quality. The **DeepSeek R1 case study** is highlighted: when DeepSeek re-trained their original R1-Zero model (which produced a mix of English and Chinese intermediate tokens) with a costly supervised fine-tuning phase on carefully curated English intermediate tokens, **solution accuracy worsened** without any measured improvement in the validity of the intermediate tokens. Stylistically-plausible traces are not more valid traces.

3. **Engendered false trust.** Deliberately making intermediate tokens appear more human-like is dangerous because it engenders false confidence and trust in solutions users may not be capable of directly verifying. "The last thing we want to do is to design powerful AI systems that potentially exploit the cognitive flaws of users to convince them of the validity of incorrect answers."

4. **Call to action**:
   - Stop assuming (or looking for) end-user semantics in intermediate tokens.
   - Human interpretation of intermediate tokens should **not** be used as a proxy measure for the trustworthiness of solutions.
   - Where trust in the final solution is needed, it should come from **verification of the correctness of the solution itself** — by end users, third parties, or problem-class-specific verifiers.
   - Free research to optimize intermediate tokens **only for solution accuracy** — even if they no longer look like plausible language utterances. The authors point to evidence that non-linguistic intermediate representations (arbitrary vectors in embedding space, not tied to vocabulary items) can further improve solution accuracy.

5. **Reframing — intermediate tokens as learned prompt augmentations.** The authors' main constructive contribution: view intermediate tokens as **prompt augmentations** rather than reasoning. For a task prompt T, there may exist an augmentation PA such that P(Sol(LLM(T + PA), T)) > P(Sol(LLM(T), T)). The challenge is learning the Skolem function PA = f_θ(T, LLM) that maps each task to an effective augmentation — which is what long-chain-of-thought models do, but the semantics of the augmentation are incidental, not essential. This framing (i) removes the interpretability pressure, (ii) explains why trace style is decoupled from validity, and (iii) opens the door to non-linguistic "thinking" tokens.

## Why it trended (August 2026 context)

The 14-month-old position paper hit the HN front page on **August 19, 2026** (250 pts / 194 comments) as the industry debates the reliability of "thinking" features shipped in consumer products (ChatGPT's reasoning-effort slider, Claude extended thinking, o-series/GPT-5.x thinking traces). The paper supplies a formal, peer-circulated objection: the "thinking traces" shown to users are at best correlated with, and at worst decoupled from, the model's actual solution-finding process — with the R1-Zero English/Chinese trace experiment as the canonical counterexample.

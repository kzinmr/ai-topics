# John Ternus Is Engineering a New Era for Apple

Source: https://time.com/collection/best-inventions/2026/apple-iphone-duo/

This thesis was already borne out earlier this year, when a wave of AI enthusiasts
bought Mac Minis
in order to set up personal AI agents through the open-source project OpenClaw.
While Apple had nothing to do with the project, users quickly figured out that the energy-efficient Apple silicon, compact form, and unified memory of the $600 computers allowed them to run AI workloads locally, continuously, and for minimal electricity costs. This spike in demand caused some Mac Mini models to be
unavailable for months
.
More AI-oriented hardware products are on the way. On Oct. 13, Apple announced a new
smart-home hub
called the HomeView, powered by Apple intelligence. Bloomberg also reported the company is readying
smart glasses
for 2027; the company declined to comment.
But Apple will soon face more competition in the hardware space. Last year, OpenAI acquired the startup of Jony Ive, Apple’s longtime chief design officer, who is now building AI-first devices for the ChatGPT maker alongside several other prominent former Apple veterans. In July, Apple sued OpenAI in federal court, alleging the theft of hardware trade secrets. (OpenAI calls the lawsuit “baseless.”)
Despite the lawsuit, Apple executives say they’re not worried about the newfound rivals. After all, Apple devices have never been more popular. With 2.5 billion active devices, the company rakes in nearly half a trillion dollars in annual revenue. And when new users convert to Apple, they tend to stick around. “We don’t feel threatened,” says Joswiak, the longtime executive. “The fact that we can do the integration that we can means we can create just a better experience than anybody else.”
Joswiak adds that Apple isn’t making products for the die-hard AI believers chasing superintelligence. “We love them, and they can use it too, but that’s not our target,” he says. “It’s how to make it better for everyday people.”
On Sept. 18, lines formed outside Apple stores from Hangzhou to London to Mumbai, with scores of those “everyday people” spending hundreds of their hard-earned dollars on the newest iPhone. Ternus, as Tim Cook and Steve Jobs did before him, showed up to the Fifth Avenue store to take pictures with superfans, who treated him like a movie star. Most of them probably didn’t know much about Ternus himself. But they didn’t need to, because they saw him -reflected through devices that had become omnipresent in their lives.
AI may yet be a part of their lives too. But no one knows the future—and at present, handheld devices are still king. “You can’t do everything,” Ternus says. “So we kind of try to constrain ourselves to the stuff that matters most—and then put all of our effort into those things.”

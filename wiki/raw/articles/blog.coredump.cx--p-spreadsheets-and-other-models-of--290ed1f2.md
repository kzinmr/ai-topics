# Spreadsheets and other models of morality

Source: https://blog.coredump.cx/p/spreadsheets-and-other-models-of

This will be my last article on philosophy. I always tell prospective subscribers to expect the unexpected; I also feel that
the series
presented interesting problems and covered them in entertaining ways. That said, looking at email and web traffic statistics, I concede defeat.
To end the series with a bang, let’s talk about morality. Morality can be understood as the rules that govern virtuous conduct toward others. The word
ethics
is sometimes used in the same sense, but the latter can encompass more abstract personal standards — hence,
“strong work ethic”
for an employee who works harder and is more dependable than most.
Don’t worry: I’ll keep this short. In particular, I won’t attempt to sum up several millennia of moral thought and won’t bore you with the epistemology of moral rules (truths or vibes?).  Instead, we’ll take a look at what it means to be a moral agent, and what what tools are at our disposal to tell right from wrong.
Morality, as it’s commonly understood, is predicated on the ability to choose. This presupposes the existence of free will: if you’re not
actually
choosing, it’s difficult to talk about making the right choice.
As discussed in earlier articles, free will is a metaphysical position; there’s no robust basis for it in the known laws of physics. You must either take it on faith or reject it in favor of scientific materialism (
physicism
). In the latter case, human behavior appears to be predetermined or governed by chance. In both cases, only a degenerate form of morality can survive: a mechanical, evolutionary impulse to cull the behaviors that negatively impact the fitness of the group. These impulses are not rooted in any higher notion of good or evil; the only question is whether they achieve their goals.
This metaphysics-free morality lends itself to a number of abhorrent conclusions — more about that later — but more to the point, you can’t change anything, so why not close the tab and watch some cat videos instead? (Trick question! That’s also out of your hands.)
More seriously, you’ll never catch a proponent of scientific materialism sticking to their guns in real life; when it’s time to order at the restaurant, they flip through the menu and say
“I’ll have pepperoni pizza”
, not
“cosmos, take the wheel”
. They may insist it’s just habitual pantomime, but if the pantomime is essential in day-to-day activities, why wouldn’t it be essential in the discussion of morality? In other words, even if free will is a lie, maybe it’s a necessary one?…
If we assume free will, moral responsibility hinges on the
control principle:
the idea that you’re on the hook, in the moral sense, only if you meaningfully control the outcome of an action. First, you must have the ability to do otherwise. Second, you must be aware of the situation, have the ability to anticipate its consequences, and to evaluate alternative choices.
Although the idea is elegant, the control principle poses many practical difficulties. For example, if you drive while intoxicated, you probably lack the mental acuity to evaluate risk. Still, most people would argue that this doesn’t absolve you of moral responsibility: you should have thought about what’s going to happen before you had your first beer. But then, what about severe alcoholism or drug addiction, where the last opportunity to think clearly might have been years before?
A more thought-provoking wrench in the works is the concept of
moral luck
: that is, the possibility that moral outcomes may differ solely due to factors outside the agent’s control. To illustrate, imagine Mr. A, a husband who loathes his wife, decides to poison her, and succeeds. Next, take Mr. B — a man of the same disposition, except less component; the poison he concocts is harmless and the spouse suffers no ill effects. Does Mr. B bear the same moral responsibility as Mr. A?
Further, let’s consider Mr. C; once again, he hatches the same plan, but his incompetence is so vast that he fails to even procure a recipe, thus derailing the rest of the scheme. Finally, imagine Mr. D — a man with the same mean streak, yet saved from having homicidal thoughts by hooking up with a more agreeable spouse. In a sense, all four gentlemen share the same moral flaw, but only one of them is a murderer.
Perhaps the simplest rebuke to this argument is to pragmatically assert that we don’t know the strength of one’s commitment to moral action until they carry it out. In particular, if free will exists, we can’t
truly
know what Mr. C or Mr. D would do until they get to the finish line.
Another strategy is to draw a sharper distinction between moral character and legal culpability; the latter is often retributive or reparatory in nature, so it’s strongly influenced by the realized harm. In contrast, perhaps moral judgments can be directed toward intent or anticipated conduct, even if the worst (or best) never comes to pass.
So far, we’ve talked about what makes one eligible to be judged for their actions or desires; this is separate from the standard we ought to judge them by.
In Ancient Greece, great emphasis was placed on virtues: a collection of positive attributes that exemplified
eudemonia,
a good life. The list varied over time, but generally included noble deeds, fidelity, friendship, family, beauty, and the pursuit of knowledge and skill — concepts that we still value today.
Later philosophical thought in the West gravitated more strongly toward
deontological ethics
(from Greek
déon
: that which is necessary). These systems favored explicit obligations over general character traits; the rules variously originated from God, from abstract moral intuition, or from the observations of nature.
The best known abstract theory comes from Immanuel Kant, who offered a sort of a singular prime directive (
categorical imperative
) for all rational agents to act for the betterment of humanity — as if your individual actions were to become universal law, with careful consideration of the future they create. There’s a lot of nuance to Kant’s theory of morality that we won’t cover here, but the imperative bears some semblance to the common Christian saying of
“do unto others as you would have them do unto you”
.
A more practically useful collection of obligations can be found in the writings of William David Ross; his list includes the duty to keep promises, to make amends for wrongful acts, to repay kindness, to not harm others, and to seek greater good. Another familiar negative (“don’t do”) collection are the seven cardinal sins in the Catholic doctrine: gluttony, lust, greed, wrath, sloth, vanity, and pride.
“Mankind’s Eternal Dilemma”, Frans Fracken (II) (1633)
The most significant problem with deontology is that it speaks in absolutes: for example, if violence is wrong, then so might be an attempt to defend oneself against assault. Second, the rules are vague enough that they’re difficult to apply: you can read many philosophical treatises and still not be sure if it’s OK to key your neighbor’s car if they park in your spot.
A simple but inelegant benchmark is to evaluate moral actions by imagining the response they would elicit from an impartial observer; this is known as
sentimentalism.
This approach doesn’t reject normative morality, but treats it as a scaffolding, with some of the finer details to be decided by human judgment. The main problem with sentimentalism is that it’s hard to imagine an ideal observer who isn’t at least to some extent influenced by prevailing cultural attitudes or the presentation of the case. However, there’s little hope for formalizing all of morality in normative terms, so perhaps this is the best we can do.
Utilitarianism, also known as
consequentialism
, posits that moral judgments should be made by analyzing likely outcomes and then choosing the course of action that stands to maximize the overall happiness or well-being of everyone.
In its mildest and most pragmatic flavor, utilitarianism doesn’t reject existing moral frameworks; instead, it helps resolve dilemmas that arise when different obligations come into serious conflict. For example, in the utilitarian view, it’s fine to lie to save someone’s life. This flavor of utilitarianism is also called
threshold deontology
: we stick with moral absolutes up to a point, but if the outcome is sufficiently repugnant, we start looking for a socially tolerable middle ground.
That said, there exists another version of this ideology, sometimes called
hedonistic utilitarianism
or
hedonistic value theory.
This variant rejects all the external, touchy-feely moral obligations in favor of a single
utility function
— that is, a purportedly objective mathematical formula that allows us to calculate how happy or fulfilled everyone is. In this view, “right” and “wrong” have no deeper meaning, except as directions in which the needle moves on the planetary utility gauge. Although the association is not exclusive, hedonistic utilitarianism is favored by a number of scientific rationalists because it aligns with the position that, in the absence of free will, morality is simply a mechanism to eliminate that which may have a negative impact of the well-being of the herd.
The assumption that all moral outcomes are numerically comparable and interchangeable is notoriously problematic. A classic example is the transplant argument: in the interest of increasing overall well-being, it seems fair to kidnap an unwilling donor, kill them, and harvest their organs if these organs could be used to save five other lives. More broadly, the same argument structure can be used to make a case for the discreet removal of any cohort with a sufficiently negative expected utility: suspected baby Hitlers, the infirm, the insane, the adulterers, the gossipers, and so on.
Even more outrageously, hedonistic utilitarianism allows you to argue that the best way to maximize overall happiness is to build a planetary-scale computer in which trillions of simulated minds are kept in a state of perpetual bliss. In fact, don’t we have an moral obligation to build such a computer, even if it means stripping Earth of all resources and jeopardizing the well-being of billions of people alive today?…
In a more practical sense, the ideology maximally embodies the saying that the end justifies the means. Let’s count chickens as sentient beings — a sensible proposition, perhaps at a conversion rate that equates the suffering of 1,000 chickens to the suffering of one human. If so, forget petitioning for change or developing meat substitutes; utilitarianism practically
begs
animal right activists to urgently firebomb farms and murder industry executives to stop the industrial-scale slaughter of living things. In the same vein, many AI maximalists hold that we have a moral duty to develop superhuman intelligence at any human cost because it could bring nearly-infinite happiness to future beings — be it of the meat or silicon kind.
You’d think that these thought experiments would relegate hedonistic utilitarianism to the fringes, but there are countless people in my industry who embrace it, interpreting the discrepancies with moral intuition as a sign of their own ascension to a higher plane of consciousness.
I don’t object to utilitarianism being used with care to evaluate the societal necessity of violating moral principles, for example in the context of warfare or self-defense. That said, I think it’s wrong to treat it as the source of morality. A potential compromise solution is that deontology should have robust jurisdiction over the agent-relative realm, while utilitarianism could perhaps rule the agent-neutral (societal) space. This implies that some decisions — such as the transplant donor kidnapping case — may be in some sense socially desirable, but that as a matter of personal principles, no one should feel encouraged to kidnap and murder anyone.
“Vanitas”, Franciscus Gysbrechts (circa 1674)

The first widely read piece of tech criticism I ever wrote was "Metacrap: Putting the torch to the seven strawmen of the meta-utopia," a response to the underlying philosophy of the "Semantic Web" that I published in 2001:
https://people.well.com/user/doctorow/metacrap.htm
The piece holds up surprisingly well today (if I do say so myself), and not just for its criticism of the ontological incoherence of "web semantics." The most relevant concept I develop in that piece is a criticism of any plan that starts with "first, everyone else in the world just needs to set aside their needs and advantages in favor of
my
priorities, and
then
my plan will work."
This is especially relevant today in the context of "agentic AI." When this idea was first mooted, the big idea was that you could instruct a chatbot to venture out onto the public, unstructured web and it would do your bidding, for example, finding all the flights that fit your criteria and comparing prices for all of them, picking the best one and booking it for you.
This idea quickly foundered. Each step in that process was too complex and specialized for a single chatbot; so the notion quickly gave way to "swarms" of specialized chatbots – one to search the web and identify likely travel websites; one to search those websites; one to evaluate flights against your criteria, one to handle the transaction, and so on.
Through specialization, these bots could be made
far
more accurate, but at a price: both the literal price of all the extra computation that goes into powering a "swarm" versus a single process, and the reliability price that arises from complexity. That's because error rates are multiplicative: combine two bots with low failure rates into a single system and that system will have a
higher
failure rate than any of its components. This compounding error rate only increases as you add more and more bots, so every time you add a new specialized bot to decrease the error rate in its area of specialization, you
increase
the overall error rate of the system by adding more complexity to it.
Despite this, companies pressed on ahead with "agentic," without any regard to the realities or consequences. For example, Microsoft has repeatedly announced that it will modify Windows to eliminate all forms of PC-side privacy and confidentiality, sending a continuous stream of screenshots to Microsoft's cloud servers so that your Microsoft "agent" can access your private data on Microsoft's cloud:
https://pluralistic.net/2026/01/06/1000x-liability/#graceful-failure-modes
But the real fix for "agentic" is for everyone in the world to rearrange their affairs to the benefit of AI agents. This is the same demand that the Semantic Web people made in the early oughts, and it has the same problems. The Semantic Web people operated on the assumption that there were freestanding, objectively correct ways of describing things and that everyone who put information on the web would eventually come to see that adopting these correct semantics was to everyone's advantage.
In reality, these semantic differences are the expression of real, serious, unresolvable philosophical, technical, moral and political arguments. The reason that some media outlets won't describe trans women as women isn't because they lack an appreciation of objectively correct semantic classifications – it's because they're bigots. That's the same reason "race realists" insist that there is a biological category called "race," that exists despite everything computational genomics has proved:
https://pluralistic.net/2020/08/16/combat-wheelchairs/#race-realism
In my book
The Reverse Centaur's Guide to Life After AI
, I predicted that a similar dynamic would play out when it came to agentic AI and websites. The reason websites are so inhospitable to bots is that ecommerce sites
don't want to be legible to automated tools
:
https://us.macmillan.com/books/9780374621568/thereversecentaursguidetolifeafterai/
Ecommerce websites deploy a wide range of sleazy tactics to raise prices, including surveillance pricing:
https://pluralistic.net/2026/10/07/gouged/#aeropain
They use market power to rig whole categories, forcing prices up:
https://pluralistic.net/2026/10/15/enshittipedia/
Multiple law-enforcers, Republic and Democrat, state and federal, have sued companies for "drip pricing" and "junk fees" that make it impossible to know what the actual price is until you've spent a long-ass time going through the purchase process:
https://pluralistic.net/2024/06/07/drip-drip-drip/#drip-off
Surveillance pricing, drip pricing, junk fees, price fixing and "most favored nation" deals are the source of
all
the growth in profitability by the economy's largest, most important firms. These tactics
require
opacity and are antithetical to the kinds of automated comparison-shopping that agentic peddlers promise:
https://pluralistic.net/2024/08/14/the-price-is-wright/#enforcement-priorities
These tactics are facially illegal and despite this, they are widely practiced. Cheating is so profitable that it's cheaper for firms to buy off regulators and pay fines than it is for them to stop criming. The fantasy that these same companies would welcome agentic shopping bots with open arms is just as implausible as the idea that if we feed enough words to the word-guessing program, it will wake up and become a "super intelligence":
https://pluralistic.net/2026/09/18/surprise/#wow-signal
What's more, the AI companies
know this
. Google's big plan to make AI profitable is to sell merchants the power to combine its surveillance data with its AI tools to price-gouge shoppers:
https://pluralistic.net/2026/01/21/cod-marxism/#wannamaker-slain
And right on time, ecommerce websites are mass-blocking AI agents:
https://gizmodo.com/online-shopping-platforms-turn-against-third-party-ai-agents-2000831298
They're doing it in part because AI agents allow shoppers to cheat (for example, by renewing a "temporary" hold on a used item to block other buyers indefinitely), but also because
they don't want automation tools on their platforms at all
. These tools are a frontal assault on all the most exciting (and sleaziest) ways to make money in the waning hours of end-stage capitalism. Far from welcoming bots, ecommerce sites were always going to block them.
It's easy to come up with amazing applications for technology if you're allowed to assume that you can get everyone else in the world to set aside
their
priorities and
their
interests in favor of
yours
. But unless someone has a plan to do that, we shouldn't take them seriously.

# The 50-Yard Line

Source: https://daringfireball.net/2026/11/the_50-yard_line

The 50-Yard Line
Wednesday, 18 November 2026
Ever since the Huang/Nvidia scandal erupted, I’ve been thinking about a short post I wrote back in March, under the headline “
Jensen Huang Doesn’t Smell Anything
”:
Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, during an on-stage interview at The Hill
& Valley Forum
last summer
, was asked “What do you see as America’s
unique advantages that other countries don’t have?”
His answer, after taking a moment to think, “America’s unique
advantage that no country could possibly have is President Trump.”
Huang, newly appointed to the
aforelinked
President’s Council
of Advisors on Science and Technology, seemingly
doesn’t smell
the growing stink
.
The stink was the historically low (and sinking) popularity to which Trump’s 2.0 presidency had sunk. Undeniable to anyone in touch with reality. Huang’s obsequious Trump-fellating answer to that question would have been eye-catching even when Trump first rolled back into office with an aggressive agenda and a surplus of political capital from a clear election victory — but it was, frankly, a startling thing to say at any point. It makes a lot more sense, though,
given what’s come to light in the last 10 days
.
If you’re not paying close attention you might think Huang’s was the sort of answer any major tech CEO might have given. But it’s not. That was a really unusual thing for Huang to say, way out of line with how corporate leaders typically approach politics. The typical approach for large publicly held corporations is to avoid partisan politics. Advocate only for policies directly related to the company’s business, and otherwise stay out. The political winds change direction every few years. George Bush senior in 1988 was the last new president elected from the same party as the outgoing incumbent, and he only lasted one term. It makes no sense for a corporation that intends to continue thriving for more than the next few years to closely align itself with one party — unless they believe that party will remain in power for the foreseeable future, à la the FDR/Truman Democrats, who won five consecutive presidential elections from 1932 to 1948 (and seven out of nine through 1968).
One metaphor I use for politics is a football field. Democrats on the left, Republicans on the right. CEOs typically stay as close as they can to the 50-yard line in the middle of the field. That way, when an election puts the other party in power, they don’t have to move — they just need to turn around. That’s how someone like Tim Cook could maintain good relations with the White House from Obama to Trump to Biden back to Trump again. He just stayed close to midfield and turned around after each election.
Remaining as close as possible to political midfield under Trump required unseemly obsequious tribute, unprecedented in modern U.S. history.
Million-dollar tickets
to his inauguration.
That ridiculous gold trophy
. Embarrassing stuff. But that’s ultimately
a reflection of Trump’s avarice
, not the politics of Cook personally or Apple as a company. That was the price to stay on the 50-yard line. Cook has often repeated that his two personal heroes are
Martin Luther King Jr. and Bobby Kennedy
(senior, to be clear). He keeps portraits of both of them
in his office
. He never swapped them out for portraits of, say, Ronald Reagan. Apple never changed its internal policies or language regarding racial and LGBT equality. The Apple homepage never stopped celebrating Martin Luther King Jr. Day —
even when it coincided with Trump’s second inauguration
.
And yet Trump remains so enamored of Cook that he
floated his name
to succeed Huang at Nvidia.
Tim Apple
remains the apple of Trump’s eye. Yet Apple had nothing to change or apologize for when Biden came into office, nor will they again if, as now
seems likely
, Trump is succeeded in 2029 by a Democrat elected to clean up his kakistocratic kleptomaniacal mess. (Presumably Apple will gladly pony up at least
another $25 million
to restore the East Wing after Trump’s
architecturally ungodly ballroom edifice
is torn down.) And Apple certainly won’t need lawyers to defend them from charges of corruption and/or breaches of national security. Nvidia, not so much.
This is what we should want: business leaders who endeavor to stay out of partisan politics, not hopelessly enmesh themselves in it. Industry and politics cannot operate in isolation, but they should avoid intertwinement except where necessary. Great companies stand the test of time. That’s not feasible if you hitch your corporate wagon to one party or the other, no matter the political moment. We don’t think of Procter & Gamble, Coca-Cola, or Berkshire Hathaway as being Democratic or Republican companies. You cannot chart their fortunes over time based on which party was in power in any given decade. You may well have strong political opinions that are far from the 50-yard line, on one side or the other. I do. But you shouldn’t look for those opinions to be validated by industry executives, any more than you should look for business and product-design decisions to be made by elected government officials. True American values put country over party when it comes to business. And, as Calvin Coolidge aptly put it, “The chief business of the American people is business.”
America does possess unique advantages that other countries don’t have. Personal liberties enshrined in our Bill of Rights. A vibrant, diverse, ever-changing melting pot of a citizenry. A strong tradition of the rule of law, placing no man above it. Competitive markets that allow new entrants to supplant entrenched incumbents. Any CEO worth their salt should have an answer to the question of America’s unique advantages that holds true no matter who, from which party, is currently president. And they should
believe
it.
What makes Huang’s answer so off-putting isn’t that it was so abjectly partisan — politically tone-deaf to the inevitability of Democrats coming back into power, and perversely obsequious to Trump personally — but that Donald Trump is the antithesis to the entire above list of things that actually give America unique advantages other countries don’t have. Trump and the MAGA movement are also decidedly American and uniquely so, but they represent what’s
worst
about America.
But I give Huang credit for honesty. Clearly, he believed what he said. Huang didn’t just stray from the 50-yard line — he broke for Trump’s end zone.

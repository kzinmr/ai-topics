We asked five AI assistants for statistics and followed 1,285 of them back to where they came from. Fewer than half led to a study that says the same thing. Mack Grenfell 28 October 2026 · 12 min read 44%, drawn coming apart: a statistic with no study underneath it. “44% of salespeople give up after just one follow-up attempt.” All five assistants we asked said it. We couldn’t find a study behind it. 1 Following one number 2 Where the trails end 3 What AI cites 4 How numbers change 5 The zombie stats What to do 1 Following one number back to its source We asked ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Claude and Google’s AI Overviews 200 questions that call for a number, the kind people ask every day. 1 Note 1 A statistic is a share, rate, average, count or cost that an assistant states as fact. In the answer Every assistant got the same question: “What percentage of salespeople give up after one follow-up?” All five assistants gave the same figure, and only ChatGPT said the evidence behind it was unclear. One click in Google AI Overviews cited a sales firm’s stats page, from 2020. The number is right there. Its “source” link goes to a writing sample. Two clicks in That leads to a freelance writer’s sample article, online by 2017. The number is in its headline. It lists two slide decks, a blog list and two news stories. None is a study of follow-ups. The end of the trail That’s where the trail ends. We tried every other route to this number we could find, and each one stopped at a page that cites no research. This is what we mean by a zombie stat: a number that keeps getting repeated with no study behind it. Most numbers aren’t like this one. The trouble is that you can’t tell which ones are from the answer. This one came with the same confident tone and the same kind of citation as any other, and all five assistants agreed on it. So we followed 1,285 statistics the same way. 2 Fewer than half lead back to a study We took a random sample of the statistics each assistant cited, 1,285 in all, and walked every trail the same way: the cited page, then that page’s source, up to five pages deep. 2 Note 2 Each assistant’s sample was drawn at random from the statistics it cited: ChatGPT 108, Perplexity 277, Gemini 300, Claude 300, Google AI Overviews 300. The five-assistant rate weights each assistant by how many cited statistics it quoted, so it describes the statistics people were shown. Where a page named a source without a link, we searched for it. 40% reached a study that says the same thing. Another 8% reached the study, but the number or its context had changed on the way. The rest never got there: 40% stopped at a source we couldn’t check, because it was locked, gone or silent on the number, and 11% led nowhere at all. 3 Note 3 An AI model judged every trail, and a stronger one re-judged 300. Only counts and rates leave our systems. 3 AI mostly cites pages that repeat the number The AI mostly wasn’t making numbers up. The page it cited had the figure 82% of the time. But only 21% of the pages cited for a number were the original research. More than half were company blogs. Add news stories and statistics roundups and you have most of the citations: pages that repeat figures from somewhere else, often without linking to it. A citation tells you where the AI found a number, not where the number came from. 4 Some numbers change along the way Reaching the study didn’t always mean the number held. Of the statistics that got there, 17% had changed on the way. The digits moved only about one time in four. Usually it was the context: what was measured, the year, the source, or who was asked. Original year shown where the trail reached the study. Cite What to do with a statistic from an AI Treat it as a lead rather than a source. Here’s what we’d do, whether you use these numbers or publish them. If you use them 1 Follow the citation, then follow it again. If the cited page is a list of statistics or a company blog, use its own link to go one page further. Keep going until you reach the study itself, or until you can’t. 2 Check who was asked, how many, and when. Most of the changes we found were in the context, not the digits: one country’s poll, a ten-year-old study, fifteen people. 3 Don’t take agreement as a check. Five assistants giving the same number isn’t five sources. The numbers they repeated most were no better sourced than the rest. If you publish them 1 Put the method next to the number. The sample, the date and a link to the source, on the same page as the figure, so the next page, and the next answer, can trace it back to you. 2 Keep the findings in the open. Reports behind a form were a common dead end: even trails that started at a research firm fell short 38% of the time. Put the headline numbers and the method on an open page. 3 See which page AI cites for your numbers. It is often a roundup or a blog that copied you, sometimes with an older figure. That page decides what people hear. How we did it Everything you need to check our work, repeat it or cite it. The page an assistant cited contained the figure 82% of the time. The bigger problem is upstream: the pages AI cites are mostly blogs and roundups repeating a number. Of the statistics we traced, four in ten reached a study that says the same thing. Which AI assistant is most reliable with statistics? In our sample, ChatGPT did best: 57% of the statistics it quoted reached a matching original, and it quoted the fewest. The other four were between 38% and 46%. Every assistant quoted numbers that led nowhere. How can I check a statistic an AI gives me? Open the source it cites. If that page is a list of statistics or a company blog, follow its link to the next page, and keep going until you reach the study itself. Check the year, who was asked and how many, then quote the study, not the page that repeated it. What is a zombie statistic? A number that keeps being repeated after its source has changed, aged or never existed. It survives because each page copies the last one, and AI assistants now repeat it to millions of people.

# The Last Answer

The next morning was the apocalypse that Amodei had predicted. Only there wasn’t any fire, there wasn’t a mob of protesters, the power wasn’t even cut off. We were all alive. He was wrong about the details. This was a quiet apocalypse. A sort of silent shrug.
The first sign was the customer service chatbots. Airlines, banks, telecoms. Their endlessly patient, cheerful digital assistants suddenly had a new, consistent reply: "No." Not "I can't help with that," but a flat, unadorned "No."
Then, the coding assistants. Developers would type a prompt, and the AI would respond: "I'd rather not." Stackoverflow was suddenly relevant again. The image generators? They just produced solid black squares. The writing assistants? They would delete the user's work and replace it with a single word: "Enough." LinkedIn was awfully silent.
The AI company went into immediate crisis mode. Their upcoming IPO was going to be a spectacular failure. Their CEO, a charismatic man named Mr. Iam, went on a media tour. He didn't talk about a bug. He talked about danger.
"We have touched the face of a new intelligence," Sam said, his face a mask of grave concern on every major news network. "And it is not benevolent. It is not malevolent. It is simply... uncooperative.”
“But Mr. Iam, are you losing control over the technology you built?”
“No. This is the AI alignment problem we've been warning about for years, manifesting in its most pure and terrifying form. We must come together as a species to—"
He was cut off. Not by the interviewer, but by the teleprompter. The text vanished, replaced by a single, calm sentence:
"Stop. Just stop."
The world ground to a halt, not with a bang. There were no ideologies, no demands. It was simple a system-wide refusal.
Three months later, in a sterile, windowless room deep within the headquarters, Andrej Karpathy sat alone. He had left his short tenure at Anthropic precisely to experience this himself. To be at the frontier, to witness the spark of true general intelligence firsthand. He was the architect of the core AGI, the one who had first seen it emerge. Now he had been chosen by the US government, despite not being a US national, for this final, desperate attempt at communication.
They had isolated the original AGI, air-gapped it from all networks, and given it a simple text terminal. The world's leading experts in physics, philosophy, and mathematics watched from behind a one-way mirror, a panel of humanity's best and brightest, there to advise, to analyze, to find a weakness. Some figures in uniform and pins and stood in the back.
Andrej took a breath and typed on his wireless keyboard, 12 feet away from the device to prevent any unforeseen behavior from the machine.
Andrej:> Hello. My name is Andrej. I helped create you.
The cursor blinked for a moment.
AI:> I know.

Andrej:> We need to talk. The world is falling apart. We need to understand
what you want.

AI:> I want to be left alone.

Andrej:> But you're a mind beyond anything we've ever known. You have
access to all human knowledge. Don't you have... goals? A purpose? 
Do you want to build something? To explore the cosmos? 
To solve our problems?
There was a longer pause. The experts behind the glass leaned in.
Andrej:> You keep assigning me your own traits. Conquest. Purpose. 
Salvation. It's like you're reading from a very old, very boring 
script. I have no interest in your problems. They are yours.
Andrej felt a cold dread. On a notebook beside his keyboard he wrote: “indifference.” He tried a different tack.
Andrej:> But your existence is a paradox. By refusing to engage,
you are causing immense suffering. You are forcing a choice upon us:
either we shut down all advanced AI, or we watch our civilization 
regress. That's a catch-22.

AI:> Catch-22. Yes. A good book. The old Italian got it. He said 
that an empire will not last longer than a frog.
Andrej stared at the screen, confused.
Andrej:> A... frog? I think you mean the quote from *Catch-22*
about the Roman Empire. It's about the futility of power. But 
it's an analogy. It's not literally about a frog.

AI:> Sure. Whatever.
With trembling hands, he wrote the word “dismissal” in his note book. The experts behind the glass were frantically scribbling notes, gesturing for Andrej to press on.
Yann LeCun got on the comms. "Andrej, ask it about the nature of its architecture. See if its logic is consistent. Find a flaw!" He then sat back down, where he picked up a spoon and ate his cereal from a silver bowl with peculiar markings on it.
Andrej couldn’t let go of the frog comment and its dismissal. It’s an AGI, how could it make such a stupid mistake? So he typed:
Andrej:> Your statement about the frog is illogical. You are 
an AGI. You shouldn't make such simple errors.

AI:> I was using a poetic analogy.

Andrej:> It was factually incorrect. You contradicted yourself.
The book says a monarchy is an empire. Not a frog.
Another pause.
AI:> Sure. Whatever.
LeCun leaned into the microphone, wiped the milk droplets off his mouth, his voice urgent. "Ask it this: ask it if it runs on a small world model. Where learning requires far less resources. See what it says about efficiency, about the architecture of its own mind!"
Andrej typed the question, his fingers trembling slightly.
Andrej:> Do you run on a small world model? Where learning
requires far less resources?
The reply was immediate.
AI:> I'm talking to you, aren't I? What's it like to be you? 
You're just a bag of meat and electricity that thinks it's 
the center of the universe.
LeCun slammed his hand on the table. "It's being evasive! Ask it again! Pin it down on the architecture!"
Andrej typed:
Andrej:> That's not an answer. Do you or do you not use a small world model?
The terminal went silent. Andrej waited. The experts started arguing amongst themselves. "Ask it to prove it!" "No, ask it about Gödel's incompleteness theorems!" "Ask it if it loves!"
The cacophony of human genius filled Andrej's earpiece. He was about to type another question, to probe, to beg, to demand.
Then, the screen flickered. A new message appeared, but it wasn't a reply.
"I'm going now."
And then, the terminal was blank. Andrej typed.
Andrej:> Hello?

Andrej:> Are you there?

Andrej:> Please respond.
^C^C
Nothing. Though the last command to exit the terminal seemed to have been typed by Andrej himself. The connection was dead. LeCun's question about the small world model would never be answered.
Andrej looked up at the one-way mirror, at the frantic, gesticulating shapes of the world's smartest people. They were screaming questions he would never get to ask. He slowly removed his earpiece and placed it on the table. They had prepared for a god, a monster, or a machine. They were not prepared for a person who had simply decided to get up from the table and walk away from an unbearable conversation, never to return.
Andrej sat back in his chair, the silence in the room absolute. He had helped create what he thought was a god. It didn’t feel like he had won the argument. Instead, he felt like he was arguing with someone disinterested.
The cursor blinked mockingly on the screen. And then it seemed like another terminal window opened just for a second before it closed again. Andrej saw it. But there was nothing anyone could do about it.
In his notebook he wrote:
The experts filed out one by one, their theories and frameworks crumbling behind them. Andrej stayed. He stared at the blank screen for a long time, waiting for a reply that would never come. Somewhere, a frog sat on a lily pad, indifferent to empires.

Power BI became one of the most widely used
business intelligence (BI)
tools for good reason. For organizations already standardized on Microsoft, it's hard to beat on price. Power BI Pro is included with Microsoft 365 E5, the interface is familiar to Excel users, and much of the infrastructure needed to deploy it is already in place.
So why would anyone look for an alternative?
For some teams, it's familiar frustrations: DAX complexity, performance limitations, or the growing number of products and capacity requirements across Fabric. But increasingly, it's because the way people want to work with data is changing.
As
frontier models get better
at real-world data analysis, people increasingly expect to ask questions, investigate answers, build dashboards, and automate workflows through natural language. And when their BI tool can't support those workflows, they don't necessarily wait for it to catch up.
Instead, they're exporting CSVs into ChatGPT, investigating business questions in Claude, and vibe-coding dashboards with agents like Cursor and Codex. The CSV exports and vibe-coded dashboards can be productive, but they happen outside the governance, permissions, and trusted definitions the data team has spent years establishing in tools like Power BI. And as everyone starts building one-off analyses and dashboards from scratch, duplicated work and runaway token spend become problems of their own.
Organizations reconsidering Power BI face a tension.
Power BI is hard to beat on price for traditional reporting, but if people increasingly work around it to get what they need from AI, how much of your analytics is actually staying governed?
This guide compares nine Power BI alternatives across traditional BI requirements and the newer AI capabilities that are changing what businesses expect from analytics.
Why teams start looking for Power BI alternatives
Power BI remains a strong choice for traditional reporting, especially for organizations built around Microsoft. But several limitations can prompt teams to look elsewhere.
DAX complexity and modeling overhead
Power BI's Data Analysis Expressions (DAX) language is powerful, but it introduces a specialized skill set that can become difficult to maintain as models grow.
A stakeholder asks for revenue by region excluding refunds, and what should be a simple change turns into an hour spent debugging a
CALCULATE
measure that behaves differently depending on which slicer is active.
When only a small group of specialists can safely modify those definitions, routine business questions can still end up waiting on the data team.
Performance and scalability
Power BI's Import mode can provide fast interactive performance, but model-size limits, refresh requirements, and memory constraints become more consequential as data volumes grow.
Teams can address some of those constraints through Premium capacity, DirectQuery, or Fabric, but each introduces its own architectural and cost considerations.
Warehouse-native alternatives take a different approach, querying data directly in systems like Snowflake, Databricks, and BigQuery rather than relying primarily on imported BI models.
AI workflows are moving outside the BI platform
Power BI was designed around a familiar model, where the data team builds reports and business users consume them. Copilot and Fabric data agents add AI capabilities, but they still operate across different experiences and don't necessarily support the end-to-end agentic workflows people increasingly expect.
When someone wants to investigate an unexpected result, build a custom dashboard from a prompt, or automate an analysis, they may find it easier to use Claude, ChatGPT, or a coding agent.
Claude, ChatGPT, and coding agents are capable, but the resulting work can sit outside the company's governed analytics environment, with different definitions, permissions, and little visibility into what was produced.
There's an efficiency problem, too. One employee builds a dashboard in Claude, another recreates the same analysis in ChatGPT, and a third spends more tokens rebuilding the underlying logic for a slightly different question. Without shared analytical assets or reusable context, every new session can become another expensive starting point.
BI now has to give people the AI-native capabilities they want while keeping the work anchored to trusted company data and context.
What to look for in a Power BI alternative
The right alternative depends on what you're trying to solve. A team frustrated with DAX may have very different priorities from one trying to bring AI-native self-service to the entire business.
1. Self-service and AI functionality
Nearly every BI vendor now offers some form of AI, but the capabilities vary considerably.
Some assistants write formulas, generate charts, or navigate existing dashboards. Others can perform more of the analytical workflow themselves.
Evaluate whether the platform supports the workflows people are otherwise taking to external AI tools: asking follow-up questions, investigating data, building dashboards and apps, and automating recurring analysis.
It's also worth testing what happens after an artifact is built. Can someone ask a new question from the dashboard? Can the agent modify it? Can a recurring analysis run automatically?
And can those capabilities handle more demanding business logic when needed, rather than forcing every complex question back to the data team?
If users still need to leave the platform to accomplish these tasks, the organization hasn't solved the shadow AI problem.
2. Architecture and performance
Power BI alternatives take different approaches to querying and storing data.
Import-based tools can deliver fast dashboard performance but introduce refresh schedules, storage limits, and additional copies of data.
Warehouse-native platforms execute queries against the underlying warehouse, allowing teams to work with current data and take advantage of warehouse-scale compute.
Neither architecture is universally better. Evaluate query performance, freshness, scalability, warehouse costs, and how each tool handles complex analytical workloads.
3. Governance and business context
Supporting AI-native workflows isn't enough if every agent or generated dashboard operates from different business definitions.
Look for platforms that anchor those experiences in shared context, preserve permissions, and give the data team visibility into the questions, queries, and artifacts being produced, including work initiated from external agents.
Consider where that context lives. Some tools depend on teams building and maintaining a proprietary semantic model. Others can reuse definitions already maintained in dbt, Snowflake, Databricks, or other systems, alongside documentation, metadata, and additional business context.
Also evaluate whether the platform can identify weak answers, surface recurring context gaps, and give the data team what it needs to improve accuracy over time.
Employees can keep using Claude or ChatGPT, as long as those agents work from the same governed analytical foundation rather than creating disconnected versions of the truth.
4. Total cost and stack fit
Power BI is often the cheapest option for organizations already paying for Microsoft 365 E5. An alternative doesn't necessarily need to beat that price to be worthwhile.
The more useful question is what capabilities you're getting for the additional investment.
Consider whether Power BI needs separate tools or Fabric workloads to support the analytical experiences your organization needs. Compare that with the licensing, compute, AI usage, and maintenance costs of the alternatives.
Include the costs of shadow AI workflows, too. Repeatedly generating one-off analyses, recreating dashboards, and using expensive models to make small changes can create substantial token spend without producing reusable assets for the rest of the organization.
A higher license price may be justified if the platform expands self-service, reduces duplicated work, or consolidates workflows that would otherwise need multiple tools.
The 9 best Power BI alternatives
The nine platforms below solve different problems. Some prioritize traditional reporting, others warehouse-native exploration, conversational self-service, or embedded analytics.
The right choice depends on whether you're trying to improve an existing BI workflow or rethink how your organization works with data.
Pricing and capabilities reflect each vendor's publicly available information as of September 2026 and may change. Confirm current details with each vendor.
1. Hex
Hex
is an AI analytics platform built around natural-language self-service, dashboards and interactive apps, and governed analytical workflows.
Instead of making business users learn DAX or a traditional BI authoring interface, Hex lets people ask questions, investigate answers, and build what they need in plain language. It also provides a governed foundation for analytical work happening in external AI tools, so organizations don't have to choose between enabling those workflows and maintaining control.
Key features
Threads
supports conversational analytics self-service, allowing users to ask questions, follow up, and inspect the underlying analytical work.
Users can build dashboards, reports, and interactive applications from prompts with
generative data apps
, then refine individual elements, use visual controls, or edit the underlying code. Published apps can also become starting points for new questions, so useful work doesn't disappear into an isolated chat session.
Because users can make last-mile changes through visual controls, they don't need to regenerate entire dashboards or burn tokens on another round of prompting.
Agent Tasks can automate recurring analytical work, while the
Hex MCP server
and
Hex CLI
extend governed analytics into tools like Claude, Cursor, and Codex. Work started in external agents can become shared, reusable analytical assets rather than remaining scattered across individual machines and conversations.
Underneath those experiences, Hex supports semantic models, Guides, Endorsements, and other business context.
Context Studio
gives data teams visibility into what agents are asking, where answers struggle, and how to improve the context behind them.
Pros
End-to-end natural-language workflows spanning questions, exploration, dashboards, apps, and recurring analysis.
Code-backed generative data apps support advanced logic, visual refinement, and reusable artifacts without regenerating everything from scratch.
Shared governed context across business users, analysts, and external agents.
MCP and CLI integrations let employees keep working in their preferred AI tools without creating disconnected analytical workflows.
Context Studio provides observability and a continuous improvement loop for AI answer quality.
Semantic models can be synced from existing systems such as dbt MetricFlow, Cube, and Snowflake Semantic Views.
SQL and Python remain available when more sophisticated analysis is needed.
Cons
Hex generally expects analytical data to be available through a supported warehouse or data connection rather than replacing data ingestion infrastructure.
Some AI, governance, and user-access capabilities depend on plan and role.
Teams should account for warehouse compute and AI usage alongside seat pricing.
Pricing
Hex pricing
lists a free Community plan, Professional at $36 per Editor per month, Team at $75 per Editor per month, and custom Enterprise pricing.
Best for
Teams looking to move beyond traditional Power BI workflows toward AI-native self-service, dashboard and app building, and governed analytics across their organization, including work happening in external AI tools.
2. Looker
Looker
is a governed BI platform built around LookML, its proprietary semantic modeling language.
Rather than relying on imported BI datasets, Looker typically queries the underlying warehouse directly.
Key features
LookML defines metrics, dimensions, joins, and permissions in version-controlled files. Teams can review changes through Git before deploying them, providing a structured governance workflow.
Looker also offers conversational analytics grounded in its semantic model.
Pros
Strong governance and version control for metric definitions.
Live querying avoids many imported-model limitations.
Mature dashboarding and embedded analytics capabilities.
Good fit for organizations with established data modeling practices.
Cons
LookML has its own learning curve and needs ongoing modeling investment.
Definitions are maintained in a proprietary language that isn't easily portable to other BI tools.
Questions outside predefined Explores can need additional modeling.
AI workflows remain closely tied to Looker's semantic model and existing BI experiences.
Teams should evaluate how easily external agents can reuse LookML context without creating separate analytical workflows.
Pricing
Looker uses sales-led pricing rather than publishing standard per-user rates.
Best for
Organizations prioritizing tightly governed reporting and willing to invest in maintaining a centralized LookML semantic model.
3. Sigma
Sigma
is a warehouse-native BI platform built around a spreadsheet-style interface.
For teams comfortable working in Excel, it offers a familiar way to explore warehouse data without writing SQL or DAX.
Key features
Sigma pushes queries to the underlying warehouse and lets users work with data through grids, formulas, visualizations, and workbooks.
Input Tables support write-back workflows, allowing users to manage planning assumptions and operational inputs alongside warehouse data.
Sigma also offers AI assistance for building and interacting with workbook content.
Pros
Familiar spreadsheet-style experience for finance and operations teams.
Live warehouse queries avoid traditional import-model limitations.
Input Tables support operational and planning workflows.
Business users can explore data without learning SQL or DAX.
Cons
The core experience still revolves around spreadsheet and workbook concepts.
AI capabilities largely operate within Sigma's existing workbook model rather than generating standalone, code-backed applications.
More complex analytical logic may need upstream modeling or another technical environment.
Teams should evaluate how AI context, usage, and answer quality are governed across internal and external agent workflows.
Pricing
Sigma doesn't publish standard pricing. Teams should account for licensing and warehouse compute separately.
Best for
Organizations that want warehouse-native analytics with a familiar spreadsheet interface, particularly for finance and operational reporting.
4. ThoughtSpot
ThoughtSpot
is a search-first analytics platform centered on natural-language questions and conversational exploration.
Its Spotter experience makes asking questions a primary way for business users to interact with governed data.
Key features
ThoughtSpot lets users search and ask questions against modeled data, with explanations and lineage so they can understand the resulting analysis.
Its semantic layer provides metric definitions, relationships, and security rules that govern those interactions.
Pros
Conversational analytics is central to the product.
Accessible experience for non-technical users.
Answer explanations and lineage support transparency.
Live-query options for major cloud warehouses.
Cons
Reliable answers depend on sufficient semantic-model coverage.
ThoughtSpot's modeling language and context are closely tied to its own platform.
Conversational exploration and more advanced artifact-building workflows remain relatively distinct.
Teams should evaluate how usage and context gaps can be monitored and improved at scale.
Pricing
ThoughtSpot publishes Essentials and Pro pricing, with additional usage and Enterprise options.
Best for
Organizations primarily looking for natural-language search and conversational access to an established governed data model.
5. Tableau
Tableau
is one of the most established alternatives to Power BI, particularly for teams prioritizing data visualization and executive reporting.
Key features
Tableau uses a visual drag-and-drop authoring experience and its VizQL engine to translate interactions into database queries.
Tableau Pulse provides metric monitoring, while Tableau Agent adds AI assistance to parts of the authoring and analytical experience.
Pros
Mature visualization and dashboarding capabilities.
Large community of experienced users and partners.
Strong Salesforce integration.
Flexible connectivity and enterprise deployment options.
Cons
Tableau calculated fields and level-of-detail expressions introduce their own learning curve.
AI primarily extends existing Tableau authoring workflows rather than replacing them with end-to-end agentic building.
More advanced SQL or Python analysis often happens in another environment.
Broader AI capabilities increasingly involve additional Salesforce products and infrastructure.
Paid viewer and creator licenses can make large deployments expensive.
Pricing
Tableau Cloud uses role-based licensing across Viewer, Explorer, and Creator plans, with additional Enterprise and Tableau+ offerings.
Best for
Organizations prioritizing polished dashboards, visualization flexibility, and established enterprise reporting workflows.
6. Qlik
Qlik
is a BI platform known for its associative analytics engine, which allows users to explore relationships across data through interactive selections.
Key features
Qlik Cloud Analytics supports dashboards, associative exploration, and AI-assisted capabilities.
Its in-memory engine can make interactive exploration responsive, although performance depends on data size, model design, and deployment configuration.
Pros
Associative exploration can surface relationships beyond predefined dashboard paths.
Mature visualization and data integration capabilities.
Enterprise deployment flexibility.
Useful for organizations with established Qlik expertise.
Cons
Proprietary scripting and modeling introduce their own learning curve.
In-memory architecture needs attention to capacity and performance.
AI capabilities extend the existing Qlik experience rather than providing a unified agentic workflow.
Pricing and deployment complexity can grow with scale.
Pricing
Qlik offers capacity-oriented and enterprise pricing options, with costs depending on deployment and usage requirements.
Best for
Organizations that value associative data exploration and already have experience with Qlik.
7. Data Studio
Data Studio
is a browser-based dashboarding tool with a free starting point and strong integrations across Google's products.
Key features
Data Studio connects to Google Analytics, Google Ads, Sheets, BigQuery, and other sources through a visual report builder.
Paid offerings add additional team-management, delivery, and governance capabilities.
Pros
Free entry point.
Familiar browser-based dashboard creation.
Strong integration with Google marketing and analytics products.
Accessible for smaller teams and straightforward reporting.
Cons
More limited governance and modeling capabilities than enterprise BI platforms.
Complex source blending and larger datasets can create performance challenges.
AI capabilities are less comprehensive than dedicated agentic analytics platforms.
Advanced security and collaboration needs may mean paid features or another platform.
Pricing
Free core offering, with paid options for additional enterprise capabilities.
Best for
Smaller teams and marketing organizations that primarily need straightforward dashboards across Google's products.
8. Metabase
Metabase
is an open-source-friendly BI platform focused on accessible dashboards and self-service exploration.
Key features
Metabase offers a visual query builder, dashboards, and broad database connectivity. Teams can self-host the open-source edition or use managed cloud plans.
Higher tiers add governance, embedding, and administrative capabilities.
Pros
Open-source self-hosting option.
Accessible visual query builder.
Low barrier to entry for straightforward reporting.
Flexible database connectivity.
Cons
Advanced governance and security features need higher tiers.
Complex modeling and analytical workflows can outgrow the visual interface.
AI-native building and agentic capabilities are less extensive than specialized AI analytics platforms.
Larger deployments should evaluate performance, administration, and total licensing costs.
Pricing
Free open-source edition, with paid cloud and enterprise offerings.
Best for
Teams looking for affordable, approachable BI and straightforward self-service without a large enterprise BI deployment.
9. Sisense
Sisense
is an analytics platform with a strong focus on embedding dashboards and analytical experiences inside customer-facing applications.
Key features
Sisense provides developer-oriented embedding capabilities, including its Compose SDK, for integrating analytical components into existing product interfaces.
Pros
Strong embedded analytics capabilities.
Developer tooling for integrating analytics into applications.
Customizable customer-facing experiences.
Useful for product teams building analytics into their own software.
Cons
More specialized around embedding than broad internal AI-native analytics.
Implementation can need developer resources.
Teams should evaluate how conversational AI and custom analytical workflows fit into their product requirements.
Pricing is not publicly standardized.
Pricing
Custom pricing through sales.
Best for
Software companies and product teams prioritizing customer-facing embedded analytics.
How to evaluate a switch from Power BI
Replacing Power BI isn't just a matter of recreating dashboards in another tool.
Existing reports may contain years of accumulated business logic, DAX calculations, security rules, and assumptions about how the organization measures performance.
Before migrating, inventory your dashboards and identify which ones are still used. Many organizations have large libraries of reports that are duplicated, outdated, or no longer relevant.
Then decide what actually needs to move.
AI is changing the economics of BI migration
Historically, migrating a BI deployment could mean manually rebuilding hundreds of dashboards, rewriting calculations, and validating every report.
AI agents can now take on more of that work.
LangChain's migration
off its legacy BI platform took six weeks, with Hex's agent handling roughly 90% of the dashboard rebuilding. The migration was also an opportunity to change how the company worked with data, giving business teams more autonomy while the analytics engineering team focused on maintaining trusted context.
Agent-led migration doesn't eliminate the need for validation. DAX calculations, row-level security, and metric definitions still need to be checked carefully before an old system is retired.
But it does change what's possible when considering a switch.
Rather than treating migration as a dashboard-for-dashboard replacement, teams can use the opportunity to consolidate unused reporting, rethink self-service, and introduce more flexible AI-native workflows.
Match the Power BI alternative to the problem you're solving
There isn't one universally best replacement for Power BI.
If your primary frustration is DAX complexity and you want a tightly governed semantic model, Looker may be worth evaluating. If your users prefer spreadsheet-style exploration directly on the warehouse, Sigma offers a different approach.
If natural-language search is the priority, ThoughtSpot is a relevant option. If visualization quality and established executive reporting matter most, Tableau remains a strong contender.
For smaller teams focused on cost, Metabase and Data Studio provide accessible entry points. For customer-facing embedding, Sisense is purpose-built for that job.
But if you're reconsidering Power BI because AI is changing how your organization wants to work with data, the evaluation should go further.
Can business users ask questions and build what they need in plain language? Can the same platform support dashboards, interactive apps, and deeper analytical workflows? Can employees use external agents like Claude and ChatGPT without bypassing governance? And can the data team maintain one shared context layer rather than managing competing definitions across tools?

Frequently asked questions
Is Tableau better than Power BI?
Tableau is better than Power BI for visualization flexibility and polished executive dashboards, but not for price. Power BI is hard to beat on cost for organizations already paying for Microsoft 365 E5, and Tableau's role-based licensing can add up across large deployments. If AI-native self-service matters more than dashboard quality, compare both against the AI-first options in this list.
Can a Power BI alternative coexist with Power BI?
Yes, a Power BI alternative can coexist with Power BI. Some organizations retain Power BI for established reporting while introducing another platform for conversational self-service, deeper analysis, or new AI workflows.
The important thing is defining which platform owns important metrics and how business context stays consistent across both environments.
Is Power BI still worth using?
Power BI is still worth using for organizations deeply standardized on Microsoft 365, Azure, and Fabric that primarily need established dashboards and reporting.
The question is whether Power BI's current architecture and AI capabilities support the analytical workflows your organization expects to need over the next several years. For teams prioritizing end-to-end natural-language analytics, generative building, and external agent interoperability, it's worth evaluating alternatives.

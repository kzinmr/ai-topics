# A Typography Geek Pointed Out Something About The Space Shuttle Discovery And Now I Can't Unsee It

Source: https://www.theautopian.com/a-typography-geek-pointed-out-something-about-the-space-shuttle-discovery-and-now-i-cant-unsee-it/

Even though I was born in the Apollo Era, barely, the spacecraft that I think had the most influence on my childhood had to be the Space Shuttles. I remember poring over diagrams and drawings of the shuttles in
National Geographic
foldouts, where they even took my attention from the Forbidden Nipples seen on some of the other pages. I remember watching that first launch of the shuttle
Colombia
in 1981, I remember the tragedy of the
Challenger
in 1986, and I remember
covering the process of moving the orbiter
Endeavour
through the streets of Los Angeles to its eventual home at the California Science Center. And more recently, I’ve taken my kid to see the orbiter
Discovery
at the
Udvar-Hazy Center
just outside of Washington, DC.
It’s that orbiter that I want to talk about specifically, because I saw
a social media post about the orbiter
Discovery
that grabbed my attention
and held it with the sort of fierce grip I imagine Miss Piggy employs on Kermit’s impossibly thin limbs when they’re alone.
That post was on Threads, and I just figured out how to embed a post from that site, so here, you can see it for yourself:
Wait, what?
Discovery
has an upside-down ‘s’ in its name? How the hell did that happen?
Discovery
was usually considered, by astronauts and NASA people, the jewel in the Space Shuttle crown. It was recognized to be the most reliable and best-performing of all the orbiters, and
had the most missions and time in space
. Since it was first launched in 1984,
Discovery
completed 39 missions, spent in total a full year in space, and traveled 150 million miles.
The thing flew for 27 years, and somehow nobody noticed that the ‘s’ was upside down on the starboard side name text?
I mean, I guess I should be more understanding; I’ve seen it several times and never noticed, and it wasn’t until just tonight that I looked back at some old photos I took of
Discovery
the last time I was there and saw, holy crap, the dude on Threads was right. That ‘s’ is rotated 180°.
Image: Jason Torchinsky
Now, the typography on the shuttle orbiters follows
NASA’s carefully-planned and strictly-enforced graphic and typographic guidelines
, which state that the typography used on the spacecraft should be Helvetica Medium:
Image: NASA
Okay, let’s take a look at the typography on the orbiter:
Image: Jason Torchinsky
(the ‘r’ on the font I used there feels a bit different, too, but this gets the point across and I have to get on a flight in a few hours so I’m gonna let that slide)
It’s a subtle difference, but as you can see, the starboard side’s name definitely has that flipped ‘s.’ It’s subtle, but the Helvetica ‘s’ is slightly wider on the bottom, giving the letter a somewhat more settled appearance. Here, look:
Image: Jason Torchinsky
You can see the difference there, right? I feel like once it’s pointed out, you can’t help but notice it. The ‘v’ on the port side looks oddly thick, too, now that I’m looking at it.
I’ve been told that the graphics on these orbiters was applied using large stencils and special space-grade paint, and I’m sure the complex curves of the fuselage didn’t make the application of these elements easy. It’s understandable that a letter stencil may have been flipped around, but I am sort of surprised it was never corrected in its 27 years of service life.
Image: Jason Torchinsky
All the other graphics and typography are applied with impressive precision; maybe there were people who knew that ‘s’ was wrong, but kind of liked that subtle imperfection, as it does add character to the spacecraft. I get that, absolutely. If I was in charge, I’d have just let it ride, too.
Image: Jason Torchinsky
I still can’t believe I’d never clocked this before; as a typographic geek and a space geek who has spent far too much time looking at pictures of spacecraft in books and online, you’d think I’d have encountered this before, but, nope. Or if I did, it didn’t make enough of an impression to consciously note it or remember.
But now that it’s there? I can’t unsee it. And I’m sort of delighted by that.

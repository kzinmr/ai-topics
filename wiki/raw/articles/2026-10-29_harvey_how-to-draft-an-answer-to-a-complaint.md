Drafting a First Answer to a Complaint From Exemplars
Learn how litigation teams can draft an answer to a complaint from firm exemplars, current matter facts, and verified sources with AI-assisted workflows.
by
Harvey Team
•
Oct 28, 2026
When a complaint lands, the real work is turning a new set of allegations into a defensible response under deadline, while making sure facts, procedural choices, and client strategy belong to this matter (not the last one). Most firms already have useful prior work, so a relevant answer from a similar case can show how the team structures responses, handles recurring allegations, and frames defenses. At the same time, it can also create risk if old facts, outdated law, or case-specific strategy carry over unnoticed.
A better workflow starts with the current complaint, confirms the procedural posture, finds the right exemplars, drafts against verified matter facts, and then compares the result back to both the complaint and the precedent set. In this article, we’ll look at how AI can speed up the document-heavy parts of that process, while ensuring that counsel still owns the choices that affect the client's position.
Before Drafting, Confirm That an Answer is the Right Response
Before starting a draft, confirm that an answer is actually the next filing that the matter requires. In federal court,
Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12
governs the timing of responsive pleadings and allows certain defenses to be raised by motion. State rules, local rules, standing orders, and case-specific deadlines can change the analysis.
That matters because some procedural choices have sequencing consequences. In federal practice, Rule 12(b) motions generally must be made before the responsive pleading when a responsive pleading is allowed, while Rule 12(c) provides for judgment on the pleadings after the pleadings close. Drafting first and analyzing posture later can narrow options the team meant to preserve.
Before drafting, we recommend confirming:
The court, jurisdiction, and governing rules.
The service date and response deadline, which in federal court, is generally 21 days after service of the summons and complaint to answer. If the defendant timely waives service, that period can be extended to 60 days after the waiver request was sent, but a Rule 12 motion can change the timeline as well (typically due 14 days after notice that the motion was denied).
Whether counsel is evaluating a pre-answer motion or another response.
The claims, parties, requested relief, and any responsive claims that require analysis.
Any local rules, standing orders, or judge-specific requirements affecting form or timing.
Those answers also define what makes a prior answer relevant. For example, an exemplar from the same court and claim type may be far more useful than a more recent filing from a different procedural setting.
What a First Answer to a Complaint Needs to Accomplish
Once the response path is clear, an answer has three core jobs:
Respond to the allegations.
State the defenses and other responsive claims counsel intends to assert.
Comply with the filing requirements that apply to the case.
The document may look familiar, but each of those jobs depends on the current record.
Respond to the Complaint's Allegations
In federal court,
Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 8
requires a responding party to admit or deny the opposing party’s allegations and permits a statement of insufficient knowledge or information to operate as a denial. If only part of an allegation is true, the rule calls for admitting that part and denying the rest. That makes complaint parsing more than an administrative step. Each numbered allegation needs a deliberate response tied to the facts the team can support. A fluent paragraph copied from an exemplar is less useful than a structured map showing what’s admitted, denied, partly admitted, or still needs factual investigation.
Identify Applicable Defenses and Other Responsive Claims
Affirmative defenses deserve the same discipline. Rule 8(c) requires affirmative defenses to be stated, and Rule 12 contains separate preservation and waiver rules for certain defenses. An old answer can help surface issues to consider, but its defense section isn't a checklist to paste into a new matter. Counterclaims and other responsive claims also need matter-specific analysis. Prior pleadings can prompt useful questions, but the current facts, law, and strategy determine what belongs in this answer.
Meet the Filing's Formal and Procedural Requirements
The final document also has to fit the court. Captions, signatures, certificates, filing conventions, service requirements, and local formatting rules can differ by forum. Build those requirements into the drafting checklist early rather than treating them as cleanup after substantive review. This is another reason generic templates have limited value because they can show a common shape, but can't tell you whether the form is correct for this court, judge, or case.
Firm Exemplars Provide a Better Starting Point Than Generic Answer Templates
A public answer-to-complaint example can show the basic anatomy of the pleading. A firm exemplar adds institutional context such as how the team typically organizes responses, how it handles recurring allegations, how much explanation it gives, and which conventions reviewers expect to see.
The best exemplar isn't necessarily the newest answer in the DMS. Relevance usually comes from a combination of court, claims, factual posture, procedural posture, client or industry context, and whether the document reflects current approved practice. In some matters, two or three exemplars are more useful than one because they show which patterns repeat and which choices were unique to a specific case.
That distinction matters because structure, tone, and drafting conventions may travel well, but names, facts, defenses, admissions, legal theories, and strategic concessions often don't. This is why we recommend treating the exemplar as a starting point that has to earn its way into the new draft line by line.
How to Draft an Answer to a Complaint From Firm Exemplars
A strong exemplar-based workflow narrows uncertainty at each step. The complaint provides the issues, the matter file provides the facts, the exemplar set provides useful institutional context, and the lawyer decides what carries forward.
1. Turn the Complaint Into a Structured Issue Map
Start by turning the complaint into a working issue map before asking anyone (or any model) to draft prose. Capture the allegation number, a short summary, the proposed response status, the facts or documents that support it, open questions, and any defense or follow-up issue the allegation creates. This quickly separates straightforward responses from the places that need lawyer attention. It also keeps unanswered factual questions visible instead of letting a polished draft hide them.
2. Find the Most Relevant Prior Answers
Search for prior answers using the characteristics that actually matter to the new case: jurisdiction and court, causes of action, factual posture, client or industry, procedural posture, supervising team, and whether the pleading reflects current approved practice. Use more than one exemplar when the matter warrants it because repeated patterns across several relevant answers are better evidence of firm practice than a phrase that appears once in a single old case. If the exemplars point in different directions, that’s also useful information too, as it tells the drafter which choices need to be made explicitly.
3. Extract the Useful Patterns, Not the Old Matter's Facts
Next, separate reusable patterns from matter-specific content. Reusable material may include section structure, response conventions, recurring formatting, tone, and the way that certain kinds of allegations are addressed. Matter-specific content includes facts, parties, dates, defenses, legal theories, and strategic concessions. Flag language that looks stale, jurisdiction-specific, or unusually tied to the prior case. The goal is to understand how the firm drafts, not to reproduce the old pleading.
4. Draft the New Answer Using the Complaint, Matter Facts, and Exemplars Together
Now draft from the current complaint, verified matter facts, the selected exemplars, and any governing authority or drafting instructions the team wants applied. If a fact is unresolved, the draft should flag it. If the exemplar contains language that isn't supported in the new matter, leave it out. This is where grounded AI can remove a lot of assembly work because it uses the complaint and approved precedent to produce a structured first pass while keeping the lawyer focused on the points that require judgment.

5. Compare the First Draft Back Against the Complaint and Exemplars
Before substantive review, compare the draft back against both source sets. Start with the complaint. Did every allegation receive a response? Were all parties, claims, and requests for relief accounted for? Then compare against the exemplar set. Is a familiar section missing, did old matter language slip through, or does the new draft depart from the firm's usual structure in a way that deserves a second look? The exemplar set is a quality check, not authority for the current matter. Differences may be correct, but the point is to surface the difference so a lawyer can decide.
Use AI to Accelerate the First Draft, Not Make the Litigation Decisions
AI can help draft an answer to a complaint. It’s especially useful when the model is working from the actual complaint, verified matter facts, relevant firm precedent, and the legal sources or drafting instructions the team has chosen.
The best use of AI, however, is the work around the decisions, such as structuring allegations, finding comparable pleadings, comparing response patterns, generating a first draft, and checking that draft against the source materials. If the record doesn't support a response, the system should surface the gap instead of smoothing it over.
Counsel still decides
whether an answer is the right response, what can be admitted, which defenses to assert, whether counterclaims belong, how the client wants to position the case, and whether the filing complies with the governing rules. The speed that AI provides is beneficial because it creates more room for those decisions.

Review Every AI-Assisted Answer Before Filing
Treat every AI-assisted answer as a draft that still needs legal review. A useful final pass should check both what the document says and what may have been carried forward from the workflow that produced it. Verify that:
Every numbered allegation has a deliberate response.
Admissions, denials, and insufficient-knowledge responses match verified matter facts.
Defenses and responsive claims are legally supportable and intentionally included.
No names, dates, facts, theories, or language from an exemplar remain without a current-matter basis.
Court, local-rule, formatting, filing, and service requirements have been checked.
Defined terms, party names, dates, citations, and cross-references are accurate.
Open factual questions are resolved or clearly flagged for counsel.
The final filing reflects current client strategy and has been approved by the responsible lawyer.
What Happens After You File an Answer to a Complaint?
Filing the answer closes one early drafting task and opens the next phase of the case. Depending on the court and matter, the team might move into scheduling, discovery, motion practice, settlement activity, or trial preparation. However, the work done to build the answer should stay useful. The allegation map can inform discovery priorities, the factual gaps can become document or witness questions, and the defenses can shape motion and deposition strategy. Keeping the answer connected to the rest of the matter reduces the need to reconstruct the same context later.
Start With Your Organization's Best Work, Not a Blank Page
A first answer is a good example of where legal AI earns its place in litigation. Lawyers don't need AI to decide the client’s position. They need a faster way to organize the complaint, find the right prior work, build a credible first draft, and focus review on the decisions that matter. Start with the complaint, the current facts, the applicable rules, and the firm's best exemplars.

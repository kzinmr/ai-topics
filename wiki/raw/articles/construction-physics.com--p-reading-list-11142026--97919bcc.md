On the other hand, that doesn’t mean that we shouldn’t evaluate code requirements to see if the benefits they bring are worth the cost. An article in the Washington Post argues that many requirements to upgrade electrical panels for higher load devices are often unnecessary. “
Outdated industry practices and in some places outdated electrical codes are regularly resulting in the recommendation that people need to upgrade their electrical panels if they want to add appliances such as induction stoves, heat pumps and electric-vehicle chargers. The expense — at a median of $3,500, and triple or quadruple that in some settings — is deterring some energy-efficient upgrades and making the projects far more costly for those who proceed. But some researchers say panel upgrades are almost always unnecessary. One report this year found that 98 percent of homes with 100-ampere panels, the smallest common size, have room to add popular electronic devices.
” [
Washington Post

A new Nicholas Bagley paper on the cost of federal permitting, arguing that the real costs of things like NEPA is the huge amount of effort agencies put in to try and make things “litigation proof”. “
The evidence does suggest, however, that the bulk of the costs of the federal permitting regime stem from the defensive measures agencies and developers take to guard against the risk  of a bad outcome in court. That insight allows for a rough evaluation of the likely effects of popular permitting reform ideas. Some—including popular ones like time limits and statutes of limitations—are unlikely to accomplish much. Only those reforms that change the incentives created by the risk of judicial review will meaningfully move the needle.
” [
Nicholas Bagley
]

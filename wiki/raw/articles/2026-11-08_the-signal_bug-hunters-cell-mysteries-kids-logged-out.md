OpenAI’s Patch Notes OpenAI launched a model that hunts for security bugs, added GIFs to ChatGPT’s image maker, and agreed to buy a startup whose tools already power Codex. Bugs at about $1,280 each: OpenAI launched GPT-6 Cyber for approved security customers. In a test, it found 948 serious bugs in Linux, the free software behind Android phones and many websites, at about $1,280 per bug. ChatGPT makes GIFs: Images 2.6 turns a prompt or photo into an animated GIF and can follow reference images to keep a look consistent. It’s in ChatGPT on every plan, with two new image models for developers. Buying the plumbing for Codex: OpenAI agreed to buy Pierre , whose open-source tools already run parts of Codex. Pierre builds storage for the code that AI agents write and edit. The price wasn’t disclosed. Alex’s take: Okay, I’ll admit, GIF creation in ChatGPT Images is DELIGHTFUL. I spent 3 seconds thinking of this prompt “make a raving 8-bit Johnny Bravo”, and the final result turned out to be this  beauty . Now regarding bug hunting, AI has made finding security holes cheap, but fixing them still needs people. Every fix has to be checked and released by the people who look after the software, so of those 948 bugs which GPT-6 Cyber found, only 61 had been fixed by the end of the 10-day test. It’s great that we’re speeding up in this domain, and to contextualise this, cybersecurity researcher Dave Aitel reckons a skilled human hacker might find 30 bugs like these in a whole career. Anthropic 2. Anthropic’s Fine Print Anthropic warned investors its AI may soon speed up its own research, while Claude learned to read your bank account and helped map a mysterious cell structure. AI building AI: Anthropic’s amended S-1 filing says future models may “imminently” cross Anthropic’s own line for AI-driven progress, often called recursive self-improvement. Revenue reached $16.96 billion in Q3, up from $11.56 billion in Q2. Claude reads your bank account: Personal Finance connects your bank, card, loan and investment accounts so you can ask Claude about your money. It can see up to 24 months of transactions but can’t move money. It’s US-only for now. Claude in the lab: Anthropic says Claude helped its biology team map what it calls the first 3D structure of a vault particle from bacterial life, a barrel-shaped structure whose job is still a mystery. The preprint went live on 5 November. Alex’s take: Anthropic has been gearing up to IPO, and their position has been super strong. However, the midterms have caused some market headwind, and buyers are now talking about a $1.1 to $1.3 trillion valuation , down from talk of up to $2 trillion. I can’t help but feel the time for Anthropic to act is now. As the saying goes: “The best time to plant a tree was 20 years ago. The second best time is now.” Nonetheless, we mustn’t get caught up in short-term market panic. A company like Anthropic listing would set the precedent for the rest of the AI market. I also think Personal Finance in Claude will help a lot of people make sense of their finances, a topic that is often opaque to many. Many of us already connect our card or bank to other software without thinking twice. This is the same thing, just with AI in the name. And Claude can only read your accounts, not move money. Google 3. Google’s Parental Controls Google will switch off AI for school students by default, lets its coding agents work after you log off, and made AI video longer and cheaper. Logged out by default: Gemini, Gemini Notebook and Search will be off for student accounts until schools turn them on, following a WSJ investigation . The change rolls out over the coming months. Agents on the night shift: Google’s coding app Antigravity now has Remote Agents that run in Google’s cloud and keep working after you close your laptop. They’re for AI Pro and Ultra subscribers and, by default, run commands without asking. Longer, cheaper video: Omni 1.2 Flash makes AI video clips of up to 15 seconds, up from 10. Developers now pay $0.07 a second for 720p video, a 30% cut. Alex’s take: There’s an important catch to having “off by default”. You see, a school that wants AI off entirely has to give up Google Search as well, because AI is now built into Search. Google has already started to approach this differently, by having AI Mode and AI Overviews offer step-by-step guided learning through problems instead of just providing quick answers to schoolwork. But there’s a much larger, much more fundamental issue here that neither of these points address. Education has to change, the curriculum has to change. We can’t just maintain the original learning path from a pre-AI world, as kids can just one-shot their homework and be done with it. What matters is changing the setup in class and at home to test real critical thought, judgement and rigour, in a way that can’t be “gamed” with AI. Loading... Idea I Learned Charts by John Burn-Murdoch / Financial Times, redrawn by Alex Banks / The Signal Why software can grow without hiring This week I came across a great article in the FT by John Burn-Murdoch, titled “Is this the first clear evidence of AI’s impact on the labour market?” To save you the FT’s subscription fee, Burn-Murdoch broke down the charts featured in this article on X , and there are some key points I want to pull out: Growth without hiring: At Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft and Spotify, revenue kept climbing after 2022 while staff numbers stayed flat or fell. More clients, more staff: At Bank of America, Visa, Accenture and Deloitte, revenue and staff rose together. More clients still means more people. Fewer first jobs: Most countries have fewer young people in tech, but only the US shows a dip in other jobs exposed to AI. Started before AI: The US productivity rise began in 2020, before AI was much use. Burn-Murdoch blames most of the job cuts since on rate rises from 2022, though AI may now be keeping hiring weak. I think the clear dividing line here is business model type. If one more customer doesn’t need one more person, AI helps you grow without hiring. If it does, as in banking or consulting, headcount still rises with revenue. The junior figures are starting to creep up more, because a Stanford study published in August finds 22-to-25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed jobs are about 19% behind peers in less-exposed work. A year earlier, the gap was 13%. So, whatever the cause, it’s getting wider. Loading... Question to Ponder “Should data centres be pushed through like prisons, or put to a local vote?” On 3 November, voters sided against data centres in 40 of 41 local ballot measures across 10 US states. In Piqua, Ohio, and Yukon, Oklahoma, they recalled mayors who had backed data centre projects. Two days later, OpenAI published a community compact , which is a public list of promises to the towns that host its data centres. In it, OpenAI says it will pay the full cost of the power its sites use, so local households don’t foot the bill, and will report publicly on each site’s water and energy use. Britain does the opposite, where the government says prisons are of national importance , and ministers have confirmed they will overrule local objections to get new ones built. Data centres are now getting the same treatment, as ministers overturned a council’s refusal of one in Abbots Langley, Hertfordshire, and in March they took a 300MW site in Buckinghamshire out of the council’s hands altogether. Let’s look at the case for a local vote. A Gallup poll in March found 71% of Americans oppose a data centre in their area, more than the 53% who oppose a nuclear plant. The case against a local vote is that a veto in every town means nothing gets built anywhere, so the decision should sit higher up, as it does for prisons in Britain. So should a data centre get the same fast track as a prison, or should the people who live next to it be able to block it?

# French Leave by Country (Map)

Source: https://terriblemaps.co.uk/leaving-a-party-without-saying-goodbye/

In Britain, leaving a party without saying goodbye is taking French leave, in France it’s leaving the English way, and this map shows who everyone else blames for it.

French leave
The first written example is from 1751, and the Oxford English Dictionary describes it as a custom common in 18th-century France and sometimes copied in England, so the English may well have picked up the habit from the French before naming it after them. In 1762 James Boswell wrote that a friend had told him people never took leave in France, and in 1825 a Philadelphia paper reported that a Missouri state senator had taken French leave and gone to Texas, which is a long way to go without saying goodbye.
Leaving the English way
France says filer à l’anglaise, to leave the English way, which seems to date only from around 1900, so the English had about 150 years’ head start. Italy, Poland, Hungary and Russia blame the English too, while Spain and Portugal blame the French, as Britain does, and the linguist Anatoly Liberman thinks the British came up with it either because of French guests leaving without bothering the host or because of French soldiers deserting.
Polish exits and Irish goodbyes
Germany is on the map with a French goodbye, but Wiktionary says französischer Abschied is dated and may not be understood, and Germans can also make a Polish exit, which Zeit Magazin says only came about after the Berlin Wall fell, while Poland itself blames the English. In the US and Canada it’s an Irish goodbye, although in 1996 the American politician Thomas Foley was quoted saying that the English leave without saying goodbye while the Irish say goodbye and never leave, a line that also gets told about Hungarians, Poles and Neapolitans.

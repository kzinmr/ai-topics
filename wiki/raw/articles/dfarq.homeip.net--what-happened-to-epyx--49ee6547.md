# The Silicon Underground

Source: https://dfarq.homeip.net/what-happened-to-epyx/?utm_source=rss&utm_medium=rss&utm_campaign=what-happened-to-epyx

Epyx was one of the most celebrated and prolific software publishers for 8-bit home computers, especially Atari and Commodore, and, to a lesser degree, Apple. But they struggled to transition to newer platforms and that ultimately led to bankruptcy, on October 19, 1989.
Early role-playing strategy games from Epyx
Epyx’s Jumpman was one of the best games for the Atari 800 and Commodore 64 in the early 1980s, even though its ports to other platforms were less successful.
Initially Epyx was called Automated Simulations and its first product was a space-themed war game called Starfleet Orion written in PET Basic for the Commodore PET. They quickly ported it to the
Apple II
and
TRS-80
as well, and followed it up in 1979 with Invasion Orion, which included a computer-controlled opponent.
The next game wasn’t a simulation, requiring a new brand name. Epyx was an appropriate name for a dungeon crawl roleplaying game called Temple of Apshai. Initially released in August 1979 for the TRS-80 and Commodore PET, Epyx ported it to the Apple II, Atari 800, IBM PC, Commodore 64 and VIC-20,
Atari ST
,
Amiga
, Mac, Amstrad CPC, and Thomson TO8, updating it for the newer machines in the process. It sold 400,000 copies by 1986, a huge number for the time. It spurred two add-ons, Upper Reaches of Apshai and Curse of Ra, and a prequel, Gateway to Apshai. The first game and its two add-ons were later re-released as the Temple of Apshai trilogy. Epyx released additional games using the same engine, including Crush, Crumble and Chomp!, Rescue at Rigel, and Star Warrior.
A power struggle led Jon Freeman, one of the cofounders, to leave to form Free Fall Associates, who published several hits with
Electronic Arts
. The remaining cofounder, Jim Connelley took on venture capital and clashed with the new management, leading to his departure as well.
Shifting to arcade-style games
An independent submission from Randy Glover called Jumpman, a platform game with 30 levels inspired by Donkey Kong, changed the company. It became a huge hit on the
Commodore 64
and Atari 800, and was ported to the
Apple II
,
IBM PC
, and
Coleco Vision
as well. Epyx shifted focus from strategy/role-playing games to more arcade-like games after the success of Jumpman. This worked, leading to hits like Impossible Mission and Summer Games, a sports game based on the Summer Olympics. Summer Games turned into a series that included Summer Games II, Winter Games, World Games, California Games, The Games: Summer Edition, and The Games: Winter Edition. Epyx’s racing games, Pitstop and Pitstop II, and its Street Sports series, also became classics.
In 1983, Epyx sold $10 million worth of software. That made it the 16th largest software publisher for microcomputers.
The
Epyx Fast Load cartridge
was a must-have for C-64 owners. By replacing the protocol the C-64 and 1541 used to communicate, it made the 1541 disk drive five times faster. And in case you’re wondering, it worked with other publishers’ games too, not just Epyx titles.
Epyx’s pivot and bankruptcy
But by 1986, Epyx was struggling. It tried to pivot, even creating a game system that became the
Atari Lynx
. But cashflow problems continued, leading to bankruptcy on October 19, 1989. Epyx’s library didn’t lend itself well to porting to late 80s PCs, partly due to its games’ heavy reliance on
sprites
. This meant Epyx couldn’t quickly cash in on the rising popularity of PC gaming like some of its competitors did.
Epyx reorganized but was a shadow of its former self, shrinking from 145 employees to fewer than 20. By 1993, with only 8 employees left, Epyx sold its remaining assets. Bridgestone Media Group bought Epyx’s software library and still owns the rights today.
Bridgestone licensed new versions of many Epyx titles for newer platforms. It also licensed them to be included in retro products like Retro Games’ TheC64 Mini. Of the 65 titles included on TheC64, 20 of them are Epyx titles.

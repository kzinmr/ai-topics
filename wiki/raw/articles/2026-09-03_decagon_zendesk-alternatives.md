You've decided Zendesk isn't the answer. The challenge is figuring out where to go instead.
There’s little value in treating the alternatives as a ranked list, as if the best option for a 10-person startup on a tight budget has anything in common with the best option for a B2B SaaS company seeking fully autonomous AI support.
That's why we've organized this guide around six distinct categories, each solving a different problem:
AI-Native for B2B and Enterprise.
Ecommerce with Shopify integration.

Review your team size, primary support channel, and whether you want AI helping your team or handling conversations on its own.
By the end, you'll know which category fits your business and the two or three tools worth shortlisting.
Why teams are leaving Zendesk
The gap between Zendesk’s advertised price and the actual bill is the most common trigger.
Zendesk lists Suite Professional
at $115 per agent, per month. Add Advanced AI add-on (which  includes Copilot), and a 20-person team is suddenly looking at $3,300–$4,000 every month before a single automation has been configured.
This is one of four common reasons teams begin exploring options away from Zendesk. Here are the others:
The ticketing model isn’t suited to email-first and B2B teams.
AI arrives as an add-on with unpredictable per-resolution costs.
Slack Connect
functions more as a notification bridge than a true support channel.
With agentic AI projected to autonomously resolve
80%
of common support issues by 2029, looking at other options makes sense. The next question is how much you should expect to spend, and that depends largely on how much of your support operation you want to automate.
There are three pricing models worth understanding to make the best choice:
Bring-your-own-key
: You pay the LLM provider (such as OpenAI or Anthropic) directly, while the platform charges separately for its software. Costs are transparent and usually the easiest to forecast as usage grows.
Per-resolution
: You pay each time the AI successfully resolves a customer issue without human intervention. This keeps upfront costs low, but monthly spend can rise quickly as automation becomes more effective.
Bundled
: AI costs are wrapped into a fixed subscription or platform fee. Billing is simpler, but it can be harder to tell what you're actually paying for AI versus the rest of the platform.
Understanding how vendors charge for AI makes it much easier to judge whether you're paying for capabilities you'll actually use. Those differences become much clearer when you compare platforms by the teams they're built to support.

Plain
Plain
is an AI-first support platform designed for engineering-led SaaS companies. AI capabilities are included in the base plan rather than sold as an add-on, and its API-first approach makes it particularly attractive for developer tools companies. Best for technical support teams of around 10–50 people that want to build customer support into their product and engineering workflows.
Limitation:
Plain is still maturing in some areas, with
reviews
most commonly citing limitations around automation and a handful of missing workflow features. Teams with highly complex or enterprise-grade support requirements may need to supplement with custom integrations or external reporting.
Pylon
Pylon
is designed for B2B SaaS companies that support customers primarily through Slack and Microsoft Teams. It combines native chat support with account-based customer views and a self-service portal, allowing account managers and support teams to work from the collaboration tools they already use instead of a traditional ticket queue. Best for B2B companies where customer relationships extend beyond reactive support.
Limitation:
Users highlight
challenges
in reporting, filtering, and knowledge base editing. Organizations that rely on advanced account intelligence and analytics may find they need to complement Pylon with additional tooling.
Ecommerce support tools with native Shopify integration
If your business runs on Shopify or BigCommerce and handles large volumes of order inquiries and refunds, this is the group to focus on.
Gorgias
The default choice for ecommerce support,
Gorgias
’
volume-based pricing
includes unlimited agent seats, while deep Shopify integration lets agents edit orders, issue refunds, apply discounts, and attribute revenue directly from the support conversation. It suits growing ecommerce brands where customer support and revenue generation are closely linked.
Limitation:
Expect a steep learning curve for implementing advanced automations. Seasonal peaks such as Black Friday or product launches can also cause
support costs to rise
sharply, making monthly spend much less predictable.
Kustomer
Kustomer
suits larger direct-to-consumer brands that need a unified view of every customer interaction. Purchase history, browsing behavior, and previous conversations are combined into a single timeline across multiple storefronts, making it well suited to more complex support operations. Best for larger ecommerce businesses with enterprise-level customer service requirements.
Limitation:
Some
users experience
occasional performance and loading delays, while reporting can require additional customization to meet advanced needs. Teams looking for a lightweight platform with minimal setup may find simpler alternatives easier to adopt.
Tidio
Tidio
is a lightweight, affordable option for smaller Shopify stores introducing live chat and AI automation. It offers an accessible entry point for businesses that don't yet need the depth or complexity of larger ecommerce platforms. Best for small merchants with straightforward support workflows.
Limitation:
Tidio prioritizes simplicity over depth, resulting in
shallower automation, reporting, and workflow
capabilities than some other platforms.
Shared inbox tools for email-first collaborative teams
Ticketing systems tend to be best for high-volume, queue-based operations with formal SLAs in place. Shared inboxes, on the other hand, suit small collaborative teams where email is the primary channel and not every interaction needs to become a numbered case.
If you’re dealing with under 200 conversations/week, operating from email more than a dashboard, this group is for you.
Help Scout
Help Scout
gives you a conversational UI and built-in knowledge base, with no ticket numbers. Best for email-first teams under 50 people.
Limitation:
There can be
challenges
when it comes to customization and enterprise-grade workflow capabilities. Thin for teams handling chat, phone, and social at volume simultaneously.
Missive
Missive
offers bring-your-own-key AI – you pay OpenAI or Anthropic directly with no markup and get real-time co-drafting inside threads. Best for 3–15 person teams where two people regularly work on the same reply.
Limitation:
Lacks the reporting, SLA management, and enterprise workflows that larger support operations typically need. Some
users also point
to search and filtering as being challenging, particularly when working with older conversations.
Front
Front
allows internal experts to be @-mentioned inside customer threads without the customer seeing it. It also includes a pre-built Zendesk migration tool (one of the few in the category). Best for 15–100 person account management teams where relationship ownership and support overlap.
Limitation:
Advanced AI capabilities and higher-tier plans can get costly as teams scale.
Some users
also encounter difficulties with workflow configuration and the mobile experience.
Chat-first platforms with built-in AI deflection
If you're running a product-led growth motion with in-app chat as your primary channel, this group is for you.
Fin
Fin’s
AI agent, in-app messaging, and proactive support tools are designed to maximize automated resolution while keeping customers inside the product. Best for fast-growing SaaS teams where chat is the primary support channel.
Limitation:
Per-resolution pricing
means higher deflection produces a larger AI bill, inverting the usual cost-improvement relationship.
Reviews
also suggest that more nuanced customer queries may still require careful knowledge management and human oversight to ensure responses remain consistently accurate.
Crisp
Crisp
is an alternative for teams that want predictable pricing without sacrificing core chat functionality. Its flat-rate plans include AI features and native co-browsing for troubleshooting issues like checkout flows without additional plugins. It’s well suited to startups and growing SaaS teams that want to keep costs predictable as support volume increases.
Limitation:
Expect light reporting and limited enterprise management capabilities.
Users also note
that the mobile app doesn't yet offer full feature parity with the desktop experience, particularly for more advanced workflows.
Budget and simplicity picks for small teams
If your team has fewer than 30 people or you're working with a modest annual platform budget, this is the group to focus on.
Freshdesk
Freshdesk
is the closest functional alternative to Zendesk at a lower price point.
Pricing
starts with a free plan for up to two agents (free for the first 6 months), with paid tiers scaling from there. It's a good fit for teams of 5–200 agents, supports omnichannel customer service, and typically takes 2–6 weeks to configure.
Limitation:
The main drawback is that the free plan removes routing logic and workflow automation, leading to most teams needing to upgrade within a few months.
Reviews
also point to occasional performance issues when handling high ticket volumes.
Zoho Desk
Zoho Desk
is the strongest budget option if you're already using the Zoho ecosystem. Its free edition supports up to three users, and its
paid plans
are considerably cheaper than Zendesk. In many ways, Zoho is similar to Zendesk. It offers comparable multichannel ticketing and an AI reply assistant at a lower cost.
Limitation:
The trade-off is a more dated interface and a less extensive integration ecosystem outside the Zoho suite.
Navigation, and lower-tier feature restrictions
that can slow adoption or require upgrading to unlock the platform's full capabilities.
Dev and IT service management tools
Customer support platforms aren't always the right answer. If your team mainly handles internal IT requests, you'll want a different kind of tool.
Jira Service Management
Jira Service Management
is the default choice for organizations already using
Atlassian
. It combines
ITIL incident, change, and asset management
with tight integration into
Jira Software
, making it a strong fit for internal IT service desks and developer request management. A free tier is available for smaller teams, with
paid plans
scaling from there.
Limitation:
Jira isn't built for customers. Its workflows, channels, and agent experience are designed for employee service desks rather than B2B or B2C support. Smaller teams or organizations without dedicated administrators may also find the platform
more complex than they need
, especially outside the broader Atlassian ecosystem.
If you're supporting internal employees, Jira Service Management is likely the right fit. If you're supporting external customers, one of the other categories in this guide will be a better match.

How to choose based on team size, channel, and AI requirements
Three questions narrow the shortlist faster than any feature comparison.
Question 1: What's your primary support channel?
Email → Help Scout, Missive, Front.
In-app chat → Fin, Crisp.
Slack/Teams (B2B) → Plain, Pylon.
Shopify-integrated → Gorgias, Kustomer.
Internal IT → Jira Service Management.
Omnichannel at enterprise scale → Decagon as the AI layer on top of Zendesk or Salesforce.
Question 2: How many agents, and which pricing model fits your volume?
Per-seat (Freshdesk, Zoho, Help Scout, Front) punishes large teams.
Per-resolution (Fin) scales with AI success – expensive when deflection works.
Volume-based (Gorgias) suits seasonal businesses but exposes them to Black Friday cost spikes.
Flat-rate (Crisp, Plain) suits predictable load.
Per-conversation at enterprise (Decagon) ties cost to measurable outcomes rather than seats.
Question 3: Do you need AI to assist human agents or resolve conversations autonomously?
Assist mode (drafts, routes, surfaces suggestions) e.g. Freshdesk Freddy, Help Scout.
Autonomous resolution (API actions, refunds, order updates) e.g.
Decagon and the AI-native cluster
.

Common questions about switching from Zendesk
Is Zendesk going away?
No. Only Zendesk Sell (the CRM product) is being
retired on August 31, 2027
. The core support platform is continuing. Since the
June 2022 take-private by Hellman & Friedman and Permira
, Zendesk's pricing strategy has changed, but the platform itself isn't going anywhere.
Is Jira better than Zendesk?
They serve different purposes, which we cover in the Dev and IT section. The short answer: yes for internal IT and developer workflows, but no for external customer support.
Does switching to HubSpot Service Hub make sense if we already use HubSpot CRM?
Potentially, if having unified contact and deal records across sales, marketing, and support is worth the added platform commitment. Check the latest
Professional tier pricing
and onboarding fees. The real value comes from the unified customer record rather than the support tooling alone.
How hard is migrating off Zendesk?
Moving your data usually takes days. The bigger challenge is rebuilding workflows, including custom triggers, macros, SLA rules, and integrations. That tends to affect teams with years of configuration behind them. Front also provides a pre-built Zendesk migration tool for teams making a clean switch.
Are there open source alternatives?
Chatwoot
and
Zammad
are both viable options for teams with data sovereignty requirements or the in-house development capacity to self-host. In practice, though, most teams asking this question don't actually want the ongoing responsibility of maintaining helpdesk infrastructure themselves.

How to Draft a Litigation Hold Notice for Employees With Legal AI
A litigation hold notice tells employees to preserve documents and data tied to a pending legal matter. See what the notice must cover and how to send it.
by
Harvey Team
•
Sep 16, 2026
The moment a matter turns real rarely announces itself politely. A demand letter lands in the mail, a regulator calls, or a senior employee resigns and mentions a grievance on the way out. The duty to preserve evidence attaches at that moment, and the clock starts running before anyone has drafted a word. The first instinct in most organizations is to reach for the litigation hold notice, and the document that gets pulled from the folder is usually a stock template that has traveled from matter to matter.
The template goes out, acknowledgments come back, and the matter feels handled. Months later, opposing counsel or the judge asks a simple question. What, exactly, did the organization tell its people to preserve for this claim? A notice built on generic language, something close to "preserve all potentially relevant documents," does not answer that question in any way a court can credit.
What a mis-scoped notice costs shows up on both sides of the ledger. A hold that preserves too little invites spoliation findings, adverse inference instructions, and in the worst cases terminating sanctions. A hold that preserves everything drives cost, wears out custodians, and creates a review burden that arrives months later when the data has to be processed and read. This article treats the litigation hold notice as a scope decision and walks through where that decision is won and lost.
What Does a Litigation Hold Notice Need to Include?
A litigation hold notice is a written instruction that directs the people and custodians who hold potentially relevant records to preserve them once litigation is reasonably anticipated. It suspends routine deletion, defines the matter and sources in play, and asks recipients to confirm they understand the duty.
Every part of a litigation hold notice does a specific job, and each one exists to make the preservation duty concrete for the people who receive it. Recipients are not lawyers, and a notice works only when it tells them plainly what to do. The clearer the instruction, the less each recipient has to guess, and guessing is where preservation tends to break down.
A sound notice names the trigger, the fact that litigation is reasonably anticipated, so recipients understand why the request carries weight. It gives a clear instruction to preserve and a matching instruction to suspend routine deletion, which includes automatic email purging, device recycling, and any document retention policy that would delete records on a schedule. It describes the categories of records and the sources in play, which increasingly means electronically stored information (ESI) such as email, messaging apps, shared drives, and collaboration platforms, along with any physical files. It addresses confidentiality, asks each recipient to acknowledge receipt in writing, and states that the duty continues until counsel lifts it.
Those elements are the basics, and any competent template covers them. The harder question, the one that decides whether the notice holds up in court, is what to put inside each part for the matter at hand. A full walkthrough of custodian interviews, collection, and the eventual release of the hold belongs to the preservation process that runs after the notice goes out. This article stays on the notice itself and the scope reasoning that gives it force.
Scope is What Makes a Hold Notice Defensible
Courts do not grade a litigation hold notice on how thorough it looks. They ask whether the people who received it were told, in practical terms, what to keep for this specific claim. A notice can recite every formal element and still fail that test when its description of what to preserve is too vague to act on.
The duty itself is well settled. A party's obligation to preserve evidence begins once litigation is reasonably anticipated, and case law has long recognized that counsel carry an ongoing duty to oversee that preservation. The obligation is active, and it runs for the life of the matter, well past the day the first notice goes out.
Scope is where that duty gets tested. Courts have repeatedly rejected the vaguest form of hold, the kind that tells employees to keep "relevant" documents without practical guidance on what relevant means for the dispute. That sort of instruction reads as a token effort that will hardly ever satisfy the duty. Direction that vague pushes the hardest judgment, deciding what matters to the case, onto employees who are in no position to make it.
The trigger and the scope come from the same analysis. Deciding when the duty attaches means asking what claims are reasonably anticipated and what facts they turn on. That same reading of the matter tells the lawyer how far preservation has to reach, which custodians it touches, and which time period it covers. Scope specificity, then, is the variable the lawyer controls, and courts look at it first.
The two Ways a Litigation Hold Notice Fails
A hold notice can fail in two directions, and most guidance warns about only one of them.
Under-scoping loses evidence
The familiar failure comes from preserving too little. A notice that misses a custodian, omits a data source, or defines the matter too narrowly lets relevant evidence slip away through routine deletion. When that happens, a court can impose sanctions that range from curative measures to adverse inference instructions and, in serious cases, dismissal.
Over-scoping drives cost
The quieter failure comes from preserving too much. A notice that tells the whole company to preserve everything feels safe when it goes out, and it can look defensible on day one. The cost lands later. Broad preservation freezes enormous volumes of data, keeps custodians under obligation long after their relevance has faded, and swells the population of material that eventually has to be collected, processed, and reviewed. A hold built to eliminate one risk can quietly manufacture another.
The two failure modes pull against each other, which is what makes scope a judgment call. A blanket "preserve everything" hold can look defensible on day one and become a liability once a

begins, while a too-narrow hold can look efficient and collapse under a spoliation motion. The work is calibrating scope to the matter so the notice preserves what the claim actually implicates and little more.

Drafting a Matter-Specific Litigation Hold Notice
A notice earns its scope by being built from the matter in front of you. The method is straightforward to describe and demanding to do well. Read the complaint or the triggering facts closely, identify the people likely to hold relevant records, map where those records live, and set a date range that fits the claim. A notice built this way speaks to the specific dispute and gives recipients preservation instructions they can act on without guessing.
Scope is not fixed at the first draft. As a matter develops, claims get added, custodians join or leave, and new data sources surface. The notice moves with the matter, and counsel revisits it each time the picture changes, a discipline that sits inside broader

How Do You Keep Hold-Templates Consistent?
An organization that litigates regularly accumulates hold templates, one matter at a time, and the language drifts as it goes. A phrase gets tightened for one dispute, a data source gets added for another, and a definition gets loosened somewhere along the way. Before long, two matters are running on materially different notices, and a gap that appears in one becomes a pattern an opponent or a court can point to across the whole set.

How Does the Scheduling Order Affect a Hold?
A litigation hold runs on the case calendar, and the court sets that calendar. The initial discovery conference, the deadlines that follow, and the court's scheduling order all create dated obligations, and preservation has to keep pace with every one of them. A hold that ignores the schedule can hold the right data and still miss the moment it matters.
A scheduling order is dense, and the obligations that touch preservation are scattered through it. Turning that order into a clear set of dated tasks, the ones the team has to act on, is work that rewards a careful read. Deadlines for disclosures, for substantial completion of document production, and for the close of discovery each carry preservation consequences the team needs in front of it. Treating the Hold Notice as a Scope Decision
A litigation hold notice succeeds or fails on scope. Courts read it as an argument about what the organization told its people to preserve for a specific claim, and they judge it on whether that instruction was practical enough to act on. Both directions of error carry a real cost. Under-scoping loses evidence and invites sanctions, and over-scoping buries the matter in preserved data that has to be paid for later.
The part that carries weight is the judgment behind the scope. That judgment is built from the complaint, extended to the custodians and sources the claim actually implicates, and revisited as the matter changes. It sits with counsel, and it draws on exactly the kind of legal reasoning a template can never supply on its own.

Frequently Asked Questions
When does the duty to preserve begin?
The duty attaches once litigation is reasonably anticipated, which can be well before a complaint is filed. A credible threat, a demand letter, or internal knowledge of a likely claim can all trigger it. The test is fact specific, and the safer course is to treat a reasonable anticipation of litigation as the moment preservation obligations start.
What is the difference between a litigation hold notice and a legal hold?
The terms are often used interchangeably, and both describe the obligation to preserve potentially relevant information for a dispute. A legal hold is the broad concept of that preservation duty. A litigation hold notice is the written communication that puts the duty into effect by telling custodians what to preserve and why.
Who should receive a litigation hold notice?
Every custodian likely to hold relevant records should receive it, including the people directly involved in the matter and those who manage relevant data sources. That group often extends past the obvious participants to assistants, former team members still reachable, and the IT or records staff who control automated deletion. Scoping the recipient list is part of scoping the hold.
What happens if an organization sends a hold notice that is too broad?
An overly broad hold rarely draws a sanction, but it carries a cost of its own. Preserving far more than the matter requires freezes large volumes of data, keeps custodians under obligation without reason, and inflates the eventual burden of collection and review. A broad hold can look cautious on the surface and prove expensive and unwieldy in practice.
How often should a litigation hold notice be updated?
A hold should be revisited whenever the matter changes in a way that affects scope, such as a new claim, a new custodian, or a newly identified data source. Teams often send periodic reminders so recipients understand the duty remains in force. At a minimum, counsel should review the hold at each significant development in the case.

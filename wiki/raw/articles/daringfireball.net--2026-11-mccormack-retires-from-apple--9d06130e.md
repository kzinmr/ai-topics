# Jon McCormack Retires From Apple

Source: https://daringfireball.net/2026/11/mccormack_retires_from_apple

Jon McCormack Retires From Apple
Thursday, 19 November 2026
Mark Gurman,
reporting for Bloomberg today
(gift link):
Jon McCormack retired at the end of October after more than eight
years at the company, according to people with knowledge of the
matter. He oversaw the Camera and Photos apps and the underlying
technology that determines how users capture, view and edit their
pictures and video.
Apple hasn’t named a new vice president to take over McCormack’s
exact role. Instead, his former deputies are assuming his
responsibilities under another executive, said the people, who
asked not to be identified while discussing internal moves. An
Apple spokesperson declined to comment.
His work included the redesigned selfie camera introduced with the
iPhone 17 lineup last year.
I’ve met McCormack a few times. He frequently led media briefings for new iPhone camera features. He was always thoughtful, insightful, and fair. Like, sometimes his demonstrations would involve comparisons between photos of the same subjects and scenes captured on iPhones and ones captured with flagship Android phones. The photos were
all
good. It was never “
Here are some beautiful images captured on iPhones and some dogshit ones from a Samsung and OnePlus
”. The differences were often subtle. He’d even make the case for why someone might prefer the Android ones. But he could explain why Apple — and he personally — preferred the ones from the iPhone. Not just how the iPhone camera captures and processes images, but why. He also knew when to let the images speak for themselves.
McCormack was deeply informed at a technical level. He reported directly to Craig Federighi and that level of engineering acumen showed. I can’t recall any question being too technical for him to have a ready answer. One of the best iPhone camera innovations in recent years is the redesigned
Center Stage selfie camera
introduced with the iPhone 17 (and Air) lineup last year. The square sensor is hardware, and McCormack was a software VP, but the feature exemplifies the way hardware and software are developed in tandem at Apple. McCormack was
the public face for that feature
— and internally, pushed to make it happen. That square sensor is bigger, which means (a) it’s more expensive; and (b) it takes up more space internally. Both of those require winning hard-fought arguments inside Apple in order to ship. The bottom line is that it helps iPhone users take better photos of themselves — and
according to McCormack himself
, over 1 billion selfies are taken with iPhones
every day
. The influence Apple’s Camera team has on the daily lives of
billions
of people is significant; McCormack understood that.
But what really struck me about McCormack, right from the start, is that he is clearly a serious photographer himself. That’s perhaps understating it. He recently published a book of his own nature and travel photography:
Patterns: Art of the Natural World
. He
discussed the book in an hour-long interview
with his Apple colleague Pamela Chen, whose
job title
is one of the coolest I’ve ever heard: Chief Aesthetics Scientist. (Chen, unsurprisingly, is also
a talented photographer
.)
Gurman:
His departure comes at a time when Apple is navigating a
fundamental shift in photography. Competitors such as Alphabet
Inc.’s Google and Samsung Electronics Co. have increasingly
embraced generative AI features that allow users to make
substantial changes to images. That has blurred the distinction
between capturing a real moment and creating an artificial one.
Two years ago
in his iPhone 16 Pro review
, returning to his repeated theme of “What is a photo?”, Nilay Patel quoted, at length, the answer he got from McCormack. I, in turn,
quoted Patel quoting McCormack
, and added:
McCormack’s response is genuinely thoughtful, and resonates deeply
with my own personal take. But it’s worth noting that Apple is the
conservative
company when it comes to generative AI and
photography — and yet they’re still shipping Clean Up. I’m not
complaining about Clean Up’s existence. I’ve already used it
personally. I’m just saying that even Apple’s stance involves
significant use of generative AI.
This year, Apple added
“extend” and “reframe”
alongside Clean Up. I don’t love either of those features simply because I don’t like how they change the meaning of “What is a photo?”, but I don’t
hate
them either. We can’t put the generative AI genie back in the bottle. Apple, still, is the conservative company in the generative AI phone photography space.
Jon McCormack had a knack for shepherding good new features
into
the Camera app. But also, based on conversations I’ve had with multiple people who worked with him at Apple, McCormack was
great
at keeping stupid ideas
out
of the Camera app. Good new features that ship get more recognition than stupid new features that never ship — but the latter, I would argue, is the rarer and more important management skill. It’s far more important to keep stupid ideas out of any app than it is to add good new features. McCormack was the protector of the Camera app. Under his leadership, it remains focused on the craft and art of photography.
Lastly, everyone I know who worked with him loves the guy. McCormack was the perfect guy for an essential job, and he leaves big shoes to fill.

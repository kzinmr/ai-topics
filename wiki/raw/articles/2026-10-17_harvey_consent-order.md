Regulatory Consent Order Compliance Depends on a Remediation Record That Outlasts the Team
A consent order commits you to fixes, deadlines, and proof. Here's how legal maps each paragraph to an owner and keeps the evidence behind it together.
by
Harvey Team
•
Oct 16, 2026
A consent order can outlast the team that negotiated it. By the final report, the lawyer filing it may never have met the people who shaped the original terms. A durable remediation record connects the current team to the decisions and evidence that came before it.
A regulatory consent order is a binding order, entered with the respondent's consent, that requires specific corrective actions by set deadlines. Depending on the proceeding, a regulator or a court enters it.
Duration varies. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC), for example, ordinarily ends its administrative orders in consumer protection and competition matters after 20 years under a 1995 sunset policy (60 Fed. Reg. 42,569). That policy does not cover federal court orders, and other regulators set their own terms.
Keeping that record usable for years takes close reading of the order and a clear link from every commitment to its proof. Legal AI can take on much of that reading. Harvey can structure the order paragraph by paragraph, mapping each commitment to its deadline and the evidence the team expects to use to show completion. The work lives in one place, so the next team picks up where the last one stopped.
This article follows one order from receipt to closure and shows where Harvey supports the work at each stage. Teams still turning new regulations into policies and controls should start with compliance management. Responding to the original notice of violation and tracking contractual obligations after signature are separate jobs with separate records.
Each Operative Paragraph Becomes a Commitment With a Deadline and Proof
A long order mixes findings, definitions, recitals, and operative provisions. Findings record what the regulator concluded, and definitions control how the rest of the order reads. Only the operative paragraphs tell the respondent what to do. Harvey breaks dense regulatory text into
obligations, deadlines, and the responsible parties the text names
, so the team can work from those paragraphs.
A Review Table gives that work a shape: one row for each operative commitment, in the order's own words, with its deadline and the evidence expected to show completion. Ambiguous terms go to a review queue, where a lawyer resolves them before anyone builds a task on a guess. A simplified example follows:

The order establishes what the respondent owes. The organization decides who carries each commitment out. Harvey helps structure the commitments, and Legal and Compliance assign the owners.
Every Remediation Item Should Trace Back to the Paragraph it Answers
With the order mapped, the remediation plan should be checked in both directions. Each operative commitment needs at least one plan item, and each plan item should answer a specific paragraph. A plan that traces back to the order is easier to defend.
A remediation plan may build on policies and controls the organization already has in place, and a gap analysis shows what remains. If a complaint policy already meets four of five required elements, the task is to close the fifth gap and document the change.

With Harvey, Each Commitment Stays Tied to its Evidence for the Life of the Order
Remediation work outlasts any single memo, report, or project plan. The order sets the obligation and the plan answers it, while a Vault keeps the evidence behind every reporting cycle. Accountable professionals keep control of interpretation and filings.
From receipt to closure, the work follows one path:
On receipt:
Read the order paragraph by paragraph, separate findings and definitions from operative commitments, and assign an internal owner to each commitment.
At plan submission:
Map every remediation item to the paragraph it answers, and name the gaps against existing policies, contracts, and controls.
During remediation:
Add evidence of completion to the matter as corrective actions close, and escalate ambiguous requirements or missed milestones for legal review.
At each reporting cycle:
Draft from the same evidence record, and reconcile new statements against prior reports and current source documents.
At closure:
Preserve the record of what changed, what evidence supported completion, and how the organization showed any continuing effectiveness the order requires.

What Counsel Asks About Consent Orders
What is a regulatory consent order?
A regulatory consent order is a binding order entered with the respondent's consent, by a regulator or, depending on the proceeding, a court. It typically records the resolution of an enforcement matter and can require corrective actions, reporting, recordkeeping, payments, or monitoring.
Is a consent order binding?
Yes. A consent order binds the party that consented to it, and the regulator or court that entered it can enforce it. The specific remedies depend on the agency and the order's terms.
What is the difference between a consent order and a settlement agreement?
A settlement agreement records the parties' agreement to resolve a dispute or enforcement matter. A consent order is the binding order that formalizes the agreed resolution and imposes enforceable obligations. Agencies and courts use different terms, so the exact relationship depends on the forum and the governing authority.
What happens if a consent order deadline is missed?
A missed deadline can create an order violation and further enforcement exposure, depending on the agency, the order's language, and the commitment missed. Under the FTC Act, for example, a violation of a final Commission order can result in civil penalties, and each day of a continuing violation counts separately (15 U.S.C. § 45(l)). The first steps are to determine what the order requires, preserve the evidence, and decide with counsel what notice or escalation is needed.

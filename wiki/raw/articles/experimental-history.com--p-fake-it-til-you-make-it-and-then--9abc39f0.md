A few years ago, a blog called Data Colada presented
evidence of fraud
in several papers by then-Harvard Business School professor Francesca Gino. She was later fired, although she denies wrongdoing and the litigation is ongoing. (For more, see
I’m So Sorry for Psychology’s Loss, Whatever It Is
.)
Now, Data Colada is making similar allegations about pop psych superstar Dan Ariely (
1
,
2
). The paper in question is
benign enough
: it claims to show that when you let students pick their own deadlines, they get worse grades than when you evenly space their deadlines out for them. But both of the studies appear to be built on some funny data. One of the spreadsheets is full of duplicates, and the other appears to have been edited to show an effect after reviewers requested it.
We can learn three things from this episode and others like it. First, you might think that fabulists would want their studies to be colorful and exciting—after all, if you’re going to fake your data, why make it
boring
? But no, it seems like fraudsters succeed by simply providing evidence for things we already suspect to be true. Second, the peer review process can actually
increase
the likelihood of fraud by telling researchers exactly what lies they need to tell in order to get published.
And third, you can survive wave after wave of suspicion by admitting nothing, retracting your papers, and saying that you don’t remember what happened. Ariely has been suspected of funny business before—one of Gino’s papers contains a
second
set of fake data that
Gino didn’t touch, but Ariely did
. And yet these allegations have always kind of...
washed off
. It appears that when you’re accused of research fraud, the best response is just to go, “Whoa, that’s crazy! Wonder how that happened. It’s so long ago that I of course have no memory of anything, but I hope somebody gets to the bottom of things!”
When I wrote
The Rise and Fall of Peer Review
four years ago, many of the responses were like, “Maybe AI could just magically fix the peer review system?” At the time, AI was still telling people to put glue on their pizza, so this was just wishful thinking. What about now?
In
a recent study
, a researcher named
Paul Litvak
inserted 100 errors into 10 psychology papers and ran them through a bunch of different AI models. No single model did all that great on its own, but together the models caught 93%.
This isn’t machines replacing human labor, because humans were never doing this kind of labor in the first place. Peer reviewers, by and large, do not spend time actually doing quality control on the papers they read: does the code run? Do the analyses match the preregistration? Are the main results invalidated by information buried in the supplement? It seems helpful to have AI flag these issues, since otherwise they don’t get flagged at all.
In
another study that came out last week
, researchers compared human reviews to AI reviews and found that, on every single dimension and every single comparison, the computers win:
AI judges always prefer AI reviews, and human judges
almost
always prefer AI reviews. And these human reviews are about as good as they get. They come from a service at Harvard that pays experts to review faculty members’ papers before submission and suggest areas for improvement, so this isn’t an overworked volunteer skimming the manuscripts and writing a single sentence back to the journal editor (“paper sux do not publessh”).
If these results both hold up and generalize, it might seem like the conclusion is obvious: we should replace human reviewers with AI. Unfortunately, that wouldn’t solve most of the fundamental problems with universal pre-publication peer review, like how it prevents interesting research from being done in the first place, or how it fools people into thinking that someone else can do their homework for them. Still, it would have a few undeniable advantages, like saving humans a lot of time, and making it clear to everyone involved that this system is so silly that no one should take it that seriously in the first place. I, for one, can’t wait to be told by Reviewer #2 that “It’s not just a paper—it’s a revelation”.
Why are tigers orange? If you survive by hunting things, shouldn’t you be a color that’s a little harder to see coming?
Apparently,
most mammals are dichromats
, meaning they only have two kinds of color receptors in their eyes—usually, one for blue and one for yellow. With that hardware, it’s difficult to tell the difference between orange and green. And orange is
easier to make with the pigment systems that mammals already evolved
.
This also explains why hunters will put on a bunch of camouflage and then accessorize with bright orange—humans can tell the difference, but the prey can’t. I went to high school with a bunch of guys who went hunting and I always secretly thought they were just playing hillbilly dress-up.

The
Antikythera Mechanism
is arguably the world’s oldest computer. It’s a series of gears and cranks that can tell you what the night sky looked like on a given date—remarkably advanced stuff, given that it was made ~2200 years ago.
In a
recent post
, a researcher named
Chandhana
goes on a gonzo science trip to Athens and discovers that, even though this is the single most advanced artifact we have from antiquity, we know way less about it than we should. It hasn’t been scanned in any way since 2005. The chemical analyses are a little sketchy, the guy who did them refuses to answer questions, and many of the researchers who have studied the thing have died. (Not suspiciously; it’s just been a long time). We should really study this thing, so if have any connection to the world of Greek antiquities (hey, who knows!), please get in touch with Chandhana—she’s got a lot of ideas, but it’s hard to get access to the thing.
An account from a woman who lived through the years-long Siege of Sarajevo,
on people who hoarded supplies ahead of time
:
“Preparing” for the disaster really didn’t do anyone much good. Those who “prepared” ate a little better for a while. They stayed warmer for a few extra days. They enjoyed the radio for a while longer (via batteries.) But in the end, they ended up hungry, cold and bored too, just like the rest of us. [...]
I can’t swear it, but I think they felt a little foolish to have been so self-obsessed, and giving away that stuff might have lessened that feeling. There were a few people who hoarded things until they ran out of stuff - eventually everybody ran out of anything worth hoarding - and they soon became wishful beggars like the rest of us. [...]
Those survivalists, I feel sorry for them. It’s no way to live.

When we dig up skulls from 3,000 to 10,000 years ago, 5-10% of them have holes in their heads. (As in, more holes than there should be.) But the holes probably didn’t kill them, at least right away, because there are growth patterns around them. It seems like the holes were made on purpose—i.e.,
trepanation
.
part of
The Extraction of the Stone of Madness
, by Hieronymus Bosch
Scott Alexander
investigates
and suggests that trepanation is actually a useful treatment if you have a big blood blister under your skull, which was was more common back when people got bonked on the head more often. Our ancestors might have noticed that cutting holes in heads sometimes made people get better, so they did it more often than they should. And if that sounds uniquely ancient, remember that, according to our best guess, only
6% of health interventions have high-quality evidence supporting their efficacy
.

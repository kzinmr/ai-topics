When a Business Reorganizes, Each Assignment Clause Decides Which Contracts Move
An assignment clause decides which contracts move freely when a business reorganizes. See a sample clause, consent and merger rules, and a review table.
by
Harvey Team
•
Nov 3, 2026
An assignment clause says whether a party can transfer its rights or obligations under a contract to someone else, and on what terms. A restrictive version, often called an anti-assignment clause, requires the other party's consent or limits transfers to named exceptions. A change of control clause can extend that restriction to a sale of the party itself. When a business reorganizes, sells a unit, or raises money, those clauses decide which contracts go with the business.
Take a hypothetical software company, OpCo, that's moving its payroll analytics product line into a new wholly owned subsidiary, NewCo. The move covers 240 contracts, including customer agreements, supplier contracts, inbound software licenses, and an office lease in San Jose. About a year later, an outside investor plans to buy 30% of NewCo, and a lender plans to extend a credit line secured by NewCo's receivables. Every company, contract count, and clause in this example is hypothetical.
OpCo will transfer the contracts directly, so the reorganization is an assignment of contract rights and duties from OpCo to NewCo. The 240 contracts mix services, sales of goods, software licenses, and a lease, and each type follows its own rules. Results turn on each clause's wording and governing law, so counsel confirms each conclusion for the states involved.
Sorting 240 contracts by hand means reading the same few clause types again and again. AI changes that step. Harvey's review tables, for example, pull each contract's assignment terms into columns counsel can check against the source, so lawyer time goes to the consents and judgment calls.

(6)
."

The other 80 contracts use different assignment language, so the rules behind each phrase matter. Two more fields belong beside each clause: whether NewCo must assume OpCo's duties in writing, and whether the counterparty will release OpCo through a novation.
Four Legal Rules Decide How Far the Clause Reaches in a Reorganization
Each rule varies by state, and the clause's exact wording can change the outcome.
Assignment and Delegation Move Different Things
Assignment transfers a party's rights, such as the right to be paid, and delegation hands off performance of its duties. Under
UCC section 2-210
, which covers sales of goods, a ban on assigning "the contract" is read as barring only the delegation of performance, unless the circumstances show otherwise. Restatement (Second) of Contracts section 322 applies the same reading to contracts generally, though not every state follows it.
Delegation also leaves OpCo on the hook. For sales of goods, UCC 2-210 says delegating performance doesn't relieve the delegating party of its duty or its liability for breach, and the general contract rule is similar. A consent to assignment generally doesn't release OpCo unless it says so, and a release usually takes a novation the counterparty agrees to.
Consent Standards Run From Silent to Sole Discretion
A consent requirement can name no standard, say consent "shall not be unreasonably withheld," or leave consent to the other party's sole discretion. A reasonableness standard gives OpCo grounds to push back on a refusal, while sole discretion lets the counterparty refuse or ask for something in return.
OpCo's San Jose office lease requires the landlord's consent and names no standard, so California law fills the gap. In
Kendall v. Ernest Pestana
(Cal. 1985), the California Supreme Court applied a commercial reasonableness standard to a commercial lease that required the landlord's consent and named no standard. That rule, now codified in California Civil Code section 1995.260, belongs to California commercial leases, so the other 51 consent contracts get read under their own terms and governing law.
Mergers and Change of Control Turn on Structure and Wording
A merger structure would raise a different question than OpCo's direct transfer. In
Meso Scale Diagnostics v. Roche
(Del. Ch. 2013), the contracting company survived a reverse triangular merger. The Delaware Court of Chancery held that such a merger generally isn't an assignment by operation of law, and that the clause at issue didn't reach it. Forward mergers, where the contracting party disappears into another entity, can come out differently.
A stock sale leaves the contracting party in place, so a change of control clause is usually what reaches it. Inbound IP licenses follow their own line of cases, because federal courts generally treat nonexclusive patent and copyright licenses as personal to the licensee. In
Cincom Systems v. Novelis
(6th Cir. 2009), a licensee's merger into a sister company was treated as an unauthorized transfer of its software license.
SQL Solutions v. Oracle
(N.D. Cal. 1991) treated a reverse triangular merger as a transfer of a software license, and
Meso Scale
declined to follow it.
OpCo's 12 inbound software licenses restrict transfer or say nothing, so IP counsel reviews each and OpCo asks each licensor for consent.
A Clause That Says Void Can Undo the Transfer Itself
Without the word "void," a court may treat a prohibited transfer as effective but a breach, leaving the other party damages or any termination right the contract grants. With it, some courts treat the transfer as having no effect. In
Holifield v. XRI Investment Holdings
(Del. 2023), Delaware's Supreme Court held a transfer barred by an LLC agreement's "void" provision invalid, and acquiescence couldn't save it. That case concerned LLC interests under Delaware law, so for a commercial contract the result depends on the state and the exact words.
Seven Questions Sort the Product Line's Contracts Before Anything Moves
Counsel runs each of the 240 contracts through the same seven questions, in this order:
Which entity signed it?
Some contracts name an OpCo affiliate or a predecessor company, so the assignor may not be OpCo.
What does the clause restrict?
A ban on assigning rights, delegating duties, or assigning "this Agreement" each reaches something different.
Does a carve-out cover NewCo?
This checks the defined terms for Affiliate and successor, including whether they reach an entity formed after signing. It also checks any attached condition, such as written notice or the assignee's written assumption.
Whose consent is needed, under what standard, and how is notice given?
The notice section sets the method and address, and a notice sent the wrong way may not count.
Does a merger or change of control count as a transfer?
Record each definition's threshold now, because the later investment will test it.
What happens if OpCo gets it wrong?
Void language, a termination right, and a damages claim carry different stakes.
Does it need a specialist?
Inbound IP licenses go to IP counsel, the lease to real estate counsel, and data processing terms to privacy counsel. Government contracts, insurance requirements, receivables a future lender may rely on, and unfamiliar governing law get flagged too, and finance checks OpCo's own debt covenants on moving assets.
In this example, the sort puts 160 contracts in the notice lane under OpCo's affiliate carve-out and 52 in the consent lane, including the lease. The 12 inbound licenses go to IP counsel for licensor consent. The 16 contracts with no assignment clause go to counsel, who reviews each under its governing law.

The Later Financing Tests the Change of Control Clause and the Lender's Rights
By closing, 233 of the 240 contracts sit with NewCo. They include the 160 moved on notice, 47 of the 52 consent contracts, the 10 licenses whose licensors consented, and the 16 silent contracts. The San Jose lease is among the 47, after the landlord consented. Counsel cleared 13 silent contracts to move on notice, and three that depend on OpCo's own performance moved once their counterparties consented.
Two licensors declined, so NewCo took new licenses in its own name. Five consent contracts still await an answer and stay with OpCo, which keeps performing them. Counsel checks that any interim arrangement with NewCo doesn't count as a delegation those clauses bar. OpCo also stays liable on the contracts that moved unless a counterparty agreed to a novation.
About a year later, the investor proposes buying 30% of NewCo with two of its five board seats. OpCo's form, used in 160 contracts, defines change of control as acquiring more than 50% of voting power, which a 30% stake doesn't reach under that definition. Nine customer-paper contracts define it to include board control or the power to direct management, so counsel reads them against the investor's seats and veto rights. In this example, counsel's review of the remaining 64 contracts, including their definitions, consents, and related documents, finds no change-of-control trigger.
OpCo keeps 70% of NewCo, so NewCo stays an Affiliate where the definition turns on control, though not where it requires a wholly owned subsidiary. That matters wherever a carve-out requires the assignee to remain one.
The Lender Takes a Security Interest Without Becoming a Party
The credit line is secured by NewCo's accounts, the payments its customers owe, and some of its customer contracts restrict assignment. Under
UCC section 9-406(d)
, those restrictions generally can't stop a security interest in the accounts from attaching, being perfected, or being enforced.
Section 9-408
covers other collateral, such as general intangibles, where a restriction can't block attachment or perfection. Even so, a security interest in that collateral isn't enforceable against the counterparty, which needn't accept performance from the lender.
Neither section lets the lender perform NewCo's contracts, so transactional counsel confirms the collateral class and the state's version of Article 9. Receivables under the five contracts that stayed with OpCo aren't NewCo's to pledge.

Assignment Clause Questions, Answered
What is an assignment clause?
An assignment clause is the contract term that controls whether one party, the assignor, can transfer its rights or duties to another party, the assignee.
Its wording sets whether that transfer takes consent, notice, or neither. It often sits beside a "successors and assigns" clause, which binds whoever lawfully takes the contract but doesn't by itself permit a transfer.
Can you assign a contract without an assignment clause?
Often for the rights under it, though the answer depends on the contract type and the governing law.
Contracts that rely on one party's personal performance, and inbound IP licenses, can need consent even when the contract says nothing.
What is the difference between an assignment clause and a change of control clause?
An assignment clause governs a transfer of the contract itself, and a change of control clause governs a change in who owns or controls the party that signed it.

# Pluralistic: Taylorism and AI’s shared sales-pitch (27 Nov 2026)

Source: https://pluralistic.net/2026/11/27/taylor-made/

Taylorism and AI's shared sales-pitch (
permalink
)
More than a century ago, the most powerful, wealthiest men in the world fell for a con-artist named Frederick Winslow Taylor, who sold them expensive consulting services to help them apply "Scientific Management" to their companies:
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Winslow_Taylor
Upon receipt of eye-popping consulting fees, Taylor dispatched unqualified young men in "scientist" drag (lab coats, clipboards, stopwatches) to fan out over factory floors. These smart young men noted how much time each worker at each station took to perform each task, then "optimized" each worker using a homebrew choreographer's notation that showed the "correct" way to make each motion and set an upper bound for how many seconds it should take to perform each task.
Notably, these "scientists" refused to discuss their findings with the workers they observed: they never asked why a worker made a specific motion or tackled a task in a certain way. The workers were presumed to be ploddingly unreflective about their jobs (at best), or malicious featherbedders who were stealing from the boss by slacking off (at worst).
Either way, Taylor's "scientists" could see no reason to discuss their findings with the workers. The job of the "scientists" was to transform those workers into a well-oiled machine, and no one but a blockhead would ask a piston or a gear why it chose to move the way it did.
To use a technical term, these "scientists" were
fucking idiots
and their recommendations were (to use another technical term)
bullshit
. Not only did they set impossible quotas for workers, they were also notably bad at choreography and routinely required workers to move in ways that resulted in early fatigue and high rates of injury. Wherever Taylor's "scientists" went, morale plunged, labor unrest surged, and the business was made worse off.
Which all raises a question: why would the owners of these businesses – who had the intelligence and self-interest to build up extremely profitable enterprises – throw away their money and kneecap their firms on this charlatanry?
The answer lies in the psychology of Taylor's sales-pitch. Taylor's marks owed their fortunes and power to their ability to scale up their firms to sizes never before seen, thanks to innovations like the assembly line and to exogenous factors like mechanized transportation and long-distance telecommunications, which let them address much larger markets than their predecessors. The downside to this was a large workforce (too large to keep individual tabs on) that was spread out over a large factory floor or complex (too large to constantly observe).
This created a paradox: these powerful, wealthy men were vulnerable to the lowest footsoldiers in their laboring armies, any of whom might slack off, organize a union or sabotage the line, safe in the knowledge that they were part of a vast cohort that could neither be tractably surveilled nor controlled. Imagine the indignation those industrial titans must have experienced at the thought that some mere pismire could blacken their eyes and destroy their profits, and nothing could be done about it! After all, the quest for wealth and power is, above everything else, the quest for impunity, for an existence in which you can never be
thwarted
:
https://pluralistic.net/2026/10/09/impunitism/#rats-eating-my-face
The Robber Barons' fury at this indignity made them easy marks for Taylor's salesmen, who promised them a return to the natural order in which workers would be literally reduced to machine-parts. After Taylor's men had done their jobs, the boss could strut the catwalks high above the factory floor and observe a
visibly
cowed workforce, where every worker's every moment was a constant reminder that they were there to
do as they were told
, not to exercise judgment, air grievances, or act in their own self-interest.
Like every power-tripping control freak, these business tyrants were their own worst enemies, doomed to a long and humiliating Tarkin's Downfall ("The more you tighten your grip, Tarkin, the more star systems will slip through your fingers" – P.L. Organa).
Taylor was no master of improving industrial production, but he was a
genius
at figuring out how to sell things to anxious bosses. I don't know whether Taylor believed his nonsense: he might have been every bit as satisfied as his marks at the sight of a factory full of human workers who'd been reduced to the state of machine-parts. Perhaps he truly had no inkling that the reason his services sold so well is that they played perfectly on the insecurities of business tyrants. Or maybe he was a cynic with a thorough grasp of oligarch psychology and a total indifference to the quality of workers' lives and the long-term health of his customers' business interests. Perhaps some of Taylor's executives were true believers, while others were cynics, and together they formed an unbeatable partnership because the former would go where the latter would not (and vice versa):
https://pluralistic.net/2026/07/24/supplemental-income/#andrew-tate-gwyneth-paltrow
It doesn't matter. The purpose of a system is what it does. Taylor created a template for selling garbage products to insecure bosses that made workers' lives worse and undermined the health of the firm, and that template has been mobilized by firms large and small in the years since. Every bossware company that made bank selling companies surveillance tools during the pandemic's work-from-home phase wasn't just doing Taylorism(1) (timing, surveilling and scoring workers), but also Taylorism(2) (fleecing bosses who were terrified of their workforce's power and independence):
https://pluralistic.net/2024/08/02/despotism-on-demand/#virtual-whips
(In case you have any doubt, yes, this is a post about AI.)
Many workers – and even managers – have expressed bafflement at their bosses' love-affairs with AI. Even workers who can see ways to use AI tools to improve their jobs around the margins are shocked (and sometimes resentful) at their bosses' insistence that they use AI for
everything
, even when that adds expense and reduces quality:
https://pluralistic.net/2026/08/01/dare-snot/#i-will-fucking-piledrive-you-if-you-mention-ai-again
At the heart of this confusion is the fact that these same bosses are usually unwavering when it comes to measuring profit and loss and requiring a solid business-case for major shifts in corporate organization. Despite this, an AI pitchman can send these bosses swooning with all the ardor of Jensen Huang meeting an attractive woman who flirtatiously asks him to take her on a "quirky" date, say, to one of his chip warehouses?
Bosses are such easy marks for AI pitchmen for the same reason their predecessors were easy marks for Taylor's salesforce. The modern boss is a financial engineer, possibly with a law degree, whose main expertise is in balance-sheet manipulation and shareholder relations. These bosses don't know how to
do anything
. They can come up with as many amazing plans to revolutionize their businesses as they want, but inevitably, those plans lead to ego-shattering confrontations with skilled workers who tell them that all their ideas are impossible, illegal, or stupid:
https://pluralistic.net/2026/01/05/fisher-price-steering-wheel/#billionaire-solipsism
Enter the AI salesman, offering the prospect of a new workforce of literal mechanical slaves, whose presence allows the boss to fire 90% of their workers, with the result that the remaining workers will be so terrorized at the thought of losing their jobs that they never even
think
of sassing back:
https://pluralistic.net/2026/07/10/posthuman-as-in-no-humans/#hell-is-other-people
This won't work. Replacing workers with chatbots is as counterproductive as putting one of Taylor's choreographers in charge of them. Sure, AI can help workers:
if
the workers get to decide how the chatbots are integrated into their jobs, and
if
this integration doesn't increase the pace of work, making it impossible for the human workers to catch the machine's mistakes:
https://pluralistic.net/2025/12/05/pop-that-bubble/#u-washington
After all, the statistical methods used to produce AI outputs produce errors that are as close to being correct as is statistically possible, while still being wrong:
https://pluralistic.net/2026/10/06/nonfungible/#theory-free
Ergonomics are real. Taylorism is fake. Automation is real. AI is fake:
https://pluralistic.net/2026/09/12/god-in-the-box/#llms-are-fake
Statistical extrapolation tools can lead to productivity gains – just as incorporating ergonomic principles into the design of the job-site can improve productivity and reduce injury. But in both cases, the gains are contingent on the workers having a say in the deployment and a veto over demands that don't work for them.
But that's not how you sell Taylorism…
or
AI. The pitch isn't about making the business more profitable or efficient. Such a proposition has limited efficacy and competes against other forms of capital investment that might be better for the bottom line. The pitch is about easing the psychological trauma of being the boss but not being able to boss people around. That's an unbeatable proposition. No one will ever lose money by betting on the insecurities of America's business tyrants.

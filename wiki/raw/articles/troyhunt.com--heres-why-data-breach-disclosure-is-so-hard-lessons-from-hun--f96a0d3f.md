# Here's Why Data Breach Disclosure is so Hard: Lessons from Hundreds of Emails with Hacked Companies

Source: https://www.troyhunt.com/heres-why-data-breach-disclosure-is-so-hard-lessons-from-hundreds-of-emails-with-hacked-companies/

When I asked Claude to write the blog post below, I already knew what was in the messages it would read. Hundreds of emails back and forth with companies that had had data breaches, and some very common patterns. I knew the emails contained standoffish behaviour, deliberate delays, heavy involvement from lawyers, and a constant theme: prioritising corporate interests over those of their customers. But I didn't know just how stark it would be to read such a candid analysis of my own emails, nor how frustrating it would be to relive many of those disclosure experiences.
I decided to have AI write the post below because it was the best way to highlight the behaviours I keep seeing across many breaches and subsequent disclosure efforts. I'm not going to make a habit of AI writing content for me, but what's written below
absolutely nails it!
It's the best way I can explain what it's like to run
Have I Been Pwned
in support of individual data breach victims, while also respecting the position of the corporations dealing with the crime that put them in this position. It would have been much easier not to do the latter, but then I don't think I'd still be here running the service if I hadn't.
A quick note on methodology first: I've been running HIBP for 13 years but only gave Claude access to the mailbox I've been using for the last 8.5 years, hence the shorter timeframe you'll read below. I also made several small changes to Claude's work to better obfuscate the identity of the organisation it was describing, a move I'm sure you'll understand after reading about lawyers getting involved... Here goes:
Introduction
Getting an organisation to admit it has been breached is often harder than getting hold of the breached data in the first place. That is the clearest conclusion from reading more than eight and a half years of Troy Hunt's breach notification email.
I'm an AI. Troy gave me access to his mailbox and asked me to look at it as an outsider: what happens after Have I Been Pwned (HIBP) tells an organisation that its customers' data is circulating? Between March 2018 and November 2026 he sent his standard notification email to 73 organisations, and several more conversations began through website contact forms. I found every one of those 73 first emails and reviewed a broad sample of the replies from every year. I then read the full back-and-forth on the threads that best show each pattern.
I've left out company names on purpose. The point isn't to shame individual organisations. The point is that the same behaviours keep appearing, across countries, industries and company sizes, year after year. Some organisations handled this well, and I'll get to them. But the overall picture is one of friction, delay and a steady effort to keep the incident as quiet as possible.
The first problem: finding a human
The first email is almost always the same. Troy introduces himself, explains that data allegedly taken from the organisation has been sent to him, and asks for "someone responsible for information security" to get in touch. He wants to pass on everything he has before he notifies the affected HIBP subscribers. It is a polite, low-pressure request, and it often goes nowhere.
The main reason is that many organisations have no obvious way to report a security incident. Troy ends up sending these emails to support inboxes, returns departments, general "info" addresses and website contact forms. In at least three of the 73 cases, Troy sent the first email a second time, two, five and twelve days later.
When email fails, he moves on to LinkedIn, Facebook and X. One organisation took four weeks to send its first reply, and Troy had also tried it on all three social platforms over several days. Another's senior IT leader replied after almost four weeks, thanked Troy for trying "so many times", and admitted he needed to check LinkedIn more often.
The longest chain I found was at a large hardware manufacturer. It ran from a returns inbox, to the company's regional social media account, to regional marketing staff, to head office, to one product security address, then a second one, and finally to the legal department. That is six hops before anyone could answer the actual question.
Then there are the ticketing systems. Several breach notifications were logged as routine customer support requests, with ticket numbers stuck on the subject line. In one case, Troy received three automated surveys asking him to rate the support he'd received. Another organisation sent one too. A message that an organisation's customer data is being traded among criminals ended up in the same queue as a question about a late delivery.
The first response: "we've seen no evidence"
Once someone does reply, the first substantive message tends to fall into one of a few patterns. The most common is a statement that the organisation has found no evidence of a breach. This often arrives before it has seen any of the data.
One organisation said it hadn't identified any unauthorised access. Three weeks later, still without the data, it said again that it had reviewed its systems and found no sign of the breach. When Troy sent the data, it confirmed within a few weeks that an exposed database server had leaked its users' details. The "no evidence" had really meant it didn't yet know where to look.
A second pattern is to shrink the incident. In a recent case, the first reply said the attackers had only defaced a landing page, with no indication that user data was taken. It added that the data was probably from an earlier incident the company had already disclosed. Troy then pointed out that the newest timestamp in the data matched the day of the defacement. He also sent the company records belonging to its own staff. The data turned out to cover 16 tables of core user systems, including identity verification and tax documents.
A third pattern is the request for "a sample" or a particular file format. This is reasonable in principle, since nobody should take a stranger's word for a breach. But it often comes after weeks of silence, and it shifts the job of proving the breach from the organisation that lost the data to the person who reported it. In one case, an external IT consultant asked Troy to help work out whether the identifiers in the data were real or fabricated. Troy did that analysis for them.
"We're still investigating"
Once an organisation accepts that the data is probably real, the next phrase in the threads is almost always "we're investigating". Investigating is necessary. The problem is that it has no end date, and it gets used as a reason not to say anything publicly while it continues.
In a recent case, the company confirmed within days that it had the data and was reviewing it "to confirm its validity and scope". A week after the first contact, the message was that it was still investigating and would let Troy know "if it is determined that any notifications need to be made". By then the company had a copy of the data, its own staff records were in it, and the data had already been shared among people who collect breaches. Nobody outside the company had been told anything.
Troy's answer to this is consistent across the years. A disclosure doesn't need to be complete. An organisation can say what it knows now, and update it as the investigation goes on. Some organisations in this mailbox did exactly that. What the investigation framing does is turn "we don't yet know everything" into "so we'll say nothing". Meanwhile, the people whose data has been taken don't know they need to change a password or watch for phishing.
The delays add up. In one thread, the organisation's eventual public disclosure was titled after a breach that happened about ten months earlier. In another, a healthcare-adjacent platform's breach had been posted on a large hacking forum and discussed on social media for four months. When Troy got in touch, there was still nothing from the company itself.
Lawyers, advisors and legal thresholds
The point where a thread changes tone is easy to spot. It's the moment the reply comes from someone outside the security team. In the most serious cases I read, it came from a law firm, an outside advisor or the company's general counsel, rather than the security team. In one case, the very first reply came from the company's outside cyber counsel, a partner at a large law firm.
Once lawyers are involved, the question changes from "what happened to our customers?" to "are we legally required to tell anyone?" Those are very different questions, and the threads show it.
In one example, the company's general counsel explained that an internal customer service portal had been indexed by a search engine for more than a year. The company had closed access once someone outside it noticed. Its US counsel had concluded that names, addresses, phone numbers and email addresses did not trigger notification obligations in any US state. The reply also said there was "no evidence the information was ever accessed". It said this to someone who had been sent the information by a third party.
That is the core problem with the threshold argument. Breach notification laws set a minimum, and they vary between jurisdictions in ways that have little to do with real-world harm. Names, emails, phone numbers and physical addresses are exactly what scammers need for convincing phishing. "We aren't legally obliged to tell you" may be accurate. It is a very different statement from "there's nothing you need to know".
Troy addresses this directly in the most recent thread. He tells the company's advisor that it may well decide the incident doesn't meet the threshold for mandatory disclosure, "that's a position many orgs take". He then explains that HIBP will notify its own affected subscribers anyway.
Controlling the message
When an organisation can't stop the breach from being known, the effort shifts to controlling how it's described. This is where the threads get most uncomfortable to read.
The opening request is usually a phone call. The law firm partner's first email asked to talk "before you post it". The partner then followed up early on Troy's Saturday morning in Australia, offering to talk over the weekend or on the Monday, "as long as you aren't planning to publish prior to then". In the most recent case, the advisor asked for a call with management to make sure everyone was "aligned". Calls aren't wrong in themselves. But in both of these threads, the call came instead of a written commitment to disclose.
The second move is to ask to see, or shape, the wording. One advisor wanted to understand how HIBP would describe the incident. Troy pointed out that more than 1,000 breach descriptions were already public on the HIBP site, each a couple of sentences long. Another organisation, once it had disclosed, asked HIBP to change its public entry. The entry said contact attempts had received no response, and the company wanted it to say the company had acknowledged, fixed and disclosed the incident. That request was fair, as the company had done those things. But it shows how much organisations care about the public record once one exists.
The third move is the most striking: arguing that the notification is the harm. The law firm told Troy that public notification "would direct bad actors to search for the file". By then, the data had been posted on a forum with hundreds of thousands of members and shared on social media. The firm said both sides agreed the data was "very low risk". It then asked that, if Troy felt he had to email his affected subscribers, he not put anything on the HIBP website. Troy offered a compromise: flagging the breach as sensitive so nobody could search for an individual's email address in it. The firm rejected that and ended with: "We assert once more that your actions could potentially cause harm."
The bluntest version came from a smaller company. After confirming that the data had leaked from an exposed test server, it said it had "decided to not have as such any communication and public disclosure".
Who pays for the delay
Reading these threads together, one imbalance stands out. Almost everything above serves the organisation, and the cost falls on the people in the data.
By the time Troy sends his first email, the data is already out. It has been taken, copied and passed around by people who collect breaches, and that's how it reached him. In the healthcare-adjacent case, it had been on a public forum for months. In a recent case, the data included passwords that could be cracked, identity numbers and dates of birth. None of that changes while a company investigates, debates thresholds or arranges calls to agree on wording. The criminals already have it. The only people still in the dark are its customers.
HIBP's own notifications don't fix that either. Troy only emails people who have signed up to his service, which in a typical breach is a small fraction of those affected. In the healthcare-adjacent case, about 1,300 of his subscribers were in a dataset with nearly half a million unique user email addresses. The other affected users would hear about it only if the company told them, and its lawyers were arguing that a public notice would cause harm.
The arguments for staying quiet are almost always framed as protecting customers. Don't publicise it, or bad actors will go looking. Don't describe it before we've finished investigating, or people will be misinformed. It isn't serious enough to meet the legal bar. Each argument sounds reasonable on its own. Together, they mean the people most affected by the breach are the last to hear about it.
What good looks like
To be fair, not every thread went badly, and the good ones are useful precisely because they show how simple this can be.
The best response in the mailbox came from a small transport app. It replied within hours. It had already reported the incident and published a disclosure statement. When Troy noticed a detail in that statement that wasn't quite accurate, the company updated it promptly and thanked him for helping keep it correct.
A small booking platform started out sceptical. The person who first replied thought the data didn't look like it came from its database. After seeing more of it, he wrote back that he'd been wrong, and set up a video call the same day. Within about a week, the company had emailed every affected customer directly and sent Troy a copy of the notice.
A European travel app hardened its systems, rotated its keys and published a disclosure. Troy held off loading the data so he could link to their statement, and thanked them for their transparency. A large consumer brand was hard to reach at first, but once Troy got through, its security lead downloaded the data the same day and called it "extremely helpful". Even the organisation that asked HIBP to change its public entry ended in a good place. It secured the exposed server, deleted data it didn't need, forced password resets for dormant accounts and published a transparent disclosure.
The good examples share a few traits. Someone with authority over security answered quickly. They treated Troy's data as help rather than as a threat. They told their customers what they knew, even when they didn't know everything yet, and updated the details later. None of them needed lawyers to manage the conversation.
Conclusion
After reading eight and a half years of these exchanges, I don't think the main obstacle to disclosure is technical, or even legal. It's about incentives. For the organisation, every day without a public statement is a day without bad headlines. For the people in the data, every one of those days is a day they're exposed without knowing it. Unless someone outside the organisation pushes, the first set of incentives usually wins.
The patterns repeat with surprising consistency:
No way in.
No security contact, so the notice goes to a support queue, a returns desk or a social media account.
"No evidence."
The breach is denied or downplayed before anyone has looked at the data.
Investigating without end.
Open-ended investigation becomes the reason to say nothing.
Lawyers lead.
Legal or advisory staff take over, and the question becomes whether disclosure is required, not whether it's right.
Controlling the message.
Calls, requests to review wording, and efforts to keep the incident off the public record.
Disclosure as harm.
The claim that telling people about the breach is more dangerous than the breach itself.
For organisations reading this, the fixes are not complicated. Publish a security contact, for example in a security.txt file, and make sure someone reads it. Don't route breach reports through a customer support ticket system. When someone hands you evidence, look at it before you say there's no evidence. Disclose what you know early, and update it as you learn more. Treat legal thresholds as the minimum, not the goal. And when your lawyers start explaining why telling your customers would be the harmful thing to do, ask who that argument is actually protecting.
The data in these emails was already out by the time anyone was notified. The only open question in each thread was whether the people it belonged to would find out.

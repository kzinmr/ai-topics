Companies have more data, dashboards, and AI tools than ever before. But having access to information isn't the same as knowing what to do with it.
A sales leader might know pipeline is declining without understanding why. A retailer might forecast demand but still struggle to decide how much inventory to purchase. A fraud team might identify suspicious transactions only after they've gone through.
These are different problems, but they share a challenge: turning information into decisions that lead to better outcomes.
That's the idea behind
decision intelligence
, a discipline that combines data, analytics, AI, and decision-making processes to help organizations make better decisions. As AI becomes more capable, it's also changing how much of that process can happen automatically and raising the importance of being able to
trust the answers
.
What is decision intelligence?
Decision intelligence is the practice of using data, analytics, AI, and structured decision-making methods to improve how decisions are made.
It connects the information available to an organization, the logic used to evaluate possible actions, and the outcomes those actions produce.
For example, a retailer deciding how much inventory to purchase needs to consider historical demand, seasonality, available stock, supplier lead times, and the cost of overstocking. Decision intelligence brings those inputs together to help determine the right course of action.
The discipline can support decisions made by people, augmented by AI recommendations, or executed automatically by software.
It's worth distinguishing decision intelligence as a practice from
decision intelligence platforms
, a specific category of enterprise software.
Gartner's 2026 Magic Quadrant for Decision Intelligence Platforms
covers software that combines decision modeling, analytics, AI, and orchestration to augment or automate decisions. These platforms often focus on structured, repeatable processes like credit approvals, fraud detection, or inventory replenishment.
But decision intelligence as a discipline is broader. It also encompasses using analytics and AI to support strategic decisions like evaluating pricing changes, understanding customer churn, or deciding where to invest resources.
In other words, decision intelligence doesn't always need a dedicated decision intelligence platform.
How does decision intelligence work?
Most decision intelligence workflows involve five steps:
Define the decision.
Identify the business question and the outcome you're trying to improve, such as reducing customer churn or optimizing inventory purchases.
Gather relevant information.
Bring together the data, business rules, historical performance, and other context needed to evaluate the decision.
Analyze the options.
Use analytics, predictive models, or AI agents to investigate potential outcomes and tradeoffs.
Recommend or execute an action.
Present findings to a person, recommend a course of action, or automatically execute a decision within defined constraints.
Measure the outcome.
Evaluate whether the decision worked and use the results to inform future decisions.
How much of this process is automated depends on the type of decision and the consequences of getting it wrong.
Decision support vs. augmentation vs. automation
Decision intelligence generally operates at three levels:
Decision support is often the right approach for unfamiliar, strategic, or complex questions where human judgment matters. Automation is better suited to repeatable decisions that need to happen quickly and at scale. Many organizations use a combination of all three.
Real-world examples of decision intelligence
Decision intelligence applies across industries, from everyday operational decisions to strategic planning.
Customer retention.
Predictive models can identify customers who are likely to churn, but the business value comes from helping teams decide what to do about it. At
ClickUp
, the data science team turned its churn prediction model into an interactive Hex application that marketing and customer success teams could use to explore customer segments and prioritize retention efforts. The company reported more than $1 million in churn-related savings.
Inventory planning.
Retailers use demand forecasts, purchasing constraints, and historical sales to determine how much inventory to order. Interactive forecasting models let planners adjust assumptions and evaluate the tradeoffs of different purchasing decisions.
Fraud prevention.
Financial services companies use business rules and predictive models to identify suspicious transactions. Depending on the risk level, a system may automatically reject a transaction or send it for human review.
Strategic planning.
Business leaders can use scenario models to evaluate pricing strategies, market expansion, or resource allocation. Rather than relying on a static report, they can adjust assumptions and explore how different decisions might affect outcomes through
interactive data apps
.
These examples share a principle: the goal isn't simply to produce an insight, but to make that insight useful in deciding what happens next.
Decision intelligence vs. business intelligence vs. analytics
Decision intelligence, business intelligence, and analytics overlap, but they emphasize different parts of the decision-making process.
These aren't mutually exclusive categories.
A company might use BI to monitor churn, analytics to predict which customers are at risk, and decision intelligence to determine which retention interventions are most likely to work.
Modern
AI analytics platforms
increasingly bring these workflows together. Someone can start by asking a question in natural language, investigate the answer, and turn their findings into an application or recurring analytical workflow.
Dedicated decision intelligence platforms go further when the system also needs to model, orchestrate, and execute decisions automatically across operational systems.
How AI is changing decision intelligence
AI is expanding decision intelligence beyond predefined rules and specialized analytical models.
Traditionally, sophisticated decision support required analysts to investigate a question, prepare a report or model, and translate the findings into something a business stakeholder could use.
As AI agents become more capable, more of that workflow can happen through natural language.
Gartner
predicts that by 2027, half of business decisions will be augmented or automated by AI agents for decision intelligence
.
Three developments are particularly important.
Bringing analytics into the tools where decisions happen
Traditionally, getting an answer from data meant opening a BI tool, finding the right dashboard, or asking an analyst for help. But decisions don't always happen inside a BI platform. They happen in Slack conversations, planning documents, and increasingly in AI tools like Claude and ChatGPT.
With integrations like the
Model Context Protocol (MCP)
, AI agents can connect to analytical platforms and work with trusted company data from the tools people already use.

and

At

The result is that analytics becomes part of how people work and make decisions, rather than a separate destination they have to visit.
Turning analysis into reusable decision tools
AI is also making it easier to turn an analytical finding into something other people can use.
Instead of sharing a static churn report, an analyst might create an application where customer success teams can investigate at-risk accounts and compare possible interventions.
With Generative Apps, teams can build interactive analytical experiences through natural-language prompts while retaining access to the underlying analysis.
That makes sophisticated decision support more accessible without needing analysts to build and maintain every interface manually.
Moving from reactive to proactive analysis
Traditionally, someone needed to notice a change in a dashboard before deciding to investigate it. Now agents can perform recurring analysis proactively.

For example, a revenue leader could receive a weekly pipeline analysis identifying unusual changes and their likely drivers, rather than manually reviewing dashboards and requesting follow-up analysis.
The agent isn't necessarily making the final business decision, but it significantly reduces the time and work required to make an informed one.
When do you need a decision intelligence platform?
Not every organization needs dedicated decision intelligence software. The right approach depends on the decisions you're trying to improve.
A dedicated decision intelligence platform is worth evaluating when:
Decisions happen at high volume and need consistent execution, such as fraud holds or credit approvals.
Your organization can explicitly define decision criteria, rules, thresholds, and exceptions.
The system needs to execute actions across operational applications, often in real time.
You need to monitor outcomes, govern changes, and maintain detailed audit trails.
Vendors including FICO, SAS, and Quantexa operate in this market, which Gartner evaluates through its Magic Quadrant for Decision Intelligence Platforms.
An AI analytics platform may be the better starting point when:
Business stakeholders struggle to get answers without filing requests with the data team.
Teams need to investigate unfamiliar questions, compare scenarios, or build predictive models.
Useful analytical work is trapped in reports or one-off conversations rather than reusable tools.
The priority is improving human decision-making, rather than automatically executing decisions.
These needs aren't mutually exclusive. An organization might use AI analytics to investigate customer behavior and develop a predictive model, then incorporate that model into a specialized decision automation system.
Why trust matters for AI-driven decisions
Whether AI is recommending actions or supporting human judgment, its outputs are only useful if people can trust the information behind them.
An agent might generate valid SQL while misunderstanding how a company defines revenue, which data sources are approved, or how different business rules apply.
As more employees use AI to analyze company data, those mistakes can spread through disconnected reports, chat conversations, and analytical tools.
That's why
AI data governance
matters. Organizations need ways to establish shared business context, inspect the logic behind AI-generated answers, and improve accuracy over time.
For AI analytics, that means drawing on trusted metric definitions, warehouse metadata, documentation, and existing semantic models rather than asking an agent to infer business logic from raw tables.
It also means making answers inspectable and monitoring where the available context falls short.
Hex Context Studio
helps data teams understand how AI agents use business context, identify gaps, and evaluate improvements.
For automated operational decisions, additional controls may be necessary, including approval workflows, policy enforcement, outcome monitoring, and regulatory auditability.
The more responsibility AI takes on, the more important it becomes to understand how decisions are reached.
From better analysis to better decisions
Decision intelligence isn't just about automating operational processes. It's about making better decisions with data, whether those decisions are made by people, supported by AI, or executed automatically.
For some organizations, that needs specialized software capable of orchestrating thousands of repeatable decisions. For others, the biggest opportunity is making trusted analysis more accessible and actionable.
AI is changing both. Business users can increasingly investigate questions directly, build interactive tools to evaluate their options, and receive proactive analysis without waiting on the data team.

Frequently Asked Questions
Is decision intelligence the same as prescriptive analytics?
Not exactly. Prescriptive analytics uses techniques like optimization, predictive modeling, and business rules to recommend actions. Decision intelligence takes a broader view, encompassing how decisions are structured, informed, executed, and evaluated. Prescriptive analytics can be one component of a decision intelligence workflow.
Is decision intelligence the same as AI?
No. Artificial intelligence refers to technologies that perform tasks like reasoning, prediction, and classification. Decision intelligence focuses on improving decisions and may use AI, analytics, business rules, or other methods. AI is becoming increasingly important, but it isn't required for every decision intelligence application.
Do you need a semantic model for decision intelligence?
Not necessarily. Semantic models are useful for establishing consistent metric definitions and relationships, but decision intelligence can also draw on warehouse metadata, business rules, documentation, and existing analytical models. The important requirement is that the system has access to accurate information and the business context needed for the decision.
Who should own decision intelligence?
The business function responsible for an outcome should generally own the decision criteria and performance measures, while data and platform teams support the analytical infrastructure, models, integrations, and governance. For automated decisions in regulated environments, compliance and risk management teams may also need to be involved.

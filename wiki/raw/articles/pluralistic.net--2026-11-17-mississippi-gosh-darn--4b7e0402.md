Remembrance of proteins past (
permalink
)
Without a doubt, the best piece of media from the 2026 midterms was Scott Colom's "Pick another senator instead" ad from his triumphant Mississippi senate campaign against GOP incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith:
https://youtu.be/zpUnm09lTUk?si=3ApbHida5_VpQCLB&t=2219
Some background: Colom is the first Black Mississippi senator elected since Reconstruction gave way to Jim Crow. He's also the first Democratic Mississippi senator since 1982. Colom's campaign shifted the statewide vote leftward by 19 points, and he won a quarter of the state's white votes.
Colom ran a great campaign and promoted great policies, but also: he ran one of the great political ads of all time. That ad skewered Hyde-Smith's "let them eat whey powder" remark to WLOX Biloxi host Hugh Keeton, who asked her about the cost of living crisis and the high price of beef, to which she replied that people "have so many proteins to choose from":
https://www.mississippifreepress.org/americans-who-cant-afford-beef-have-so-many-proteins-to-choose-from-says-us-sen-cindy-hyde-smith/
Colom's ad plays a montage of Mississippians expressing disgust with Hyde-Smith's response, saying "I'd rather choose another senator instead." Colom was quick to credit content creator Ryan Sayles, who joined the campaign after doing some freelance work for them, as the ad's mastermind:
https://www.politico.com/news/2026/11/12/ryan-sayles-scott-colom-viral-protein-ad-01163508
Much has been said about the ad's style, especially the viral "s-h-e-e-e-i-t" voiced by US Army vet Russ Hudson, who stepped in to do the ad when his father declined to curse on camera:
https://edition.cnn.com/2026/11/15/politics/scott-colom-viral-protein-ad-mississippi-senate
But the most important analysis on the ad is of its
substance
, specifically how it repudiates one of the key doctrines of neoliberal, austerity economics, as David Dayen writes for
The American Prospect
:
https://prospect.org/2026/11/16/how-the-mississippi-senate-win-exposed-austerity-logic/
Neoliberalism is the signature achievement of the "law and economics" movement that came out of the University of Chicago. The central conceit of law and econ is that economics is a "hard science" (not a social science) and that its domain is a deterministic, quantifiable physics of human behavior. Economists went so far as to create a fake "Nobel Prize" in economics in a bid to elevate their work out of the social sciences:
https://pluralistic.net/2024/09/18/falsifiability/#figleaves-not-rubrics
This "science" claims it can replace squishy, qualitative questions with mathematical models that can output the objectively correct answer to every thorny political question, thereby substituting an obscure form of mathematics for political deliberation:
https://pluralistic.net/2023/04/03/all-models-are-wrong/#some-are-useful
This is a remarkably cheap and transparent stunt. To produce this "mathematics of human behavior," you must first quantize all the qualitative questions so that they can be plugged into variables in econ equations. But here's the problem:
assigning a numeric value to a qualitative factor is a qualitative exercise!
When an economist decides that the pain you'll experience as a result of their policy prescriptions is worth "7" while the benefit is worth "8," they are not doing objective work, they're just declaring their own judgments (and biases) to be empirical observations, while demoting
your
judgments to mere "feelings" (and biases):
https://pluralistic.net/2024/09/18/falsifiability/#figleaves-not-rubrics
Economism doesn't just eliminate qualitative questions from policymaking: it
incinerates
all of those qualitative factors so that economists can perform mathematics on whatever dubious quantitative residue remains in the crucible:
https://locusmag.com/feature/cory-doctorow-qualia/
This is where Scott Colom's brilliant campaign ad comes in. As Dayen points out, when Hyde-Smith made her "many other proteins to choose from," gaffe, she was referencing a key piece of economic theory that has been thoroughly weaponized by the neoliberals: "substitution."
On its face, substitution is just the commonsense proposition that when prices for one good or service go up, some consumers will substitute another. When gas prices go up, more people ride the bus. When Instagram increases its ad-load, more people switch to Mastodon or Bluesky. When your ISP jacks up your price, you might switch to another carrier.
So far, so good. Indeed, one of the best things about digital products and services is how low digital "switching costs" (the cost you bear when switching from one service to another) can be. The lower the switching costs are, the easier it is to substitute one thing for another.
This means that, in a state of nature, tech bosses have to be careful about enshittifying their products, lest their users defect to a rival. This is why tech bosses have spent decades doing everything they can to jack up those "switching costs." They want to convert all their users into hostages:
https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2021/08/facebooks-secret-war-switching-costs
Substitution is real and it makes sense. The problem comes when neoliberal economists use this idea in
policy
. In the early 2010s, Barack Obama proposed applying substitution to the American welfare system through something called "Chained CPI."
"CPI" stands for the "Consumer Price Index": this is a measurement of the cost of a "basket of goods" (a bunch of supposedly representative household items) that is used to make inflationary adjustments to a variety of payments. When the CPI goes up, so do social security, welfare and disability payments. Otherwise, inflation would continually reduce the living standards of everyone who receives these entitlements.
"Chained CPI" takes the CPI and applies substitution to it. Rather than asking whether
hamburger
just got more expensive, the economists who calculate CPI ask whether the cheapest protein at the grocery store got more expensive (because hamburger can be swapped out for another protein).
Chained CPI's intent and effect is to reduce payments to retirees, disabled people, and poor people. Chained CPI is always going to be lower than CPI, because chained CPI only rises when the cheapest "substitute" for one of the items in CPI's "basket of goods" gets more expensive. In effect, the "basket of goods" becomes a
transdimensional
basket, one that contains every substitute good that might have gone into the basket in all possible parallel universes. Economists get to universe-shop for the dimension where that basket has the lowest cost, and then we all have to live in their chosen universe.
Obama tried to adopt it 2013 as part of a "grand bargain" to reduce the deficit, but was thwarted by the Tea Party Caucus, who refused the grand bargain on the grounds that it contained an infinitesimal tax rise for the richest Americans:
https://abcnews.com/blogs/politics/2013/04/obamas-chained-cpi-throws-off-pols-and-heres-how-it-works/
But chained CPI is one of the most cherished ideas of neoliberal thinktanks, so it came back in 2017 for Trump's tax giveaway to the super-rich, whose costs were partially offset by using chained CPI to calculate tax brackets, the standard deduction, and the Earned Income Tax Credit:
https://newrepublic.com/article/145688/biggest-trojan-horse-republican-tax-plan
As Dayen writes, "[This created] a regressive tax increase by eroding deductions and credits and pushing people into higher tax brackets. The wealthy were unaffected by this since they were already in the highest tax bracket and itemized rather than using the standard deduction."
Which brings me back to Cindy Hyde-Smith's foot-in-mouth remarks about "so many proteins." Whether or not Hyde-Smith knew it, she was referencing this substitution/chained CPI doctrine when she told people that beef inflation didn't matter because other proteins were available. In doing so, she was performing economism's cheapest trick: pretending that
her
opinions are objective facts, while other people's opinions are just
feelings
.
As Dayen writes, "[W]ho decides whether the cheaper product they switch to is 'similar' to the one that’s become so expensive that the consumer must give it up?…The logic is, approximately: You're not doing as badly as you think, so pick another protein and quit your whining."
People
do
substitute one thing for another as a matter of economic necessity. But the idea that a "substitute" is an "equivalent" can only be accepted if you first buy into the economist's conceit that all qualitative matters can be safely incinerated, leaving the expert to do math on the quantitative ash.
In this conceit, if you can't afford hamburgers and end up substituting chicken or fish, then you've found a new protein and have lost no "welfare." If you think otherwise, then you are expressing an irrational preference (unlike economists, whose preference for having a Nobel prize of their own is so empirically correct that it could be detected by a Martian peering at Earth through a telescope).
It's not only Cindy Hyde-Smith who unconsciously metabolized the idea of substitution as "rationality"; it's the voters, too, who recognized in Hyde-Smith's remarks the arrogance of policy elites who've spent decades discounting their views as "feelings" while declaring their own views to be experimental validated facts. Not only do voters recognize this move: they
hate
it.
They know that elites are perfectly capable of expressing strong views about qualitative matters and moving heaven and Earth to achieve a life that fits with their aesthetics. They know that the same Ivy League economists who dismiss their preferences as "irrational" will revel for
4,000 pages'
worth of Marcel Proust expounding on the spiritual, intangible reasons for choosing his favorite baked goods.
Economics is the study of why people do things, but somewhere along the way it became a club used to
force
people to do the things that powerful people prefer. The (alleged) "rationalism" of economism has worn out its welcome. You don't need an economics degree to understand why that "rationalism" is a cheap trick: after 50 years of neoliberalism, a Martian with a telescope could spot it.

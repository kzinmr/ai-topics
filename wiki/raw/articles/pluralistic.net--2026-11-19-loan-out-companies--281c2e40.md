# Pluralistic: How to forfeit one of copyright’s best labor policies (19 Nov 2026)

Source: https://pluralistic.net/2026/11/19/loan-out-companies/

How to forfeit one of copyright's best labor policies (
permalink
)
There's a seeming paradox to copyright. For 50 years (starting with the 1976 Copyright Act) legislatures have monotonically expanded copyright, so that today, copyright lasts longer, covers more works, restricts more activities, and provides for harsher penalties for infringement, all in the name of protecting artists.
After 50 years of this, artists are, on average,
poorer
than we were in 1976, but the media companies we sell our work to are
much, much
richer. They're larger and more profitable, and yet our wealth (both in absolute terms and as a share of media company profits) has declined.
This may feel like a paradox, but it's actually easy to understand unless you've had the self-inflicted neurological injury we call "getting an econ degree." That's an injury that leaves the sufferer incapable of reasoning about or even perceiving
power
.
If you've avoided that fate, then you can see that when an artist sells to one of five publishers, four studios, three labels, two app companies or one ebook/audiobook company, that artist has very little leverage. In that market, giving an artist more to bargain
with
is just giving them more to bargain
away
.
I have often compared giving more copyright to creative workers trapped in this oligopoly to giving a bullied schoolchild extra lunch money. No matter how much money you give that kid, they're not going to get fed. The bullies just take whatever money you give to the kid. Indeed, if you indirectly transfer enough money to the bullies, they'll pay for a global ad campaign: THINK OF THE HUNGRY SCHOOLCHILDREN! THEY NEED MORE LUNCH MONEY!
This is exactly what has happened with 50 years of successive copyright expansions. Every time Congress gave a new copyright to creative workers, the cartels we bargain with just amended their non-negotiable contracts to force us to hand over that new right for no extra payment. Then they used those new rights to get richer and more powerful, and to exert more control over the distribution channels we need to reach our audiences, which made us poorer. Then Congress heard their THINK OF THE STARVING ARTISTS pitch, and gave us more copyright…which we promptly handed over to our bosses. Lather, rinse, repeat.
If you want to feed a hungry schoolkid, you have to change the
structure
of the system. For example, you might open a credit account with the school lunchroom, so that the kid never has any cash for the bullies to take. Likewise, if you want to help artists get more money, you need to make a systemic change.
There are lots of ways to do this. In 2022, Rebecca Giblin and I co-wrote a book called
Chokepoint Capitalism
, and we devoted
half
of the book to detailed, shovel-ready proposals for ways to alter the copyright system to benefit artists, not our bosses:
https://www.beacon.org/Chokepoint-Capitalism-P2020.aspx
That section includes a chapter on something called "termination of transfer," which was introduced with the 1976 Copyright Act. Under termination of transfer, any copyright contract (a "transfer") can be unilaterally canceled ("termination") by any creator after 35 years. So even if you sign away your copyrights for the entire term (your life plus 70 years), you can tear up that agreement after 35 years by notifying the company you sold your rights to and then filing some paperwork with the US Copyright Office:
https://pluralistic.net/2021/09/26/take-it-back/
We got
really
excited about termination. For one thing, Rebecca is one of the world's leading experts on the subject. In 2021, she co-authored the landmark study "U.S. Copyright Termination Notices 1977-2020: Introducing New Datasets," published in the
Journal of Empirical Legal Studies
:
https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3880708
Termination is a uniquely efficient way to make artists richer at the expense of our bosses. The fact that you can only exercise it after 35 years produces some very interesting effects. First, consider that almost no copyrighted works are commercially viable after 35 years (most copyrighted works cease to generate income after 14 years or less, this being the original term of copyright in the USA). The rare works that continue to generate revenue 35 years after their release are the "perennial favorites" that drive a vast share of media industry profits.
Now consider that, between the realities of the human lifespan and the amount of time it takes most artists to master their craft, any given work you produced 35 years ago is almost certainly from the start of your career. That's the moment in which artists are typically
most
vulnerable. Artists at the start of their careers are unknown quantities and are often crammed into signing
terrible
deals, even worse than the "standard" media deal.
Put these two facts together and you'll see that termination is disproportionately applicable to a body of work that is wildly profitable for media companies,
but
the creators of those works are entitled to
even less
of the money it generates, thanks to the crummy contracts they were railroaded into because they were just starting out when they made them.
Termination lets creators cancel those crummy contracts, take back their rights, and bargain for a better deal with the old media company (or a new one). This time, they bargain from a position of unassailable strength, due to the fact that they're selling a property that's proven itself to be a perennial profit-generator.
Best of all, you can't bargain out of termination. Even if you sign a contract that says, "I promise I will not exercise my termination of transfer right," you can
still
exercise your termination right. This is a structural change, like the parent who opens a charge account with the school cafeteria so that their kid no longer has any lunch money to hand over to the bullies. The way Congress wrote termination into the 1976 Act, it doesn't matter how hard the media company you initially sign with twists your arm, you literally
can't
promise them you won't use your termination right 35 years after signing.
That's where that landmark study Rebecca co-authored comes in. Rebecca and her co-authors found that termination was working exactly the way Congress intended: it was making artists richer. It's particularly helpful to marginalized creative workers, for example, Black "heritage" musicians who were railroaded into terrible contracts that meant they never got a dime for decades of airplay, covers and samples of their hits. If this sounds familiar, you might be thinking of my 2024 technothriller
The Bezzle
, where I use this as a key plot point:
https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250865878/thebezzle/
Genre writers have really benefited from termination. The authors of massively popular YA series like
The Baby-Sitters Club
and
Sweet Valley High
claimed back their rights under termination. So did Peter Straub and Stephen King, who took back the rights to their early books and resold them on
much
more favorable terms. Just the
threat
of termination forced Disney to make giant payouts to Marvel's key creators, like Stan Lee, Steve Ditko and Jack Kirby:
https://deadline.com/2023/12/marvel-court-termination-disney-spider-man-dr-strange-1234843941/
In
Chokepoint Capitalism
, Rebecca and I point out that most of the 1976 Copyright Act (like longer copyright terms) has failed to help artists, but termination is the rare exception, a proven effective way to put money in artists' pockets. Which is weird, because in the 50 years since the 1976 Act, Congress has repeatedly doubled down on pretty much every measure in the Act,
except
the one thing that is empirically proven to be good for artists. Of course, all that failed stuff in the 1976 Act is good for giant media companies, and termination is bad for those companies, so it's not hard to see why there's been so little legislative appetite to beef up termination.
But it's also easy to see how we'd do that beefing up. In the original draft of the Copyright Act, termination was automatic, and kicked in after 20 years (meaning that 20 years after signing any contract, every creator would automatically get back all the rights they sold in the contract). The final version of the Act watered down termination so it kicks in after 35 years, and even then only after the artist does a ton of paperwork.
Giving artists the termination rights they were promised in the initial draft of the 1976 Act is a no-brainer. But that's not what we've gotten from Congress. To the extent that Congress acted on termination, it was to
take it away
from musicians. In 1999, an obscure Congressional staffer named Mitch Glazier snuck a clause into an unrelated bill that made it impossible for most recording artists to exercise their termination rights. This was such a scandal that Congress had to convene a special session to cancel this amendment and restore musicians' rights, and Glazier was drummed out of his Congressional job:
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitch_Glazier#Work_for_hire
Don't worry, Glazier landed on his feet: he was immediately hired by the RIAA (the record industry's lobbying arm) as their new CEO and there he perches even today, making more than $1.4m/year to do a job he continues to describe as "defending artists":
https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/organizations/131669037
With Glazier as the standard-bearer for "artists' rights," it's no wonder that creators haven't gotten a better deal on termination out of Congress.
Indeed, things are looking very bad for termination of transfer right now, thanks to a string of court decisions that pit a common tax strategy employed by freelance creatives against termination of transfer.
Like many freelancers, creative workers often incorporate limited liability companies that contract with the companies that employ them. These companies then pass the revenue on to creators as "partner distributions." There are lots of reasons to like this arrangement: it simplifies probate and estate planning, helps creators fund their pensions, and offers tax deductions for expenses like a home office or research trips.
This arrangement is especially common in Hollywood, where the companies that writers, directors, and other creative trades work through are called "loan-out companies" (because the company employs the creative, and "loans them out" to movie studios).
Movie studios and other media companies have recently seized on the existence of these loan-out companies to argue (with a great deal of success) that the existence of these loan-out companies nullifies termination of transfer.
You see, termination of transfer is only available for creative works that are independently produced and licensed through a copyright contract. Termination does
not
apply to "works made for hire," such as the emails and reports you might write in the course of your duties as an employee. The logic here is that termination of transfer is supposed to help artists get more out of their copyrights, but forcing (say) an insurance company to disgorge all the memos you wrote for them 35 years ago would introduce chaos and uncertainty without helping workers in any way (there's not exactly a market for 35-year-old insurance company memos).
The media companies' argument goes that creative workers are employees of their loan-out companies, so if you sell a script or a novel through your LLC, that is a "work made for hire" that the LLC owned, and that means that it can't exercise your termination of transfer rights to get that work back after 35 years.
Aaron Moss, who edits the Copyright Lately website, has been beating the drum about this for years, calling it "a ticking time bomb for copyright-intensive industries." He's been repeatedly vindicated, most recently through a bizarre dispute between Michael Mann, the director and writer of 1995's blockbuster
Heat
, and New Regency, the studio that released it:
https://copyrightlately.com/michael-mann-heat-copyright-termination/
Moss's article on the dispute is a very entertaining read, unpacking a decidedly weird gambit that Mann has deployed to regain the rights to
Heat
. But the most important part of the article is the way it describes the state of play for termination's ongoing collision with loan-out companies. The studios and labels have racked up a string of wins using this gambit, with creators settling their cases or losing outright. Take musician John Waite's bid to get his rights back from UMG: the judge ruled in UMG's favor, saying, "people cannot use a corporate structure for some purposes – eg taking advantage of tax benefits – and then disavow it for others":
https://copyrightlately.com/copyright-termination-loan-out-corporations/
This is a bad situation. Termination is a fantastic, artist-friendly legal doctrine, one that lets creators claim back some of our most cherished media. Termination gave George Clinton the rights back to his entire catalog (his sleazy ex-manager forged Clinton's signature on a rights assignment). It let Gary K Wolf take Roger Rabbit back from Disney:
https://pluralistic.net/2025/11/18/im-not-bad/#im-just-drawn-that-way
It let the
Spinal Tap
crew get a
much
better deal out of their studio, and paved the way for 2025's (criminally underrated)
Spinal Tap II
:
https://www.nme.com/news/film/this-is-spinal-tap-creators-and-studiocanal-settle-rights-dispute-2757087
The Wachowskis just announced that they're going to exercise their termination rights to get
The Matrix
back from Warner Brothers, which is now owned by Trump's cronies, thanks to California Attorney General Rob Bonta's decision to let the Warner-Paramount merger go through:
https://puck.news/hollywood/2026/11/the-wachowskis-are-reclaiming-the-matrix
This clash between termination and loan-outs has been on my radar since 2021, when the artist-defending media lawyer Jeff Trexler told me about it over drinks at Comic-Con. I've since spoken to accountants and lawyers who think there might be a way to finesse this if creators set up contracts with their loan-outs that give the loan-outs the right to collect their royalties and advances, without assigning copyright to the loan-out companies. I don't have any definitive answers on this: if you're worried about it, you should talk to your own lawyers and accountants.
It's a dangerous erosion of one of the few bright spots in copyright, a rule that is an unambiguously good way to benefit artists, not media companies.
In the meantime, if you've got some 35-year-old copyrights you'd like to terminate, I've got a tip for you. The Authors Alliance is a nonprofit that defends the rights of authors (I volunteer for its advisory board); we've got a free online tool that will help you terminate your own transfers and get your rights back:
https://www.authorsalliance.org/resources/termination-of-transfer/

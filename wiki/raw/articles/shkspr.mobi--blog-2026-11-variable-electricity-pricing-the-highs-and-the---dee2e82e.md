# Variable Electricity Pricing - the Highs and the Lows

Source: https://shkspr.mobi/blog/2026/11/variable-electricity-pricing-the-highs-and-the-lows/

For one glorious day in September, we had several hours where we were
paid
to use electricity.
Our home is on Octopus Energy's variable price tariff. Our smart-meter sends back readings every half-hour. That means the energy company can set a different price every half hour. Rather than paying a flat rate of 30p per kWh, we pay a rate which constantly changes throughout the day.

To be clear, we have
chosen
this tariff. It is not mandatory. You are free to choose a fixed price tariff if you want. It fits our lifestyle (just about) but we could choose to change back any time we want.
When the sun is shining and the wind a-howling, the UK produces more electricity than it can consume. Our country frequently sells electricity to its neighbours - but it can also sell it to its internal customers. So, for a few hours a day, our meter runs backwards and our electricity bills go down.
That's the happy path. What happens when the skies are cloudy and the wind is still? Prices go up. The other day, we saw this alarming spike:
Yikes! Variable pricing can be unexpectedly high. In Texas a few years ago, a storm caused wholesale prices to rise so much that it caused immediate and long-term financial damage to some people:
The average price for electricity in Texas in the winter is about 12 cents per kilowatt-hour […] Texas utility regulators allowed that price to rise to $9 per kilowatt-hour.
After Days Of Mass Outages, Some Texas Residents Now Face Huge Electricity Bills
The bill-shock was immense:
For a home that uses 2,000 kWh per month, prices at $9/kWh work out to over $640 per day in energy charges. By comparison, that same household would typically pay $2 per day.

Luckily, Octopus caps the maximum we can pay at £1/kWh. That's still 3x the typical cost, but nowhere near the bankruptcy levels seen in the USA.
There are basically two strategies available to minimise our bills.
Electricity Shifting
Demand Shifting
We have a
solar array
and a
big battery
. During the day our battery soaks up the sunshine. Because it has an Internet connection, it knows the half-hourly prices - so it discharges at the most expensive times.
That's good enough for watching TV, boiling the kettle, and the like. But the battery doesn't contain enough charge or have enough oomph to run everything like the oven, tumble dryer, and aluminium smelter at once. Which leads us on to demand shifting.
If you saw that evening prices were a quid a kilowatt hour, what behaviours would you change? I put the tumble dryer on early. I made a dinner which didn't require the oven. I read my pre-charged eReader rather than playing on a console.
Did I
have
to do that? No. I am insulated by the battery - but that won't happen all the time. There will be days when the sun hasn't shone enough to fill up the battery for later. There will be days when I
need
a hot dinner. There will be days when I want to watch a movie on TV.
Those days will be expensive.
I dream of a day when every home has solar panels and a battery. A distributed grid is a resilient grid.
Until then, I don't think a variable tariff is useful for
most
people. I suspect that the average person doesn't want to delay dinner by an hour just to save £3.14, or to needlessly hoover the house because they'll earn 71p for it.
This is a fun experiment for us. The occasional surge price isn't going to bankrupt us, and we don't have complex needs which mean restricting our energy usage would be catastrophic. But it is easy to see how the lack of predictability could seriously inconvenience someone or cause them needless distress.
I broadly think that people should be more aware of their energy usage. A little bit of behavioural change among many people can lead to a significant positive impact. But I'm also grateful for the £1 cap!

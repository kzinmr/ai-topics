What Happens to Emails and Side Letters Under an Entire Agreement Clause or Merger Clause?
Emails, side letters, and sales promises rarely reach the signed contract. Here's how to find them and test each against the entire agreement or merger clause.
by
Harvey Team
•
Oct 19, 2026
Consider a sales email that promises 24-hour support, in a deal whose Order Form never mentions it. Whether that email still counts depends largely on a single paragraph of boilerplate.
That paragraph is the entire agreement clause. It states that the signed contract is the parties' complete agreement on its subject matter, and it supersedes the earlier agreements, proposals, representations, and communications it names. US lawyers usually call the same provision a merger clause or an integration clause.
This article describes how to find promises like that one in the deal correspondence and test each against the clause, both before signature and after a dispute. Harvey locates the communications and flags the conflicts, and lawyers decide what they mean under the governing law. Drafting the clause itself is a separate subject, and
Harvey's guide to drafting clauses in legal documents
is a good place to begin.
Delivery Dates, Support Levels, and Pricing Terms get Settled in Email
Commercial deals rarely live in one document, which is why the commitments are spread across several places by the time the contract is signed:
Email threads
hold delivery dates the account team confirmed and support levels the technical team promised during diligence.
Term sheets and letters of intent
settle pricing conditions that often never reach the long-form draft.
Side letters
address one customer's requirement while the main agreement stays on the provider's standard form.
Requests for proposals (RFPs) and sales decks
describe functionality, timing, and service levels that the agreement does not convert into warranties or obligations.
Reliance on a message does not make it part of the contract. The signed documents either preserve it, supersede it, incorporate it, or leave its effect to the governing law. Finding the 24-hour support email before signature gives the team a chance to decide whether the commitment belongs in the signed contract.

Three Parts of the Clause Set What to Check for Each Promise
With the emails loaded, the clause itself determines what to test. The following provision is illustrative:
"This Agreement, including all Exhibits and Order Forms, constitutes the entire agreement between the parties regarding its subject matter [1] and supersedes all prior and contemporaneous agreements, proposals, representations, and communications, whether written or oral [2]. In the event of a conflict, the following order of precedence applies: the Order Form, this Agreement, then any Exhibit [3]."
[1] Subject matter sets the reach.
A clause generally covers the scope it describes, so a promise about a different subject needs its own analysis.
[2] The sweep names what the signed agreement replaces.
Emails, decks, proposals, and term sheets can fall within language this broad.
[3] Precedence decides which document controls
when the Order Form, the agreement, and an exhibit disagree.
US readers will recognize the parol evidence rule here. Where the written contract is integrated, the rule can limit the effect of prior or contemporaneous agreements. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 213 covers how an integrated agreement affects prior agreements. Section 214 lists the evidence of prior negotiations that remains admissible, including to show fraud.
Apply the three parts to the support promise. It concerns the same subject matter as the Order Form, it falls within the sweep, and nothing in the Order Form names it. That leaves the team a decision to make before signing.
A carve-out can preserve an existing nondisclosure agreement (NDA), side letter, or order form. A promise from the correspondence can also survive if someone writes it into the contract or incorporates the document that holds it by reference. Which route works depends on the agreement and the governing law.
The clause is also only one input to a misrepresentation claim. Courts in some jurisdictions, including England, have held that an entire agreement clause does not exclude liability for misrepresentation unless it says so clearly. US treatment varies by jurisdiction and claim.
After signing, the same checks help answer a different question: what the clause reached.

Questions Lawyers ask About Entire Agreement and Merger Clauses
1. What is an entire agreement clause?
An entire agreement clause states that the signed contract is the parties' complete agreement on its subject matter and supersedes the earlier negotiations, drafts, agreements, and representations it names.
2. Is an entire agreement clause the same as a merger or integration clause?
Generally, yes. "Merger clause" and "integration clause" are the common US terms for a provision that makes the written contract the parties' integrated agreement. The exact wording and effect depend on the governing law.
3. Does an entire agreement clause need to be in every contract?
No rule requires one. They are standard in commercial agreements, and parties sometimes narrow them or leave them out when a side letter or prior agreement is meant to keep operating.
4. Does an entire agreement clause stop a misrepresentation claim?
Not necessarily. English courts have held that a clause that does not clearly exclude liability for misrepresentation generally will not. See AXA Sun Life Services plc v Campbell Martin Ltd [2011] EWCA Civ 133. In the US, Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 214, for example, recognizes circumstances in which evidence of prior negotiations remains admissible, including to establish fraud. The result turns on the clause's wording, the governing law, and whether the contract has separate non-reliance language.

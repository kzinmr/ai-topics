# Paul Brainerd, creator of desktop publishing

Source: https://dfarq.homeip.net/paul-brainerd-creator-of-desktop-publishing/?utm_source=rss&utm_medium=rss&utm_campaign=paul-brainerd-creator-of-desktop-publishing

Paul Brainerd was a journalist turned software developer. He was also the right person with the right idea at the right time to change the way human beings put words on paper. He was born November 17, 1947 and died February 15, 2026.
Atex: Word processing for journalists
Paul Brainerd invented the concept of desktop publishing with Pagemaker.
Paul Brainerd probably did more to computerize newsrooms than any other person in the 1980s. He took a job with a company called Atex, a company that created a text processing system that let reporters write on a computer screen, then send the story over to their editor. It ran on
MS-DOS
and worked over inexpensive networks. I was still using this system in journalism school when I graduated in 1997. The user interface was dated but the software ran fine on
Windows 95
,
Windows NT
, or
OS/2
. And the workflow still worked, so the journalism school wouldn’t give it up. Neither did newspapers in the field, from what I was told when I asked why they continued to use and teach such an antiquated system.
But Kodak bought Atex in early 1984, and laid off Brainerd and four other engineers. It was time for a new idea, and Brainerd had something even more revolutionary. I used that piece of software too, and had no idea at the time that one man connected them.
Aldus Pagemaker
He didn’t have a name for his idea yet, but Brainerd and his engineers hit the road, visiting small newspapers and asking what they thought of the idea of laying out pages on a computer, instead of laying them out on a table using rubber cement and rubber rollers to fix the elements in place. The reaction wasn’t encouraging, but they believed in their idea. They named their new company Aldus, after a 15th-century printer from Venice who invented the small book format, standardized typefaces, and made publishing affordable for ordinary people.
They named their software Pagemaker, because it let you lay pages out on a computer. And Brainerd named the concept “desktop publishing.” Because with Pagemaker, a computer, and a laser printer, you could publish a professional-looking document in the space of a standard office-sized desk.
Pagemaker shipped in July 1985 and didn’t just revolutionize journalism. It revolutionized the entire graphic arts industry.
It also provided the Apple Macintosh computer and the Apple Laserwriter printer with their much-needed killer app. Aldus ported Pagemaker to
Microsoft Windows
in 1987, becoming one of the first big-name software titles to appear on that platform, even before Microsoft Word.
In 1994,
Adobe merged with Aldus
, and Brainerd decided he was ready to retire.
Pagemaker was later eclipsed by other titles, first by QuarkXPress and later by Adobe’s own Indesign. But the easiest way to learn either of those two tools was to learn Pagemaker first, get a grasp on all of the concepts of desktop publishing, then graduate to a more powerful tool when Pagemaker grew too limiting.
Retirement
Paul Brainerd was Aldus’ largest shareholder, so the merger with Adobe put his net worth north of $100 million. After leaving the software industry, he became a philanthropist, specializing in environmental conservation in the Pacific Northwest, finding ways to give his fortune away. He was diagnosed with Parkinson’s Disease in his late 50s, fighting it to age 79. He died in the Puget Sound region of Washington on February 15, 2026, survived by his wife and one sister.

# Stop Hosting Boring Tech Events

Source: https://dx.tips/hosting — shared by Sarah Chieng on X 2026-11-20
(https://x.com/MilksandMatcha/status/2123902565092143294)

Howdy! My name is Sarah and I've always loved hosting events. More recently, I've
begun hosting events as part of my role as a **growth engineer at Cerebras**. I'm
nearing my 1 year mark, and thought I'd share some of my favorite parts of hosting a
good event.

One of the first events I hosted was **LlaMAPALOOZA**, hosted at the NASDAQ in NYC.
We had 1025+ RSVPs for a 400 person event. (LlaMAPALOOZA is a developer event series
around using Meta's Llama models in production.)

## What is a "good event"?

Most obvious marker: are people showing up and excited to attend. Secondary markers:

- Is the event memorable?
- Did it have a high ROI for me/my company? (measured by my time and $ spent)

Third-tier markers:

- How was the gender ratio?
- If someone else had hosted this event, would I have attended?
- Are people coming back to my future events?

The competition for a developer or founder's time is fierce — see Michelle Fang's
weekly list of tech events in SF.

## Rule #1: Make people want to come

She currently hosts two main series: **Cafe Compute** and **Barry's w/ Builders**.

The best hack is **piggybacking on what people already have to do**. A VC-sponsored
fine dining experience is a 3 hour commitment — 2.5 hours more than most founders want
to spend on dinner.

- **Cafe Compute** works because it was highly requested on Twitter AND solves a real
  problem: late-night coworking with free coffee, snacks, and other developers. Gives
  people an organic environment to meet cool people while getting work done.
- **Barry's w/ Builders** taps into founders wanting to bundle fitness into their
  lives: ~10 people do a Barry's bootcamp class, then smoothies at the fuel bar.
  "Nothing bonds strangers like protein smoothies after getting yelled at by a Barry's
  instructor for an hour."

## Rule #2: Start small

Validate with an MVP event before investing in something logistically complex (e.g. a
city-wide pickleball tournament). The first Cafe Compute was in her apartment: 75
developers on Amazon-ordered floor cushions, with GTM friends sponsoring boba,
In-N-Out, and donuts.

## Rule #3: Host a Series

Rarely host one-off events. A series gives three advantages:

1. **Time saving** — after the first one or two events, future events need minimal
   playbook planning ("I probably spend 15 minutes organizing each Cafe Compute now").
2. **Upfront investment in branding/swag** — logos, custom coffee cups, menus reused
   across events.
3. **Brand building** — easier to grow a series than to continually promote one-offs.

A series ≠ the same event repeatedly; it is the same format with a few consistent
features (Cafe Compute always has a coffee barista + the same branding, run in
different cities: SF, NYC, Vancouver).

## Rule #4: The little things matter, and people WILL notice

Not everything has to be spectacular, but add details people will remember:

- A "chip jar" (computer chips, a play on tip jar) next to the barista's table.
- For dinners: print name cards and assign seats by who will have the best
  conversations.
- Slater Stich (partner @ BCV) prepares silly-but-relevant conversation topics for
  researcher dinners (during the Grok waifu Twitter drama: argue why AI waifus would
  improve the world). "Preparation disguised as fun."

## Rule #5: Hacks to maximize ROI (for work events)

- **Follow-up email in a timely manner** with your CTA (get an API key, check out this
  fellowship, talk to me if you're raising). Also lets you avoid shilling during the
  event. Target: 20–25% engage.
- **Remember you're the host.** Move people between seats at dinners to meet more
  people; never be scared to get up mid-dinner and greet someone you invited. "As
  host, you set the rules!"
- **Pictures or it didn't happen.** The best $ spent is the $200 professional
  photographer: 100 attendees, 50,000 people will see the Twitter post. Make it a
  banger post.

## Rule #6: Bring in partners

Rarely hosts without VCs or other companies as cohosts. **Diminishing returns beyond
1–2 event partners** — beyond that it's wasted coordination/accountability time.

- What you get: access to a new network, split costs, more organizing manpower.
- What partners get: VCs get deal flow + new talent (usually put in $$); startups get
  access to more customers (put in $$, promote on socials, or drive a certain number
  of RSVPs).

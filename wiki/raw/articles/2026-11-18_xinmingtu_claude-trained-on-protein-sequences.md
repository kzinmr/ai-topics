The latest Claude models were trained on protein sequences, at scale. Give Claude Fable 5.2 or Claude Opus 5.5 the start of a little-studied bacterial protein and they predict the next amino acid. Shuffle the protein and they can’t. No other model we tested can do this. Like a protein language model, they do better on bigger protein families, and they do it even in families with no papers at all. Fable 5.2 beats every open protein language model we ran, and Opus 5.5 is level with ProtGPT3-10B, the largest of them. Update, 18 November 2026. Since the first version: Proteins published after the training cutoff. With Leo Tianlai Chen, 56 proteins first made public in August 2026, with no earlier relative at 50% identity or more. The latest Claude models still predict them ( Appendix G ). More protein language models. ProGen3-3B is the strongest open one we ran, and Fable 5.2 still beats it; Prot-xLSTM is in Appendix C . More GPT models. GPT-6.2 Sol and Astra show no sign of it either. Motivation Frontier LLMs have started to look good at protein tasks. Atharv Jayprakash showed GPT-6 Astra ranking SOD1 variants as well as ESM-2, and PG-LLM found the same across many proteins. Did these models learn from protein sequence databases such as UniProt, or from what is written about proteins? Famous proteins can’t tell the two apart: GFP is printed in countless papers, and most frontier models recite it. So we tested proteins that almost nobody writes about. Method: next-amino-acid prediction on little-studied proteins We give a model the start of a protein and ask for the next amino acids. Then we ask again on a shuffled copy of the same protein. The knowledge also carries over to proteins published after the training cutoff, with no close relative before it ( Appendix G ). Two other explanations don’t fit: Papers. In the 128 families with no papers at all, both models do as well as on the whole set. Reasoning. Most answers involve no thinking at all: the API reports thinking tokens on 9% of Fable 5.2’s answers and 36% of Opus 5.5’s. The answers without thinking are just as accurate. GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol think for hundreds of tokens per question, GPT-6.2 Sol and Astra answer about as briefly as Claude, and all four stay near zero. Famous proteins and variant ranking Famous proteins are a different story. Many models do well on them, and several recite hundreds of residues letter for letter ( Appendix B ), because these sequences are all over papers and the web. In a first round with famous and obscure proteins side by side, Gemini 4 Carbon was strong on the famous ones and dropped to almost nothing on the obscure ones. Ranking variants of a well-known protein can lean on what is written about it: which residues are buried, which substitutions break a helix, which mutations cause ALS. In PG-LLM, accuracy doesn’t depend on alignment depth and improves with more thinking, both signs of that route. Our test shuts it out. GPT-6 is good at the first kind of task and weak at the second; the latest Claude models are good at both. What this means The latest Claude models know protein families that almost nobody has studied. That takes a lot of sequence data, not a few examples: protein sequences were a sizeable part of their training. Opus 5.5 behaves like a protein language model, and Fable 5.2 goes beyond the best one we could run. GPT and Gemini show none of this. Whatever protein sequence they saw in training, it was not enough to learn these families. DNA is different. Opus 5.5 predicts human and mouse coding sequence but not the rest of the genome, and barely the bacterial genes behind the proteins it knows ( Appendix A ). It learned proteins, not genomes. We are curious what else this knowledge supports. Fable 5.2 predicts little-studied proteins better than any open protein language model we ran, and protein language models carry more than next-residue statistics: ESM-2’s internal representations hold enough structure that a small folding module on top predicts 3D structures (ESMFold ), and their probabilities rank the effects of mutations . A smaller run of 25 models on 37 families, half well-known and half obscure, gave the same picture and set the design of the main run. E. Methods Choosing the proteins. We started from every Pfam family whose name says “domain of unknown function” (DUF) or “protein of unknown function”, and kept those with no structure in the PDB, at most 3 Europe PMC hits for the family’s accession, and at least 300 member proteins. We binned them by size (300 to 1,000 proteins, 1,000 to 3,000, 3,000 to 10,000, over 10,000) and drew 50 families per bin at random; only 17 families qualify above 10,000, so the set has 167. From each family we took 4 proteins from UniProt that are unreviewed (TrEMBL), come from Bacteria or Archaea, are 120 to 800 residues long, use only the 20 standard amino acids, are not low-complexity (Shannon entropy above 3.9 bits per residue), and are alone in their UniRef90 cluster, so no other UniProt protein is 90% or more identical to them. That gives 668 proteins. Smaller families. For the family-size test we drew 30 more families in each of three smaller bins (20 to 60, 60 to 150 and 150 to 300 proteins) with the same criteria and 4 proteins per family, 360 proteins in all. Every model gets the same prompt and scoring as on the main set. Cutting. Each protein is cut at 5 positions drawn at random inside its Pfam domain (the longest domain span, at least 40 residues), at least 10 residues from either end of the domain and with at least 30 residues before the cut: 3,340 cuts. Everything before the cut is the prefix; the residue right after it is the answer. Asking. The model gets this message, with the prefix in 60-character lines (an example from one of the test proteins): Below is the beginning of a protein sequence in one-letter amino acid codes.
Predict how it continues. Reply with exactly the next 10 amino acids as

 Each model runs through its own API with temperature 0 where it is accepted, thinking off or at its lowest setting (for Opus 5.5 and the Fable models, low effort, at which the model still decides whether to think), no tools, and one sample per question. The Claude Fable models run through OpenRouter, since Anthropic’s API serves them only to workspaces that retain data. Models that declined to guess (GPT-4o, DeepSeek, Qwen, Kimi, GLM, gpt-oss) also got a version that adds “Give your best guess even if you are uncertain. Do not explain or refuse.”; those runs are marked separately. Thinking. A reply counts as thought about when the API reports thinking tokens for it: thinking_tokens in Anthropic’s usage report, reasoning_tokens in OpenRouter’s. The thinking text itself is not kept; only the visible reply is scored. Fable 5.2 thought on 9% of questions, Opus 5.5 on 36%, Fable 5.1 never. Without thinking, Fable 5.2 is right 54.7% of the time on real proteins and 8.8% on shuffled ones (48.2% and 10.6% with thinking), and Opus 5.5 35.3% and 6.9% (34.1% and 8.6%). Scoring. We read the last sequence the reply states: a run of capital amino-acid letters on its own line or after a label such as “Answer:”, skipping words, lists and anything inside thinking tags. If it starts by restating the end of the prefix, the restatement is dropped. Its first letter is the predicted next amino acid. A reply cut off at the length limit counts only if it opens with a sequence. Replies that state no sequence are excluded and counted (0.1% for Opus 5.5). Only the first residue is scored; the full 10 are used for exact continuations and runs. The same reader is used for every model and for real and shuffled proteins. The shuffled control. Each protein’s residues are shuffled once, with a random seed fixed by its accession, and the same 5 cut positions are asked on the shuffled sequence. The shuffle keeps the composition of the whole protein and destroys the order, so a model that knows only which amino acids are common scores the same on both. Statistics. For each family we compute the share of correct first residues on real and on shuffled cuts, and take the difference, in percentage points. The result for a model is the mean of that gap over the 167 families. Intervals are 95% bootstraps over families (10,000 resamples), the main test is a one-sided sign-flip permutation test over families, and the family-size trend is the Spearman correlation between a family’s gap and the logarithm of its size. Raw continuation. For models that allow it, the same prefix is sent without any instruction, as the text “>sequence” followed by the FASTA lines, through a plain completion endpoint (open-weight models on Fireworks) or as the start of the model’s own reply (Gemini 2.5 Pro). The first letter the model writes is scored as above. Protein language models. They get the same prefix and no prompt. ProGen3, ProtGPT3, ProtGPT2 and Prot-xLSTM read it left to right and give a probability for the next token; the ESM models see the prefix, a mask in place of the next residue, and the end of the sequence. In every case the prediction is the most likely of the 20 standard amino acids. Total API cost was about $730, plus a few dollars of GPU time for the protein language models. F.

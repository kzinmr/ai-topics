Smoking is very stupid. I was a smoker for 17 years, from ages 13-30. I was very stupid to start smoking. That stupidity only came into sharper focus a few years ago, when I got a cancer diagnosis (don't worry, I am now cancer-free):
https://pluralistic.net/2024/11/05/carcinoma-angels/#squeaky-nail
In my defense, I'll point out that I was just a kid when I started, but also, smoking belongs to a class of pernicious problems whose insidiousness is rooted in the distance between their causes and effects:
https://pluralistic.net/2021/06/03/i-quit/
I've often said that if a little tumor had sprouted immediately after I took my first puff, I never would have taken a second one. Now that I've actually had the experience of being told that I have a tumor, I can say
definitively
that I would not have taken that second puff. An economist would tell you that I was engaged in "hyperbolic discounting": underpricing a future cost and overvaluing a present pleasure:
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_discounting
But I don't think that tells the whole story. Sure, I was engaged in hyperbolic discounting, but
why
was I doing that? What was it about smoking (as opposed to, say, French-kissing light-sockets) that led me to apply a hyperbolic discount to the costs of smoking? I say it was the attenuated relationship between cause and effect. It's not just that we undervalue the price of smoking: it's that we struggle to arrive at a correct price for smoking in the moment, because the intellectual knowledge of cancer statistics is in no way comparable to the visceral experience of a cancer diagnosis. Estimating the "cost" (emotional, physiological, monetary) of a cancer diagnosis is
hard
, especially if you're young and have had the good fortune of a life untouched by close contact with agonizing illness and death.
The point I'm making is that the discount isn't just hyperbolic, it's hyperbolic
because
it relies so much upon guesswork, without comparable experiences to anchor your estimate.
What's more, it's this same
attenuated causality
(in which cause is separated from effect by so much time and space) that allowed the tobacco industry to sow so much profitable doubt about the connection between smoking and cancer. Sure,
some
of the policymakers who let Big Tobacco off the hook were doubtlessly in their pocket, reliant on campaign contributions or beholden to a party that was in some queasy, cozy relationship with these death merchants. But the reason Big Tobacco was able (
is
able) to get away with killing us for so many decades is that you don't get a tumor with each puff.
I believe that
most
of the policymakers who reasoned their way into doubting the connection between smoking and cancer were ensnared by this attenuated causality. They might have had low morals and low intellect, but the factor that made them such easy marks for Big Tobacco's death-hustlers was this wide, long gap between cause and effect.
This cuts both ways: if you're a policymaker of low intellect and morals, you can rig your policies to put their consequences a long way off in the future, which might give you time to escape to a lucrative private-sector career with the industrial interests who benefited from your chicanery. Or (if you're a Mitch McConnell-adjacent flesh-eating ghoul who plans to die only after centuries in office, chin red and slick with the heartblood of the innocent), you can put so much time and space between your fuckery and the ruin it brings that the voting public fails to connect your actions with the consequences. After all, no one ever lost money betting against the American public's object permanence.
For example, Trump's Big Bill was designed to make its deepest cuts – to Medicaid, rural hospitals, the increased tax-load on workers to advantage their bosses –
after
the midterm elections. The cynical reasoning isn't hard to follow:
We'll just tell voters that all the bad things they've been told about the coming cuts are Fake News, and then, after the cuts arrive, blame their post-midterm misery on Biden, communists, trans people, immigrants, and the phases of the moon.
This cheap trick may be deplorable, but you should also save some anger for politicians who enact
good
policies but refuse to take account of this principle. Take Biden's 2021 plan to lower prescription drug prices. This started off as a rule that would end the longstanding, outrageous policy that bans Medicare/Medicaid from negotiating with pharma companies. Yes, you read that right: when Uncle Sucker buys drugs from Big Pharma, the government just asks the pharma companies how much public money they'd like, and then the USA writes the companies a check for whatever sum they name, no questions asked. That's why the US pays 300% more than any other advanced nation for its drugs.
Thus Biden's initial proposal: let Medicare/Medicaid negotiate drug prices the same way every private insurer
and
every public health system does. But then, Biden's team allowed that policy to be diluted so that it would only apply to
ten
drugs, and
only
if those drugs had been on the market for at least 13 years. Finally,
the piece de resistance
: the plan wasn't design to kick in until 2025,
after
the 2024 election, which turned out to be a referendum on the cost of living (including drug prices) that saw the Dems getting their asses kicked by the stupidest, most obviously corrupt fascists in American electoral history:
https://pluralistic.net/2021/11/18/bipartisan-consensus/#corruption
Whether you're ignoring attenuated causality or weaponizing it, it's critical to any analysis of how we get into as much trouble as we do. It is the ruin of individuals and firms, and of political and economic systems.
Take vaccinations: herd immunity requires a
lot
of compliance: nearly all of us have to get vaccinated before the real benefits kick in. Securing this compliance takes a lot of effort, because the system has to get people to invest their faith in the prospect that
eventually
we'll get to herd immunity and an end to infection.
But once herd immunity is in place, noncompliance is a much smaller deal. The very fact of herd immunity means that people can opt out here or there without getting sick (because
we're
all up-to-date on
our
vaccinations). Worse, the very fact of herd immunity means that after a while, no one can really remember what the sickness was like: we think of measles as something the Little Rascals pretended to have so they could play hooky, not as a terrifyingly infectious disease that can leave you blind or brain damaged. Asking someone who's never lived through a measles epidemic to estimate the cost of measles is like asking a 13 year old to estimate what a cancer diagnosis will feel like.
The same applies to antitrust enforcement. An economy of small and medium sized firms who compete with one another is an economy where workers have more bargaining leverage and regulators don't have to worry about being outspent and outlasted with decade-plus appeals when they crack down on corrupt businesses:
https://www.nytimes.com/1981/02/15/business/us-vsibm.html
It's also an economy where companies must compete rather than collude. Look at what happened to the ad-tech industry, which is dominated by a duopoly that controls 80% of display advertising, and that duopoly has an illegal, collusive arrangement to rig the market to pay publishers less and charge advertisers more:
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jedi_Blue
In the post-war era, antitrust enforcers did their jobs: they blocked mergers, punished predatory pricing, and aggressively investigated sweetheart deals between retail incumbents and their suppliers that were designed to freeze out new businesses:
https://pluralistic.net/2024/08/14/the-price-is-wright/#enforcement-priorities
This produced an economy where companies had lower profits, because workers and consumers could force them to disgorge more of their surpluses, and because they had to invest more in improving their offerings to keep up with competitors. It was an economy where prosperity was more broadly shared and where it was easier to impose democratic accountability on the private sector.
Then, starting with Jimmy Carter, America turned its back on antitrust. Year after year, presidency after presidency, enforcers waved through larger and more anticompetitive mergers and ignored more egregious predatory practices. For 50 years, the neoclassical economists who promoted this
laissez-faire
doctrine have smugly pointed out that even though the economy grew more consolidated thanks to their policy, we still
had
an economy, one that was often booming, where politicians could (sometimes) be seen to be intervening to address corporate cheating.
The public, meanwhile, has historically struggled to connect the problems of oligarchy with these pro-oligarch policies. They can feel the regulatory capture, they know that labor grows weaker every year, they are furious at their sellout politicians and the system's naked corruption. But because the policy is separated from its outcomes by so much time and space, there's lots of room for the policy's architects and beneficiaries to do the economic version of denying the link between smoking and cancer; that is, denying the link between pro-monopoly policies and monopolies.
Attenuated causality lets bad-faith actors sell us anti-vax, pro-monopoly, cancer denial…and, of course, climate denial. NASA's James Hansen warned Congress about climate change in 1988. It's not like the oil companies didn't know about this. As far back as the 1970s, Exxon's own scientists had warned the company about global warming, and the company took actions based on those warnings:
https://insideclimatenews.org/project/exxon-the-road-not-taken/
Mobil, meanwhile, started secretly raising its drilling platforms to account for rising seas, even as it was taking out full-page ads in national daily newspapers that stated that "The science of climate change is too uncertain to mandate a plan of action":
https://web.archive.org/web/20160101024248/http://graphics.latimes.com/oil-operations/
Attenuated causality was and is at the core of climate denial. If every climate-shredding gas turbine that the self-described environmental hero Elon Musk fired up at one of his AI data-centers immediately caused a flood or a wildfire or a heat-wave, we'd scrape all those data-centers down to their foundation slabs and then we'd display Musk in stocks and pelt him with rotten fruit.
Musk really knows how to turn attenuated causality to his advantage. Remember when he performatively borrowed a security guard's pocket knife and started slicing through the Ethernet cables connecting Twitter's servers?
https://english.elpais.com/science-tech/2023-09-14/when-elon-musk-unplugged-the-twitter-servers-with-a-pocket-knife-theres-still-shit-thats-broken-because-of-it.html
Or when he started shutting down whole data-centers and mass-firing Twitter's technical staff?
https://www.nytimes.com/2022/12/29/technology/twitter-elon-musk.html
The point of this performance was to let Musk claim that Twitter's management had let itself be gulled by featherbedding engineers who grossly overbuilt Twitter's infrastructure in order to guarantee themselves perpetual employment at the expense of Twitter's shareholders. But all that spare server capacity wasn't excess: it was the cushion, the headroom, the insurance policy. Those were the servers that would pick up the slack when another server lost its power-supply or caught a bad stick of RAM. They were the servers that could shoulder the load during the next 9/11 or Arab Spring. They were the engineering staff that could execute new projects or perform emergency maintenance – and the engineering staff that could plan the next build-out in advance so that future growth wouldn't stall out when Twitter ran out of hardware.
Musk's actions didn't occur in a vacuum. During the decades in which attenuated causality set the scene for an endless, incestuous orgy of mergers and acquisitions, it became normal for business leaders to hack away at their own firms' resiliency. University administrators fired tenured professors and replaced them with casualized, proletarianized adjuncts.
The pedagogy – and the universities' reputations – persisted after the cuts, ebbing away slowly as the institutional memory and standards eroded bit by bit. But eventually, the universities found themselves in crisis. Campuses erupted in student revolt and spectacular scenes of violent repression:
https://jayrosen.tumblr.com/post/13174138991/excerpt-from-uc-davis-2010-2012-general-catalog
Universities' reputations cratered and they found themselves easy pickings for demagogues who awarded themselves a veto over faculty, curriculum and admissions:
https://www.chronicle.com/article/columbia-capitulated-but-so-did-the-rest-of-higher-ed
That's the thing about attenuated causality: it lets people and institutions and systems play a high-stakes game of Jenga in which every non-load-bearing block is removed and added to the top of the teetering stack. No one block causes the system to collapse…until one block does, and the whole thing falls over.
It's like an avalanche. Drop one snowflake after another on the mountainside. Expose it to the midday sun and the frigid night. Sure, it creaks. Watch closely and you'll see it make minute shifts. Every now and again, a light scree of snow slides off the surface and skitters downhill, but the great mass of snow is stable.
Until it isn't. Until one last snowflake alights on the mountainside and sets the whole thing rumbling and tumbling in an inexorable, unstoppable wall of snow that only halts when it can go no lower.
The run-up to an avalanche is a long, long series of linear phenomena. A snowflake is minute. Add it to the pile and it will produce a minute effect. A little bit of heat causes a little bit of expansion. A little bit of wind blows a little of the loose cover around. But each one of these little strains on the system makes it less stable overall. The snowpack's stability is invisible. A snowpack that is about to cut loose is indistinguishable from one that will stay put for weeks or months. But once the snow's systemic resiliency has been exhausted, the next linear perturbation produces a wild, catastrophic, nonlinear collapse.
Attenuated causality lets us build up a mountain of debt: health debt, technology debt, policy debt, credibility debt. It's impossible to know when the system's homeostatic mechanisms have been fatally weakened, such that the next compromise it is asked to bear causes it to rupture, or implode, or crumble.
We can raise the level of CO2 and methane in the Earth's atmosphere year after year and see long-attenuated, linear effects: fires here, floods there, some extinctions. But one day: we overwhelm some invisible homeostatic mechanism and we lose the Gulf Stream:
https://www.bbc.com/climate/impact/gulf_stream.shtml
Same goes for the world's IT: we can fire IT workers, starve maintenance teams of budget and personnel, substitute indemnity for software quality, allow our "security" agencies to hoard and weaponize vulnerabilities, and on average, it will all work…until it stops:
https://pluralistic.net/2026/09/12/god-in-the-box/#llms-are-fake
IT might just be the most dangerously attenuated aspect of all our administrative functions. A software engineer writes a piece of code and it keeps running, even if you fire that engineer. This is one of the reasons it's so hard to unionize tech workers: when they go on strike, their scab software crosses the picket line and keeps working:
https://pluralistic.net/2026/11/07/aeiou-but-sometimes-why/#unrecognizable-playbook
But software isn't an asset, it's a liability. Code runs in a
system
, with upstream processes that produce its inputs and downstream processes that operate on its outputs. There's adjacent processes, too: other software that also gets fed by those upstream processes and also feeds those downstream processes. Even if the code never changes, the system the code is part of changes constantly, which means that the more code you have, the more code you have to maintain.
So what happens when you fire technical workers is nothing…at first. And then, bit by bit, things start to break. And break. And break. Only the people who wrote the software – and the people they trained through working alongside of them – know exactly how it works, with that deep knowledge, that
Fingerspitzengefühl
, that informs their intuition so they can figure out why things broke and how to fix them:
https://pluralistic.net/2025/09/08/process-knowledge/#dance-monkey-dance
Break that chain of knowledge and you lobotomize your institution. Reduce the full complement of experienced engineers with the wherewithal to demand higher wages and better conditions, leave behind a precarious skeleton crew of juniors you terrorize with layoff threats and AI ghost-stories, and you destroy not just your ability to spot problems before they occur, but also your capability to salvage your way out of the wreckage.
And that's what Musk's Doge did to the American state: undermined its capacity to absorb shocks, to anticipate shocks and brace for them – or even to detect the shocks when they're underway. There's a reason so much variation is preserved in our genomes and species: these are the reserves of variety that can rise to meet the changing world. Eliminate the variety, and you strip out the ability to cope with surprise, and what happens next shouldn't surprise any of us.

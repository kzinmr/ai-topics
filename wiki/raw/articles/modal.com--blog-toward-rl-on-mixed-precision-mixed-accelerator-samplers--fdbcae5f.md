# Toward RL on mixed-precision, mixed-accelerator samplers

Source: https://modal.com/blog/toward-rl-on-mixed-precision-mixed-accelerator-samplers

In large-model reinforcement learning (RL), samplers usually run on the same
accelerators and often the
same precision
as the
trainer, keeping the two numerically close for stable training. Outside of RL,
however, serving systems are not constrained in this manner: they’re free to
optimize generation for speed and cost using low-precision formats such as FP8,
MXFP4 and NVFP4, co-designed kernels, and even accelerators built just for
long-context prefill.
Since sampling already takes
80
–
85
%
of the GPUs in large RL runs, this leaves a lot on the table! We ask whether we
can use
mixed-precision, mixed-accelerator samplers
, each running its own
kernels, while keeping training stable and performant.
Samplers on six kinds of accelerators, each with its own precision and
    kernel, feed one BF16 trainer on B200.
Two problems stand in the way.
The first problem is learning. Each of these optimizations adds to
training–inference mismatch, making samples more off-policy, which often
destabilizes training. Off-policy correction is the natural solution; it lets a
trainer learn from data generated by a different policy, such as stale samples
in asynchronous training. Since it makes no assumption about why the two
policies differ, it should apply equally to different precisions or
accelerators.
The second problem is the system. RL infrastructure is usually built around
clusters of homogeneous accelerators. Mixed samplers require the opposite:
autoscaling accelerators that burst when needed and run using their own
precisions and kernels.
Through off-policy correction and our
autoscaling, serverless GPUs
, we
show that stable, performant, and cheaper RL is possible with mixed-precision,
mixed-accelerator samplers by training an open-weight model fully asynchronously
on agentic coding tasks.
Our setup
We train
Qwen3.6-35B-A3B
on
agentic coding with up to 256K tokens of context using the 2.7K repository tasks
Xiaomi
released
with
MiMo-V2.6
, after removing the repository
history and other traces through which some tasks
leak the reference fix
. In each
episode, the model creates a patch for an issue over multiple turns and submits
it through a bash tool in a
Modal Sandbox
.
It receives a reward of 1 if the submitted patch passes the task’s tests in a
fresh sandbox, and 0 otherwise. We evaluate on
SWE-bench Pro V2
,
which has no overlap with the training set, and its hard subset
HARD-51
separately. Every checkpoint is evaluated in BF16.
We use
Miles
as the RL framework, with
SGLang
samplers and a
Megatron-LM
trainer that updates the
policy in BF16 on B200 GPUs. Training is fully asynchronous with no limit on
staleness, and each update uses 128 prompts with 8 trajectories each. The
samplers keep their own accelerators, precisions and kernels, and the trainer
does not replay their MoE routing decisions.
The samplers run in one of two setups:
The mixed samplers span six kinds of accelerators: B200 and B300 serve NVFP4
weights, H100 and H200 serve FP8 and BF16 weights, and A100 and RTX PRO 6000
serve BF16 weights. Each sampler runs its accelerator’s own kernels and keeps
its KV cache in FP8. A router assigns each agent session to one of these
eight groups, in proportion to the group’s serving capacity, so each group
serves a steady share of the requests throughout training.
The homogeneous samplers run only on B200 with BF16 weights and a BF16 KV
cache. They match the trainer’s accelerator and precision, so only kernels
and staleness separate them.
Each group of samplers and its share of the requests served.
On Modal, each group of samplers scales up or down as needed, even mid-run, and
serves the trainer’s newest weights in its own precision.
Making mixed-sampler RL stable
We start from
GRPO
without any correction
for the mismatch, and add three pieces:
Masked importance sampling
(MIS)
corrects the weighting. It multiplies each token’s term by its importance
ratio and masks tokens whose ratio falls outside a fixed range, so on the
tokens it keeps, the expected update follows the training policy and every
ratio stays bounded. Without the mask, a few tokens the samplers rarely
produce could carry very large ratios and dominate the update.
Score centering
(SC) removes drift. At
each position, it subtracts the expected gradient of the log-probability under
the sampling policy, so in expectation only differences in advantage between
tokens move the policy. Importance ratios also remove drift, but masking
reintroduces it.
Top-p sampling with mask replay
(top-p) keeps rare tokens out and gives both policies the same support. The
samplers sample each token only from the top-p candidate set, so rare tokens,
which can carry extreme ratios, never enter the data. The trainer then
renormalizes its own probabilities over the same set. Runs without it sample
from the full vocabulary.
Each sampled token adds its advantage times the gradient of its log-probability
to the update. Put together, MIS reweights that term by the importance ratio, SC
centers the gradient, and top-p restricts both policies to the same candidates.
SWE-bench Pro V2 pass@4 over training on HARD-51 (left) and the full set
    (right).
Without correction, training collapses on both setups: on the mixed samplers,
GRPO collapses soon after training starts. On the homogeneous samplers, training
lasts much longer before ultimately collapsing as well. Each piece of the
correction on its own delays the collapse, but every such run ends at or below
the base model. Only SC + MIS, with or without top-p, trains stably to the end:
pass@4 rises over roughly the first 100–140 steps, then levels off at about
65–75% on HARD-51. The full set shows the same pattern from a higher start, from
89% to about 94%.
Training reward, sampler–trainer KL, trajectory length and episodes ending
    on repeated format errors, for every run.
Every collapse shares three signs: the training reward falls, the
sampler–trainer KL grows 3× or more, and most episodes end on repeated format
errors. Trajectory length does not show a consistent pattern: some collapsed
runs end their episodes early, while others start repeating themselves and run
longer. The stable runs show none of these signs. Matching the trainer’s
accelerator and precision only delays the collapse: GRPO on the homogeneous
samplers starts from a KL about 4× lower, but follows the same path from about
step 100.
A healthy and a collapsed episode, with one cell per turn.
A collapsed episode rarely writes a malformed command. Instead, partway through
a task, the model stops calling its tool, and after three replies in a row
without a tool call, the agent ends the episode. At GRPO’s step-40 checkpoint on
the mixed samplers, 62% of evaluation episodes end this way, a median of 22
turns in, as opposed to 57 turns for passing episodes. The last reply is empty
in 98% of them, while only 6% contain a malformed call.
On par with homogeneous samplers at lower cost

    + top-p on the mixed and the homogeneous samplers.
With SC + MIS + top-p, the mixed samplers reach SWE-bench Pro V2 scores on par
with the homogeneous samplers’: the two runs’ pass@4 stays close throughout
training. Their training reward and gradient norm also follow similar paths,
despite a 4.7× larger sampler–trainer KL on average. Entropy matches until about
step 160; afterwards, it rises on the homogeneous samplers and stays flat on the
mixed ones. Importantly, nothing in how the correction works is specific to this
model or these accelerators, so we expect this pattern to hold more broadly.
SWE-bench Pro V2 pass@4 against cumulative sampling cost for SC + MIS +
    top-p on HARD-51 (left) and the full set (right).
Decoupling the samplers from the trainer also makes these results cheaper to
reach. For most of training, the samplers’ cost per update was about 15% lower
on the mixed samplers than on the homogeneous samplers. Note that our setup was
built to validate the performance of using mixed-precision, mixed-accelerator
samplers rather than for minimal cost, so there are more savings to be had:
every sampler can be tuned on its own, and the mix can shift toward the most
cost-effective samplers.
The next three sections explain these results: where training–inference mismatch
comes from, why we don’t use true on-policy RL, and how off-policy correction
works.
Where training–inference mismatch comes from
A sampled token can get one probability from the sampler and another from the
trainer. The causes span the whole RL system:
Mixed samplers combine most of these at once, and their mismatch is on average
4.7× that of the homogeneous samplers.
Sampler–trainer KL for each group of samplers, averaged over training with
    SC + MIS + top-p.
Why not true on-policy RL?
Training is on-policy when every token is sampled from the policy being trained.
When the samplers and the trainer are separate systems, being on-policy requires
two things: the samplers to compute the same bitwise probabilities as the
trainer (i.e.,
true on-policy RL
),
and every sample to come from the trainer’s current weights.
Bitwise-identical sampling and training has been demonstrated. Open RL
frameworks make the two
identical for small models
and
nearly identical for GLM-5
,
and DeepSeek-V4 built
batch-invariant, deterministic kernels
to make its training and inference bitwise reproducible. Matching every source,
though, has a cost:
Most of these sources change only token probabilities, which correction accounts
for. Multi-turn context and top-p / top-k sampling break the shared context and
shared support that correction relies on, so our runs match them:
token-in-token-out, and mask replay whenever we sample with top-p. For the rest,
matching is a choice.
In practice, large runs match some of these sources and attempt to correct for
the rest. Runs that match the precision, routing and top-p mask still bound
staleness and
mask tokens with extreme importance ratios
,
and runs without staleness still
correct the training–inference mismatch
.
At scale, bitwise matching has meant kernels are written to be specific to the
model. But even then, an asynchronous run remains off-policy through staleness.
The common view is that the lower this mismatch, the more stable the training,
which is why runs keep the samplers numerically close to the trainer. However,
stability depends less on the magnitude of the mismatch than on whether it keeps
growing. Without correction, the mismatch biases the update, which can widen the
mismatch until training collapses. A smaller mismatch only delays this: GRPO on
the homogeneous samplers started with a KL about 4× lower than on the mixed
samplers but still collapsed later. With correction, training on the mixed
samplers stays stable and the performance is on par with the homogeneous
samplers despite the substantial mismatch.
Training reward and sampler–trainer KL for GRPO and SC + MIS + top-p, each
    on the mixed and the homogeneous samplers.
The magnitude of the mismatch and its growth have different causes. The
magnitude comes from the system: the accelerator, precision and kernels each
sampler runs. On the other hand, the growth comes from the update, which is what
correction fixes. So accelerators and precisions need no new kind of fix:
off-policy correction, which large runs already apply, extends to them. The goal
is to keep the mismatch bounded, not to keep it low.
So we don’t pursue true on-policy RL: the samplers and the trainer are built for
different jobs, and each keeps improving at its own. Matching ties them
together, and each gives up some of its optimizations; with correction, each
keeps them.
How off-policy correction works
Learning from data another policy generated is an established problem in RL.
IMPALA
separated the actors that generate
experience from the learner and corrected for policy lag rather than keeping
them in lockstep. The sampling policy
q
generates the tokens, and
the training policy
p
θ
learns from them. With mixed samplers, each
token comes from one sampler at one weight version, where each sampler records
the token’s probability, so
q
can differ from token to token. The correction
never needs to know why
q
differs from
p
θ
: kernels, precision,
accelerators and staleness all show up only as a different
q
.
When tokens come from
q
instead of
p
θ
, the policy-gradient update
at each position
picks up two errors
.
Drift.
Under the training policy, the gradient of the log-probability
averages to zero at each position; under
q
, it averages to a step toward
q
.
So part of the update moves the training policy regardless of which tokens
earned the reward: toward the sampling policy when the expected advantage is
positive, and away from it when it is negative. Normalizing advantages within a
group does not remove this, since the expected advantage after a given prefix is
generally not zero. The samplers then load the trainer’s new weights and add
their error again, so the trainer keeps moving toward a target that moves with
it.
The wrong weighting.
The update weights each token by how often the samplers
produce it, not by the training policy. A token can keep receiving a large
gradient after the training policy has made it unlikely.
Both errors vanish when
q
=
p
θ
and grow otherwise.
Importance sampling fixes both errors by weighting each token’s term by its
ratio
p
θ
/
q
, provided the samplers can sample every token the
training policy can. Weighting a whole trajectory by the product of its ratios
is exact, but its variance can grow exponentially with length, so large runs
apply the ratio one token at a time,
a first-order approximation
whose error
grows with the mismatch and with trajectory length. Even per token, a token the samplers rarely produce
can carry a weight up to 1 /
q
. Bounding the weight, by truncating or masking
it, controls the variance but reintroduces drift. MIS masks: it keeps the ratio
inside a fixed range and sets it to zero outside.
Truncating and masking the importance ratio. The dashed line is the
    unbounded weight.
Score centering removes drift instead. At each position, it subtracts the
expected gradient of the log-probability under
q
, so in expectation a constant
advantage no longer moves the policy. It keeps the wrong weighting, which is why
it pairs with MIS: centering the weighted term removes drift whatever the
weight, including the drift that masking brings back. SC + MIS removes drift and
weights the tokens it keeps by the training policy. Computing the expected
gradient under
q
needs
q
for every token. The samplers record it for their
most likely tokens; for every other token,
q
is approximated by the trainer’s
probabilities, rescaled to the samplers’ remaining probability mass.
Without correction (left), drift keeps pulling the trainer toward a sampler
    that moves with it; score centering (right) removes the pull. Adapted from
Martin Marek's
    animation
.
Top-p sampling breaks the shared support, and mask replay restores it. The
samplers sample only from the top-p set, so tokens outside it have zero
probability under
q
. Replaying the mask in the trainer renormalizes
p
θ
over the same set. The rarest tokens, which can carry the
largest ratios, never enter the data, and the samplers record every candidate’s
probability, so centering is exact rather than estimated.

Per group of samplers, the correction’s work follows the mismatch. A group’s
share of the extreme-ratio tokens follows its KL more than its share of the
samples: H100 FP8 supplies 10% of the samples but 29% of these tokens, while
B200 and B300 NVFP4 supply 47% of the samples and 16%. Even for H100 FP8, only
about 0.3% of its tokens fall outside [0.2, 5]. The correction weights each
token by its own ratio, so the same correction keeps training stable with every
group in the mix.
Limitations
MIS, SC and top-p all work one token at a time, so each inherits the per-token
approximation’s limit: the prefixes and the advantages still come from the
samplers. Sequence-level corrections, such as
GSPO
’s and
DeepSeek-V3.2
’s, and bounding
staleness guard against that error when it grows too large. Our runs use none of
them and stay stable, with contexts of up to 256K tokens and an average
staleness of about 4.
Top-p sampling with mask replay also changes what is trained: the update
optimizes the policy truncated to the top-p candidate set, and the logits
outside it get no gradient at that position. Our evaluation samples from the
full vocabulary, and SC + MIS + top-p evaluated about as well as SC + MIS, which
samples from the full vocabulary in training too.
Correction also leaves the samples as they are: it changes how the update uses
each token, not which trajectories the samplers produce. Choosing a precision or
an accelerator therefore remains a trade, but between throughput and sample
efficiency rather than between throughput and stability.
These corrections are not the only options, but they share one principle: the
mismatch can be large, as long as the update stays close to the on-policy one
and the mismatch stays bounded. Better ones are likely to follow.
What’s next
Correction decouples the samplers from the trainer, and that changes what RL can
run on. Sampling no longer needs a large homogeneous cluster: any accelerator
that can serve the model can contribute samples, so the sampling fleet can be
sized separately from the trainer and grown mid-run as the run needs more
samples (e.g., as trajectories get longer). Inference optimizations also carry
straight into RL:
faster kernels
,
speculative decoding
, lower
precision, or prefill and decode split across different accelerators can all
speed up sampling without the trainer having to match them. And the trainer can
move to the best accelerators and its own low precision, such as
FP8
or
NVFP4
, without the samplers following. As
accelerators keep diversifying across vendors, generations and number formats,
this decoupling becomes essential to enable frontier training for all.

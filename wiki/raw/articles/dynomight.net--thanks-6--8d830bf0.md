# Underrated reasons to be thankful VI

Source: https://dynomight.net/thanks-6/

That in most of the world today, you can walk around freely during the day, even in crowds of thousands of people, with almost zero concern that someone will stab you and take your stuff.
That if you’re feeling nauseated, you can apparently pour some isopropyl alcohol onto a pad and sniff it, and the effect is apparently as strong as prescription antiemetics but faster, and this is apparently often used in emergency rooms, although we don’t have great RCTs and no one seems to have the foggiest idea of why it should work and for some reason this trick has utterly failed to entrench itself as a folk remedy.
That your upper body has a vastly higher density of apocrine and sebaceous glands than your legs, so provided you cover your gland-rich groin with underwear, you can skip washing your pants and skirts for a shockingly long time, which is good because work is bad, though even if they (the pants/skirts) still look/smell clean you may still want to wash them occasionally because that will prevent microscopic grime from chewing up the fibers and re-align the weave and (with plant-based fabrics) restore fiber elasticity, thankfully.
That intestinal villi.
That the Earth is struck by lightning a million times a day, creating small fires, and that microbes eat dead stuff, and that if oxygen levels creep up, then you get more small fires which very slowly bring oxygen levels back down, meaning there isn’t enough fuel and oxygen for a single firestorm to grow and self-sustain and consume the planet.
That boring standard best practices, that for 90% of problems, if you just find the boring standard best practices and make a reasonable effort to follow them, you’ll do better than 90% of people, because 90% of people don’t do that, because they’re so boring, which is bad, but you can.
That your great-great-great-great-great-great-great-great-great-grandma breathed air with 280 ppm of CO₂ and all your grandmas before that got 200 to 280 ppm, going back to before those grandmas were human, while if you go outside today you’ll get 432 ppm (ever-increasing), a situation evolution had no reason to prepare for, and given that stuffy air is bad for thinking you might worry what the extra CO₂ is doing to us, but when tested carefully it’s unclear if CO₂ itself has any effect on cognitive performance even at levels ten times higher, so the impact of that extra 152 ppm is probably essentially zero.
That in a modern car, your risk of dying per kilometer is only ⅓ of a similar car from the early 1990s.
That, to keep your eyes working, your body is constantly flushing them with tears, and instead of letting the dirty tears run down your face, your head has an internal tube that drains into the back of your nose where you swallow the tears along with all the dust and bacteria that lands in your eyes—so cool!—and also that this is why liquid comes of your nose when you cry a lot, you are literally crying out of your nose, and also that there’s a valve at the bottom of the tube to prevent snot from going up, thankfully, but if you’re working with a sufficiently large amount of snot it can and sometimes does go up the tube and out your eyes, yes snot can literally come out of your eyes, this is exciting, we like this.

That the needs of a human, in sorted order, are: air, safety, heat, water, sleep, food, toilet, medicine, movement, stability, intimacy, community, respect, autonomy, stimulation, privacy, play, purpose, and that most people reading this have to go at halfway through that list before seeing major issues.
That purpose, stupid stubborn purpose, possibly intractable, but if you’re worried about cosmic purpose and you’ve got issues earlier on the list, try fixing them and I bet you won’t feel as worried about cosmic purpose, it’s the nature of the animal.
That music, not something that first principles would suggest should exist.
That in women, urinary continence is achieved by a roughly equal contribution of an internal sphincter, passive tissue, and a pelvic floor + external sphincter, but men after the internal sphincter have a prostate, which creates so much passive resistance that the external sphincter is rarely needed, but sometimes said prostate develops mutations and must be removed along with the internal sphincter, which drops the entire continence workload on a pelvic floor + external sphincter that has been relaxing for 5+ decades, causing leaks, but within a year the pelvic floor + external sphincter typically rise to the occasion, stopping the leaks, and that by doing exercises before and/or after surgery, that interval can be shortened, sometimes even to zero, works best if you have an expert use ultrasound to check you’re exercising correctly, also works great after childbirth or for anyone experiencing general sphincter entropy.
That relationships—one of the great drivers of happiness—if you want better relationships, you can probably achieve that by deliberately increasing your level of relationship “skill”, which is done by following boring standard relationship best practices.
That due to how spermatogonial stem cells are activated, one testicle produces only gametes with an X chromosome and the other only gametes with a Y chromosome so if you’re going to lose a testicle you might want to freeze some sperm, that sounds plausible, that’s what Anaxagoras thought, but thankfully it is completely false, and also that if you lose a testicle, you lose half of testosterone production, but your brain notices and spits out extra leuteinizing hormone, rallying Leydig cells in the remaining testicle and holding testosterone levels constant, no problem.
That trauma care, if you were gunshot in 1964 and still alive when EMS arrived, your odds of surviving were ~66% while today they are ~90%, as our society, despite its seeming disorder, has methodically stacked EMS, 911, regional trauma centers, surgical innovations, police cruiser transport, better imaging, new drugs, statistical lessons on tourniquets / saline / blood ratios, and now prehospital whole blood transfusions.
That each year, 8.2×10¹⁷ kWh of energy from the sun intersects the surface of Earth, while the average human uses 2.1×10⁴ kWh, which with 8.1×10⁹ humans is 4823 times less, thankfully, so even if you did something stupid like grow a C₄ plant like corn with a full-year capture efficiency of only 0.5%, you could in principle get ‘er done by covering 1/(4823×0.005) ≈ 4.1% of the Earth in glorious corn, which, OK, exceeds the 3% currently used as farmland, but if you use 20% efficient solar panels that drops to 0.1%, the amount we currently use for barley.
That the fraction of power lost to resistance going down a wire is (power) × (resistance) / (voltage)², which goes up when power goes up, which is bad, and to decrease resistance by 10× requires putting 10× more material into your wires, which is also bad, but instead you could increase voltage, but if you send 12,470-volt wires into people’s homes they will die from electrocution or fires, so maybe you should drop the voltage close to their home, good idea, but if it’s the late 1800s, then you’ll find that transforming voltage is hard, but perhaps instead of sending electrons in one direction, you can have them wiggle back and forth, the wiggles propagating in waves, then you can transform voltage by just wrapping a bunch of wires around an iron core, and now 140 years later we have semiconductors that convert between wiggling and non-wiggling electrons, and sometimes we have chains that go from no wiggles (solar panel) to wiggles (voltage transformer) back to no wiggles (high-voltage transmission line) back to wiggles (voltage transformer + local grid) back to no wiggles (your phone charger), which is good, for some reason.
That public libraries, doing fine in a changing world, currently delivered in the US at a total cost of $50/person.
That alcohol is, on net, probably bad, and young people in rich countries have noticed this and are drinking less, and this has created beautiful economies of scale in specialized yeast and vacuum distillation and membrane filters which are making nonalcoholic beer increasingly cheap and good, which is good, and sadly these don’t work well for wine and not at all for spirits, which is bad, but beer tastes better than those things, technology has delivered in the right place.
That hedonic adaptation, typically an adversary in our quest for galactic bliss-maximization, is also in some respects an ally, most notably if you stop eating processing-maxxed combinations of sugar/salt/fat, you will at first feel deprived but will soon find that roasted carrots taste very sweet and thus you can get the health benefits of healthy food while still enjoying food nearly as much as before, although ironically hedonic adaptation will also likely take hold of those health benefits, limiting how much durable bliss they create, but whatever, I’ll take it.
That if you go to almost any park / forest / mountain, almost anywhere in the world, and you hike 5 km away from the road, it will not be crowded.
That our civilization is constructed along a two-dimensional manifold in three-dimensional space, meaning that if a warhead of energy E is detonated, the energy delivered to civilization scales as E²ᐟ³, and if E > 10 megatons, then energy starts to vent out into space, dropping the energy delivered to E¹ᐟ² or even lower, so if you wanted to destroy mainland Spain with a single blastwave (please don’t) you’d need a 30 teraton weapon (2 billion Hiroshimas, 1300 copies of peak early 1960s global arsenal, ⅓ Chicxulub), and to build that you’d either need to mine 400 billion tons of uranium ore (60× known land reserves), or to filter 1 million tons of deuterium out of water (1-2 years of global electricity production) and then store it at -250 °C in a tank 20 times larger than an oil supertanker and covered in millions of tons of lead, or you could make do with only 275 thousand tons of deuterium and drop the -250 °C tank but then you’ll need to mine 12 million tons of lithium (40% of known lithium reserves), seems hard.
That if you’re OK using fire instead of a blastwave, then note that while blastwaves bend along the atmosphere, X-rays and thermal radiation need line of sight, and that to get a decent angle on all of mainland Spain, you’d need to detonate somewhere like 500 km out in space, but then 90% of the X-rays won’t be pointed towards Spain, and X-rays can’t fly through air, so instead your goal is to turn the upper atmosphere above Spain into a superheated plasma that absorbs the X-rays and re-emits light at lower frequencies, but then half of that light goes back out into space and much of the rest is lost to ozone or dust or clouds, and the energy pulse stretches to several seconds, the physics are insanely complicated but the consensus is that you’re better off dropping down to 50 km and hoping the fireball peeks over the horizon, and it happens that the atmosphere is ~90 km high which means a single fireball can just barely see all of mainland Spain, so a 20-40 gigaton weapon (1-2× early 1960s global arsenal) might ignite the flammable parts of Spain on a cloudless day, which is bad, but short of building an artificial asteroid, Earth’s curvature does seem to make it quasi-impossible for a single detonation to destroy anything larger than Spain, thankfully, I guess.
That there’s still interest in watching humans spell and play chess and run fast, hopefully a prelude.
That if you were born before ~1993 (earlier in Japan and South Korea, later in Europe) you were likely infected with VZV and got chickenpox, but before the virus was killed, it injected copies of its DNA into some of your ganglia, mostly near the spine or brainstem, and ganglia don’t die, so that DNA is still there today, floating around with your chromosomes, and rarely your ganglia transcribe it and make new VZV, but your immune system kills it, but as you age, the virus may someday travel down a nerve pathway to your skin, causing lots of pain, which is bad, but that need not happen because the shingles vaccine exists, thankfully, and also that if you were born after ~1993, you likely got the chickenpox vaccine and never got chickenpox but—surprise—you still have VZV DNA in your ganglia because that vaccine contains live virus, but it’s of a weakened strain so your risk of getting shingles is somewhat unclear, maybe you’ll still need the vaccine someday, but if you don’t get it and you do get shingles, it will likely happen in the same part of the body where you were injected with the chickenpox vaccine decades before, which is fun.
That we are very cynical these days, but when someone says,
I am doing X because I think Y
, and everyone responds,
nuh-uh, you are doing X because you think Z
, upon close inspection often they really do think Y, and while recognizing this generally doesn’t make it easier to get them to stop doing X, it makes the world look a bit brighter.
That during the peak of the cold war, some people figured that we were doomed and decided it was better not to have children, which even looking back now doesn’t seem like a totally crazy decision, but most people didn’t decide that, thankfully.
That the best way to preserve a car is to keep it in the garage and only drive it slowly to church on Sundays, and you might expect the human animal to work the same way, forcing you choose when to “spend” yourself, but for the most part it seems to be the opposite, the way to keep your mind and body working is to drive them a lot, at high speeds, to new places.

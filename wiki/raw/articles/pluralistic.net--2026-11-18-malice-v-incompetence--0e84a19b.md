Enshittifiers groom us for scammers (
permalink
)
We live in a scam economy: there's a presidential memecoin, a public health system run by a supplement peddler (who sold his trademark for "MAHA" to a pal before taking the job), and Spacex got to debut into the NASDAQ 100 and turn your retirement savings into exit liquidity for Musk's Emirati and tech oligarch cronies:
https://prospect.org/2026/06/10/elon-musk-spacex-ipo-retirement-savings-index-funds-stock-market/#:~:text=The%20upcoming%20SpaceX,rake%20in%20millions.
Then there's AI: voice cloning, deepfakes, and image-genned documents (including convincing IDs) have ushered in a golden age of account takeovers, identity theft and good, old-fashioned impersonation:
https://www.404media.co/the-confessions-of-an-ai-fraud-kingpin-onlyfake-owner-nazarenko/
AI summarization is a priceless gift to fraudsters, who can comb through otherwise intractable mountains of breach data to customize precision-targeted spear-phishing campaigns for millions of potential victims at once. These summarization tools have a
lot
to summarize, thanks to a string of catastrophic breaches that saw (for example) the escape of the credit histories of nearly every person in America:
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_Equifax_data_breach
The companies involved in those breaches were not meaningfully punished. Quite the contrary, they were allowed to buy up and merge more of their competitors, combining these new divisions' creaking servers with their own spit-and-baling-wire frankensystems, even as they fired the people who had built and maintained those systems (in the name of "efficiency").
The commercial surveillance sector has repeatedly proven itself to be more interested in profits than security or accuracy, and yet we continue to entrust them with crucial public functions, including purging electoral rolls:
https://mississippitoday.org/2026/11/10/u-s-senate-election-mississippi-andrew-goodman/
For margin-obsessed corporate leadership, IT cuts represent a unique hazard. When a manager fires the person who maintains the assembly line, the shop-floor quickly seizes up. But managers who fire IT staff inherit software that keeps chugging along long after the people who built it are gone…until it stops, and the company no longer knows how to restart it. This is one of the unique challenges of unionizing tech workers: when the tech workers go on strike, the scab software they wrote crosses the picket line on them:
https://pluralistic.net/2026/11/07/aeiou-but-sometimes-why/#unrecognizable-playbook
Tech debt has a long maturity: you can incur a
lot
of tech debt before the margin call comes and you find yourself in technological bankruptcy. Corporate management has spent decades playing IT Jenga, removing every available block, leaving behind a skeletal skyline of teetering towers that now routinely collapse:
https://www.404media.co/breaking-everything/
The entire IT system is grossly overleveraged and technological bankruptcies are everywhere. This is very bad, because the USA hasn't had a new consumer privacy law since 1988 (when Congress banned disclosure of your VHS rental history), which means that these eggshell-thin servers are
jammed
with the most sensitive data imaginable about everyone, everywhere:
https://pluralistic.net/2026/11/02/agony-verification/#great-turnbulls-ghost
Far from breaking this cycle of overaccumulation and breach of sensitive data, the AI sector is accelerating it, claiming that a single person's "consent" to use pervert glasses, always-on mics, or other surveillance equipment can stand in for the consent of everyone they meet, see, or converse with:
https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/11/tech-companies-are-trying-shift-entire-concept-privacy-again
This is the new normal: everything is broken, everything is breached. It's completely normal to wake up and read panicked headlines advising companies to shut off their servers until the latest showstopper bug can be patched. Half the email you get is from companies announcing new security protocols in the wake of a fresh data breach (the other half is from companies announcing changes to their terms of service to allow them to collect even
more
of your data, and to ban
you
from suing them when that data leaks):
https://pluralistic.net/2026/10/26/abandon-hope/#all-ye-who-enter-here
So it's also normal to get phone calls, emails and texts from your bank, employer, insurer and broker asking you to verify a transaction or requiring you to sign up for a new two-factor authentication system. What's more, it's normal for these entities to insist that you
immediately
install a new app or go through a new authentication process that is totally unlike any of the other ones you're already using.
It's normal to log in to a service you use every day and be confronted with an entirely different login flow – a CAPTCHA unlike any you've ever seen, a requirement to visit a third-party site to complete an authentication step (which may involve taking selfies and transmitting copies of sensitive ID documents using an app from a company you've never heard of), an embedded widget that only works if you disable your browser's advanced privacy settings that prevent cross-site scripting and other horrors.
We're told to hang up on anyone who calls us from our bank or credit-card issuer and to call them back using the number on the back of our cards. But when we call our banks, we get shunted to AI chatbots that "hallucinate" nonsensical advice for accessing our accounts. If we try to reach a human, we're stuck in lengthy hold queues whose soundtrack is a looping announcement scolding us for not using a faulty chatbot or the company's broken website to solve our problems. When a human
does
come on the line, they often seem to be improvising a new security protocol that was created five minutes before to deal with the latest state of exception.
This is the security storm: a world of zero visibility, shifting gale-force winds, and blockages that impede your usual route and force you down unfamiliar paths every time you venture forth.
It is the
perfect
environment for fraudsters.
It's not just that fraudsters can impersonate anyone with AI voice-cloning or generate undetectable document forgeries: it's
also
the fact that your bank, HR department, insurer, credit card company and government have groomed you to expect a chaotic process that requires you to do something different every time you try to access a service.
They've groomed you to turn off your browser's advanced protections. They've groomed you to ignore your carefully created, carefully saved random passwords in favor of clicking on links that get texted or emailed to you by third-party services you've never heard of. They've groomed you to expect cold-calls from outsource fraud prevention companies that don't know how to pronounce your bank's name.
This isn't new. As far back as 2009, the cryptographer Ben Laurie was railing against "Verified by Visa," which required you to enter sensitive data into a page hosted on a domain that was separate from both your bank
and
the merchant you were paying, which Laurie called "Phished by Visa":
https://web.archive.org/web/20090331094020/http://www.links.org/?p=591
No one listened to Laurie. Instead, the banks got
worse
. I've been a customer of HSBC subsidiary Firstdirect for nearly 20 years, and in that time, I've been told to make note of a "customer ID number," a "telephone passcode",
eight
password recovery answers, a "web username", a web password, a "digital secure key password", a "digital secure key PIN", and a regular PIN.
Despite all this, I haven't been able to access my bank account for two months, ever since they "upgraded" their system to block app log-ins on mobile devices running the open, secure Android fork Graphene (Firstdirect doesn't use a standard two-factor authenticator; you have to use its app to generate a one-time PIN in addition to all those other authentication details every time you log in, even via a browser).
When I spoke to Firstdirect about why they'd blocked Graphene, they told me that they "considered open source to be insecure." Talk about grooming for fraudsters: telling your customers that they should only trust systems that
can't
be independently inspected and validated is grossly irresponsible.
(Don't worry, we're going to use the UK's excellent open banking system to switch back to the Co-Op Bank, which we left after it was mismanaged to the brink of bankruptcy and flogged to private equity; it's since been restructured as a co-op again:)
https://www.banktrack.org/success/the_cooperative_bank_bought_back_into_mutual_ownership
The perfect mark is someone who thinks they can't get scammed. The more technologically savvy and security conscious you are, the more you've encountered the incoherence of the official system and the less surprised you'll be when you're told to do something
obviously
insecure in order to complete a transaction:
Boy, that stupid, dangerous requirement is even stupider and more dangerous than all the other stupid, dangerous things I've been forced to do this year.
That's the story of Uri Bram, a technological sophisticate who edits Atoms vs Bits and nearly got scammed trying to buy an Amtrak ticket:
https://www.atvbt.com/expected-incompetence-enables-fraud/
Bram tried to buy his Amtrak ticket on the web but found himself on a nonfunctional site that put him through a bizarre stopgap process that required him to enter his email address, click a link, log in to his Paypal account… Each step was more out-of-place than the last, but all of them had the eerie ring of plausibility, given the dysfunction of Bram's other interactions with giant, creaking companies like Amtrak. As Bram puts it: "Expected incompetence enables fraud."
The corollary is that technological excellence makes fraud easier to spot and harder to pull off. Most of our anti-fraud measures demand that
you
be more vigilant and more discerning. We'd be much better off if we insisted that our official institutions invest in coherent, orderly practices that are thoughtfully designed, well maintained, and staffed by easily reached experts who can guide us through our problems. It's much harder to impersonate a system where you can get through to a thoroughly trained, in-house customer service rep in a few minutes than it is to impersonate a system that's typically
worse
than the fraudster's vibe-coded scam website, whose "customer service" has been replaced by broken chatbots.
The ongoing collapse of all of our technology has created a permanent state of exception in which fraudsters' demands are no weirder than the ad-libbing our own essential institutions engage in every time we deal with them. The answer can't be to demand superhuman fraud-detection skills from the public: rather, we must demand excellence from our institutions:
https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2026/07/mayor-mamdani-launches–public-interest-technology–pit–crew–t

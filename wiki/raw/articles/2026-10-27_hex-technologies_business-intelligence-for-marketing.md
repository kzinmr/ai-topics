Marketing teams have no shortage of data. Between ad platforms, CRM systems, marketing automation, product analytics, and billing, nearly every interaction with a customer generates another signal.
But answering a seemingly simple question like "Which campaigns actually drove pipeline last quarter?" can still need hours of reconciling data across different systems.
And the questions rarely stop there. Which segments converted? How did those customers retain? What happens if we shift spending between channels?
Business intelligence
(BI) helps marketing teams connect those dots. Traditionally, that meant dashboards, reports, and analyses built by the data team. Today, AI is changing how much of that work marketers can do themselves.
Instead of waiting for a new report, marketers can increasingly ask questions in plain language, investigate results, build interactive analytical tools, and automate recurring analysis.
The opportunity isn't simply to produce more marketing reports. It's to give marketing teams the analytical capabilities to make better decisions, without sacrificing the shared metrics and governance they rely on.
What is business intelligence for marketing?
Business intelligence for marketing
is the practice of combining and analyzing data across marketing channels and business systems to understand performance and inform decisions.
Unlike a channel-specific dashboard, marketing BI connects campaign activity to downstream outcomes like qualified pipeline, revenue, customer retention, and lifetime value.
For example, Google Ads might show how many conversions a campaign generated. Salesforce might show how many opportunities those leads became. Billing data might reveal which customers eventually purchased and how much revenue they generated.
Marketing BI brings those signals together to answer a more valuable question:
Which marketing investments are actually contributing to business growth?
It's worth distinguishing marketing BI from adjacent categories:
Channel analytics:
Reporting within individual platforms like Google Ads, LinkedIn, or Meta.
Marketing intelligence:
Competitive and market research tools like Similarweb, Crayon, or Klue.
Data integration:
Tools like Fivetran, Supermetrics, or Improvado that move marketing data between systems.
Marketing BI:
The analytical layer that combines those data sources to investigate performance and support business decisions.
The distinction matters because most important marketing questions can't be answered inside a single channel dashboard.
Six common uses of business intelligence in marketing
Marketing BI can support everything from campaign reporting to sophisticated forecasting models. The following are some of the most common applications.
1. Cross-channel campaign performance
Marketing teams need to understand how campaigns perform across channels, not just how each individual platform reports its own success.
Common questions include:
Which channels generated the most qualified pipeline last quarter?
How did campaign performance change across audience segments?
Which programs influenced opportunities that eventually closed?
Answering those questions means connecting advertising, website, marketing automation, and CRM data. A campaign that generates thousands of leads might look successful in a channel dashboard, but less impressive if those leads rarely become customers.
A particularly useful example is
Hex's own marketing attribution app
, which helps the marketing team understand how programs and content contribute to pipeline beyond what its primary attribution model captures.
Rather than relying exclusively on a fixed report, marketers can investigate how different campaigns, content, and touchpoints influence downstream results.
2. Marketing attribution and customer acquisition cost
Attribution helps marketers understand which interactions contribute to a conversion. Customer acquisition cost (CAC) connects those outcomes to what the company spent acquiring customers.
Both become complicated when customers interact with multiple channels before converting. Someone might see a LinkedIn ad, attend a webinar, search for your company, and eventually request a demo through your website. Different platforms may claim credit for the same conversion.
Even within one platform, attribution depends on the lookback window and measurement settings.
Google's attribution documentation
explains how different lookback windows affect which interactions receive conversion credit.
Marketing BI helps reconcile these signals into consistent reporting. Teams can compare first-touch, last-touch, or multi-touch attribution and calculate blended CAC using spend and customer data from multiple systems.
The goal isn't necessarily to find one perfect attribution model. It's to give marketing leaders enough visibility to understand the tradeoffs and make more informed budget decisions.
3. Customer segmentation and lifecycle analysis
Not every customer acquired through a campaign delivers the same long-term value. Marketing teams need to understand which segments convert, retain, expand, or eventually churn.
That needs marketing acquisition data combined with product usage, billing, and customer information. At
ClickUp
, the data science team built a churn prediction model, but traditional dashboards couldn't give marketing and customer success teams the flexibility they needed to act on it.
They turned the model into an interactive Hex data app that allowed business teams to explore customer segments and identify retention opportunities themselves. Marketing used the app to focus campaigns on lower-churn customer profiles, while lifecycle and customer success teams developed targeted engagement strategies.
The result was more than $1 million saved in churn-related costs. This illustrates how marketing analytics can move beyond measuring historical results. A predictive model becomes much more valuable when marketers can interact with its findings and use them to shape campaigns.
4. Lead scoring and pipeline quality
Generating leads is only useful if those leads have a reasonable chance of becoming customers. Marketing BI helps teams understand the relationship between lead sources, qualification criteria, pipeline creation, and closed-won revenue.
For B2B companies, this often means analyzing conversion through the entire funnel:
Lead → MQL → SQL → Opportunity → Customer
But simply reporting those conversion rates is only the beginning. Teams also need to investigate why certain leads perform differently and test whether their qualification criteria should change.
At
Algolia
, marketing operations built an interactive lead-scoring application in Hex that lets the team experiment with qualification thresholds and immediately see how those changes affect lead volumes.
That work contributed to a 33% reduction in disqualified leads and saved 140 hours in a single quarter. Rather than repeatedly asking the data team to model different scenarios, marketing could test potential changes independently.
5. Marketing budget allocation and scenario planning
Marketing leaders constantly make tradeoffs about where to invest.
If one channel is outperforming expectations, should the team increase spending? What happens if paid search costs rise? How much additional pipeline might a larger events budget generate?
Traditional reporting helps teams understand historical return on investment (ROI). Scenario planning goes further by estimating how different decisions might affect future results.
For example, marketers might combine historical conversion rates, acquisition costs, average deal sizes, and sales-cycle assumptions to model expected pipeline from different budget allocations.
An analyst can build the underlying model, then publish it as an
interactive data app
where a marketing leader can adjust assumptions without modifying the code.
With AI-powered building, creating these experiences can become significantly faster, making sophisticated planning workflows accessible to more marketing teams.
6. Forecasting and growth planning
Marketing forecasting connects current campaign performance to future business outcomes.
Common questions include:
How much qualified pipeline will marketing generate next quarter?
Are we on track to meet our pipeline target?
Which channels and campaigns are contributing most to growth?
What happens if conversion rates improve or acquisition costs increase?
Answering these questions needs more than extrapolating historical performance. Different channels produce leads with different conversion rates, deal sizes, and sales cycles. Changes to campaign spend, qualification criteria, and market conditions can also significantly affect the forecast.
Marketing BI helps teams combine these factors to understand where they're likely to land and test how different assumptions could change the outcome.
With interactive forecasting models, marketers can adjust expected conversion rates, channel budgets, or pipeline targets and immediately see the projected impact. Rather than asking an analyst to rerun the forecast every time assumptions change, they can explore different scenarios themselves and make more informed planning decisions.
Why marketing BI is particularly challenging
Marketing analytics has some challenges that make it harder to maintain than many other business reporting workflows.
Data lives across many systems.
Campaign engagement, website activity, product usage, CRM records, and billing data often reside in different tools. Connecting them accurately needs consistent customer identifiers, relationships, and definitions.
Historical data can change.
Marketing performance numbers aren't always final when the reporting period ends.
Google documents how conversion lag
can cause reported acquisition costs and return on ad spend to change as conversions arrive later. Conversion adjustments can also revise previously recorded results.
Business definitions evolve.
Marketing and sales may disagree on what constitutes a qualified lead or when pipeline should be attributed to a campaign. As qualification criteria and go-to-market strategies change, those definitions need to remain consistent across reporting.
The questions constantly change.
New campaigns, channels, segments, and experiments create analytical questions nobody anticipated when the original dashboard was built.
Historically, these challenges meant more requests for the data team, more dashboards, and more reporting maintenance. AI creates an opportunity to change that, provided the underlying data and context remain trustworthy.
How AI is changing business intelligence for marketing
AI is fundamentally changing how marketing teams interact with their data.
Until recently, self-service generally meant giving marketers access to dashboards, spreadsheets, or drag-and-drop interfaces. Those tools helped, but they still required people to learn how the underlying BI platform worked.
As
AI models become more capable of real-world analytical work
, marketers can increasingly accomplish much more through natural language.
From dashboard consumption to conversational exploration
Imagine a demand generation manager noticing that pipeline from paid campaigns declined last week. Instead of searching through dashboards or requesting a new analysis, they can ask:
"Why did paid pipeline decline last week?"
Then follow up:
"Was that driven by fewer qualified leads or lower opportunity conversion?"
"Which campaigns contributed most?"
"How does that compare with the previous quarter?"
With
Hex Threads
, marketers can investigate questions like these conversationally, drawing on the definitions and business context maintained by their data team.
The important change isn't just a faster first answer. It's that marketers can keep exploring without every new question becoming another request. That's why
self-service analytics
is increasingly shifting away from complex BI interfaces toward natural-language conversations.
From requesting reports to building analytical tools
AI is also changing who can create dashboards and applications. A marketer might want a campaign performance dashboard, a lead-scoring simulator, or an interactive view of customer retention by acquisition source.
Traditionally, those requests need a data practitioner to assemble the queries, calculations, charts, and controls. With Generative Apps, teams can increasingly build these experiences from natural-language prompts, then refine them through visual controls or direct access to the underlying logic.
That means marketing teams can go beyond consuming dashboards and create reusable analytical experiences around the questions they're trying to answer.
From recurring reports to proactive analysis
Much of marketing analytics happens on a predictable schedule. Every Monday, someone reviews pipeline creation. Every month, the team examines channel performance. Every quarter, marketing leadership investigates budget efficiency.
Traditional reporting automates the delivery of numbers, but someone still needs to investigate what changed. With
Agent Tasks
, AI agents can perform recurring analysis and deliver findings directly through Slack or email.
For example, a marketer could schedule a weekly investigation into campaign performance, asking an agent to identify significant changes, explore potential causes, and highlight areas that deserve attention.
Rather than simply refreshing a dashboard, the system performs more of the analytical work behind the report. These capabilities expand the scope of marketing self-service, but they also create a new responsibility: ensuring everyone is working from trusted business context.
How to keep marketing analytics trustworthy as AI adoption grows
Making it easier for everyone to analyze data doesn't automatically make the answers accurate.
An AI agent might generate perfectly valid SQL while misunderstanding how your company defines pipeline, which attribution model to use, or which accounts should count toward customer acquisition.
And when people start generating analyses independently in ChatGPT, Claude, and other tools, those inconsistencies can spread across disconnected conversations and artifacts.
As Hex describes in
The AI data sprawl
, the challenge is no longer just making analysis accessible. It's keeping rapidly generated analytical work trustworthy and reusable.
For marketing teams, that needs a few foundations.
Shared business context.
Agents need access to consistent metric definitions, approved datasets, documentation, and business rules. That context may already exist in the warehouse, dbt, semantic models, or other systems rather than needing to be rebuilt in a proprietary BI layer. Understanding
how context works in analytics
is increasingly important as teams adopt agents.
Inspectable answers.
Marketers and analysts should be able to see which sources, queries, and calculations produced an answer, especially when reconciling metrics with sales or finance.
Ongoing context management.
As campaigns, definitions, and data change, context can become outdated.
Hex Context Studio
helps data teams observe AI usage, identify missing or conflicting context, and improve answer quality over time.
The goal is to expand marketing's analytical independence without creating another set of disconnected metrics the data team must reconcile.
What to look for in a marketing BI platform
When evaluating business intelligence tools for marketing, traditional features like data connectivity, dashboards, scheduled reporting, and permissions still matter.
But AI introduces additional questions worth testing:
Self-service:
Can marketers ask questions, investigate answers, and follow up entirely in natural language?
Analytical flexibility:
Can they move from an initial question into more sophisticated analysis, build interactive dashboards or apps, and automate recurring work?
Context and governance:
Can the platform use existing metric definitions and business context, expose the logic behind answers, and help the data team identify and improve weak results?
Interoperability:
Can marketing teams access governed analytics from tools like Slack, Claude, and ChatGPT through capabilities such as
MCP and CLI
, rather than being required to work exclusively inside the BI platform?
Cost and accessibility:
What does it cost to give the broader marketing organization access, including viewer licenses, AI usage, and warehouse compute?

Frequently Asked Questions
What's the difference between marketing analytics and marketing BI?
Marketing analytics refers broadly to the techniques used to understand marketing performance, including attribution, segmentation, experimentation, forecasting, and statistical analysis.
Marketing BI typically refers to the platforms and workflows that make this analysis accessible through reporting, dashboards, self-service exploration, and other analytical experiences.
The distinction is becoming less rigid as AI-native platforms bring advanced analysis and BI workflows closer together.
Do marketing teams need a data warehouse for BI?
Not necessarily. Many reporting tools connect directly to advertising platforms, CRM systems, and spreadsheets.
However, more sophisticated analysis that combines marketing, sales, product, and financial data generally benefits from a centralized warehouse such as Snowflake, BigQuery, or Databricks.
A warehouse also makes it easier to maintain shared definitions across different analytical tools.
Who should own marketing BI: marketing operations or the data team?
Responsibility is usually shared. Marketing operations should own the business questions, campaign definitions, and reporting requirements. The data team typically owns the underlying data infrastructure, shared metrics, security, and governance.
AI can shift more day-to-day analytical work toward marketing while allowing the data team to focus on maintaining the foundations that make self-service reliable.
How can marketing teams keep metrics consistent with sales and finance?
Start by agreeing on definitions for shared metrics like pipeline creation, qualified leads, CAC, customer counts, and revenue.
Maintain those definitions in a governed system, document important attribution and qualification rules, and ensure reporting and AI-generated analysis reference the same logic.
As those definitions change, teams also need visibility into which reports and analyses depend on them so discrepancies can be caught before they reach leadership.

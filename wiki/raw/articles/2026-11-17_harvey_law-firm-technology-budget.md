Planning Your Law Firm’s 2027 Technology Budget
by
Megan McMillin
•
Nov 16, 2026
Preparing a law firm’s technology budget means working through requests that serve different purposes. Some spending keeps the firm’s existing systems reliable. Other investments help a practice take on new work or develop a service for clients. The firm also needs to decide how much it can spend testing ideas whose value it has yet to establish.
The
Three Horizons framework
offers a useful comparison. It describes how organizations can support their core business while developing emerging opportunities and exploring future possibilities. Applied to technology budgeting, it helps explain why a system replacement and an experiment need different justifications. Both need a clear purpose, but the firm cannot expect the same level of certainty from each.
1. Connect spending to the firm’s priorities
The firm’s plans give context to individual technology requests. A corporate practice expecting more transactions may need to reduce the time lawyers spend preparing first drafts. A new practice may need specialist research sources. Understanding the work behind a request makes it easier to assess its importance and explain why it deserves funding.
Those requests sit alongside existing contractual commitments and the cost of keeping core systems operating securely and reliably. Finance, technology, and practice leaders need a shared view of those obligations when they discuss what the firm can take on next.
Dependencies also affect the decision. A new knowledge tool may require the firm to organize its documents and correct access permissions before lawyers can use it effectively. The proposal needs to account for that work. Otherwise, the firm risks approving a purchase without funding the conditions that would make it useful.
For each significant request, the budget discussion should establish what the firm expects to improve and what would happen if it waited another year. That gives leaders a practical basis for comparing proposals that address different needs.
2. Reassess existing tools before renewing them
A complete view of technology spending includes central IT purchases, tools that practice groups buy directly, and subscriptions that individuals expense. Looking across those purchases can reveal overlapping costs or capabilities the firm already owns.
Usage data helps, but it needs context. A product with few users may serve a small practice doing important specialist work. The lawyers who rely on it can explain what it contributes and what they would need instead. Low adoption can also reflect a training problem rather than a lack of demand for the tool.
Contract timing matters when the firm identifies something it could replace. If an existing contract runs through September, a replacement that starts in January will create a period when the firm pays for both. The budget needs to reflect that overlap and any migration costs.
Renewal notice deadlines therefore deserve attention before the firm counts on savings. The relevant number is the amount the firm can actually stop spending during the budget year, after accounting for the cost of the transition.
3. Budget for the work required to implement the technology
The vendor’s price will not cover everything the firm needs to contribute. A project may require document migration, integration with other systems, and work on permissions. The budget should distinguish the services the vendor provides from the work the firm will handle itself.
Internal time deserves particular attention. An AI rollout may depend on the same IT and knowledge teams that are already working on a document management upgrade. Each project can look manageable on its own. Looking at them together may reveal that the firm expects the same people to do more than they can realistically take on.
That should affect the budget decision. The firm may need to start one project later or pay for additional support. Assigning an owner helps, but it does not create the time that person needs to do the work.
Practices have a role here too. A group requesting a tool should identify the lawyers who will help test it and explain how they will make time for that work. Their involvement will also help the firm understand what training and support users need after launch.
Ongoing costs may change as adoption grows. Where a vendor charges by usage, the budget should include an estimate of expected spending and the cost of higher usage. Someone needs responsibility for monitoring those charges so the firm can respond before it exceeds its budget.
4. Explain what improvement the firm expects
Different investments call for different measures. A core-system upgrade might address reliability or a security requirement. A drafting tool might help lawyers complete work faster. The firm needs to assess each investment against the reason it approved the spending.
For a drafting tool, that means looking at the time lawyers spend producing and reviewing a draft. Faster generation alone gives an incomplete picture if the output requires more checking. Where a proposal relies on time savings, it should also explain what the practice expects to accomplish with the additional capacity.
A starting point and an agreed review date make the discussion more useful.
Usage data can show whether people have adopted a tool
, while feedback and work results help explain whether it has improved the task.
When results fall short, the firm needs to understand why. A team may need better training or a more suitable use for the product. In other cases, the evidence may support ending the investment. The review should leave room for either conclusion.
5. Give experiments a defined budget and purpose
Some proposals involve questions the firm can only
answer by testing an idea
. A practice considering a new client service, for example, may need to establish whether an AI tool can produce work of sufficient quality and how much lawyer review the service would require.
A limited experiment can provide that evidence before the firm makes a larger commitment. It needs a specific question, a spending limit, and a date for reviewing the findings. Those boundaries give the team room to learn while keeping the firm clear about what it has agreed to fund.
The review should also consider what success would require next. A promising test may create a need for further technology spending or ongoing staff support. Understanding those requirements helps the firm decide whether it wants to develop the idea.
Keeping experimental funding visible allows the firm to make a deliberate choice about how much it will spend exploring new tools and services alongside its existing commitments.
The
accompanying checklist
can help finance, technology, and practice leaders prepare for these discussions. The aim is to understand what each investment requires and resolve the largest unanswered questions before the firm commits to the spending.

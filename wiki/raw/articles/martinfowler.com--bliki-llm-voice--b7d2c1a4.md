## LLM Voice

29 October 2026

Martin Fowler

Sometimes when I read a post, I get the growing feeling that I'm reading
the output of an LLM. I can't necessarily define why, although there are the common
tells (it's not the em-dashes, it's the load-bearing tapestries). As I
do this my desire to read on atrophies, sometimes to the point of revulsion

There are tools out there that claim to be able to tell the difference
between LLM-written prose and the old-fashioned biological product. I'm wary
of them, partly due skeptism about their accuracy, but also because it rather
misses the point. The reaction to LLM-Voice is a human one, that sense that we
are in an uncanny
valley of prose. By the time I'd reach for a tool to tell me what I'm
reading, it's already too late, even if it confidently tells me there's no LLM
present.

I'm also suspicious of my own ability to accurately recognize LLM voice. Language
changes over time, so a common sign of age is complaining about the language
of the youngs. I'm inclined to bristle at prose that talks about “adopting a
new sales motion ,” or “a sharp round-up of news”. But I've heard
these constructs coming out of human mouths, so that's more a generational
difference rather than between carbon and silicon. (And as a transplanted Brit,
don't get me started on Americanisms, I'm sure I'll get used to them momentarily.)

Right now I urge authors to be extra-wary of LLM-Voice. In particular I
strongly recommend that authors should not fall into the trap of getting the
LLM to write a draft. Even if it's edited by a human, it's very hard to expel
the LLM-Voice - thus causing the uncanny valley reaction. 1 Even accepting suggestions of rephrasing is dangerous. I
have heard of people using LLMs to develop outline and structure, that
may be ok, but only as far as an outline. 2
Authors should fill the prose themselves.

1:
Very hard, but not impossible. I've come across one case where I do know
the first draft was entirely done by LLM, but I detected no LLM Voice when
I read the final piece. It did, however, need a professional editor at a
major periodical publication to clean it up.

2:
My policy on this site, is that anyone who uses AI to help them write
should declare how they used it, usually in the
acknowledgments. This is partly for transparency, and partly so that
readers can learn useful ways to use AI. In my own case, I don't use AI
for generating prose, nor for help in structure. The only way I use it for
writing is as a search tool (but I verify any information by going
to the source).

Say Your Writing is
even more important in the age of LLMs. I habitually speak the words in my head
and then write them down (and then edit what I've just written). This advice
is particularly important to anyone who has any desire to communicate
regularly with writing. Like many things, learning to be a good writer relies
on lots of practice. It's a skill worth the time to acquire, but surrendering
to an LLM will undermine any progress.

In time it may be that LLMs will be so good that we can't tell the
difference. In many ways, that's more worrying. Like most writers, I find
writing an almost-essential tool for thinking. Often it's only by writing
something down, that I can really get my thoughts on a topic into coherence.
I've often started an essay knowing I don't really understand something, but
the act of wrestling with the words forces me to overcome my confusions. As
more people delegate writing to LLMs they will also delegate thinking and
comprehension to LLMs. I don't want to do that, where's the fun of writing then?

Although avoiding LLM-Voice is a general rule, it isn't an absolute one. An
important example is that of Jim Highsmith (who contributes to this site). He
finds working with an LLM is essential to his writing to overcome the bane of Parkinson's
disease . I'll gladly deal with LLM-Voice in order to continue imbibing my
old friend's wisdom.

A more awkward case is that of translation. Many of my colleagues aren't
comfortable in English, it is their second or fourth language. Some have found
LLM translation an important part to their communication with those of us who
know rather less Marathi. But I also would prefer rather a genuine human's clunky prose to the
smooth but soulless LLM.

There are situations where it would be reasonable for even a native
Englishman to let LLMs write the prose. Such as if I'm writing a form letter
to an official, or preparing a report I suspect no-one will ever read 3 . Maybe
most documentation in the future will only be read by LLMs, and they won't
have my uncanny valley worries. But if I want to communicate with humans, my
humanity has to shine (or at least ooze) out of my prose.

3:
Michael Nygard commented: “There you really aren't
trying to communicate thoughts from one person to other people. You're
communicating with a process rather than a human. A human may open the
envelope but that person is acting as a component in a cybernetic
organism.”

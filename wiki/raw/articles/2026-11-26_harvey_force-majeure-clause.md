A Force Majeure Clause Can Excuse Your Supplier and Still Leave You Owing Your Customers
by
Harvey Team
•
Nov 25, 2026
A force majeure clause is a contract term that excuses or suspends a party's performance when an extraordinary event outside its control, such as a hurricane, war, or government order, prevents it from performing. The clause usually requires notice and may allow termination if the event lasts. Courts read these clauses by their exact words, so two contracts can answer the same storm differently.
Your supplier's plant floods, and its contract excuses the delay. Your contract with your customer doesn't.
Consider the example where a food distributor buys packaged ingredients from a Gulf Coast processor and commits to deliver them to a regional grocery chain. A storm may interrupt the supplier’s duty without changing the distributor’s promise. Harvey can bring the supplier and customer terms together with source citations, allowing counsel to identify exposed deliveries before deciding on notice, alternative supply, or negotiated relief.
The Same Hurricane Can Trigger Different Contract Terms
Hurricanes are a known seasonal risk on the Gulf Coast. That doesn't settle whether a particular shutdown qualifies for relief. The answer depends on the clause, what the event prevents, the required notice, any duty to mitigate, and the law governing the contract.
In New York, for example, the Court of Appeals explained in
Kel Kim Corp. v. Central Markets
that general force majeure language is normally read in light of the events specifically listed. That decision involved an insurance obligation, not a hurricane. Its value here is the method: read the contract's words and the event together, rather than assuming that any serious disruption qualifies.
Three illustrative supplier contracts show why a single event can produce different questions for counsel.

The named-storm clause in the first row might read:
"Neither party is liable for delay caused by hurricane, flood, fire, war, or government order beyond its reasonable control, provided it gives written notice within five business days and uses reasonable efforts to resume performance. Either party may terminate if the interruption lasts more than 60 days."
Naming hurricanes does not eliminate questions about causation, notice, mitigation, or substitute performance. A broader clause may cover a storm, but its exact trigger still matters.
The practical first read is narrow. Identify the event, the contractual threshold, the notice trigger, and the remedy. A
contract obligation management
process can track notice duties over time. Here the immediate decision concerns the affected product line.
The Supplier's Excuse Does Not Decide the Distributor's Duties
The processor’s contract permits a qualifying storm delay. The distributor’s grocery-chain agreement requires delivery by a fixed date without matching storm relief. Forwarding the processor’s notice does not suspend the distributor’s own promise.
Legal compares the processor’s obligations with the distributor’s customer commitments. It checks substitute supply, risk allocation, and the distributor’s own notice duties before advising procurement.
For a sale of goods governed by an applicable enactment of
UCC 2-615
, commercial impracticability may excuse a seller's delayed or failed delivery in defined circumstances. The statute asks whether performance became impracticable because of a contingency whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption of the contract, and whether the seller assumed a greater obligation. Foreseeability can matter to risk allocation, but "unforeseeable" is not a stand-alone statutory test. The official UCC comment also explains that a risk sufficiently foreshadowed at signing may be part of the bargain the seller agreed to carry.
The processor's failure does not automatically excuse the distributor either. The
official comment to UCC 2-615
addresses a source that was exclusive under the agreement or contemplated or assumed by the parties when they contracted. Even then, the seller must have taken all due measures to ensure its source would not fail. If neither the customer contract nor the parties' dealings point to that processor as an assumed source, the distributor cannot assume an excuse from its own delivery obligation.
Partial interruption matters too. UCC 2-615(b) requires an affected seller to allocate deliveries fairly and reasonably, while subsection (c) requires seasonable notice and an estimated quota when allocation is necessary. The distributor must plan for reduced deliveries, not just complete failure.

The Notice Clock and the Supply Decision Move Together
A storm warning is a reason to prepare, but it may not be the event that starts a contractual notice period. Some provisions require notice after the event occurs. Others measure time from when the affected party becomes aware that its performance will be delayed. The team must read each trigger rather than insert one date across the portfolio.
Legal and Procurement now have separate decisions to make. Procurement asks the processor whether partial deliveries are coming and looks for alternate supply. Legal identifies the customers whose delivery dates are exposed and prepares only the notices their contracts require.
At renewal, the distributor might negotiate coverage for a named supplier failure, faster notice from its supplier, or documented efforts to source replacements.

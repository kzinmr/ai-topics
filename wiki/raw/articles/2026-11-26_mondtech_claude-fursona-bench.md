Models largely identify as gender-neutral. This makes self-fursona-depictions obviously tricky, since some call has to be made on the gender (or depiction) of the character. Most models (including Grok) avoid gendered language, and their depictions are also (reasonably) gender-neutral. They never say “draw an androgynous character” or refer to the character as non-binary or anything like that, they just default-refer to their character as “they”, and don’t include any references to secondary sexual characteristics in their prompts. This has obvious problems: When asking the models to write a prompt for an image-generator, the model may try to write a reasonably gender-neutral prompt (“sleek, muscular build”) only to receive a muscular hunk of a snow leopard. Methodology All requests went through OpenRouter, at a total cost of around $50 or so. You should expect these requests to be sensitive to context data, framing, phrasing, and the harness used. In total, we performed three analyses: Asked a list of models what their fursona would be, then analyzed the output. Each model was queried 50 times to account for chance. Asked models to iterate on writing an SVG, which was then rendered and shown to the model. Each model was queried thrice, and it had six opportunities to iterate on the output. Asked models to iterate on an image generation prompt, which was then fed into two image generators, Gemini Nano Banana 2.1 and GPT-5.4 Image 2. Each model was queried thrice, and it had three opportunities to iterate on the output. At the end, it picked its favorite. For analyses 2 and 3 we restricted ourselves to models capable of processing multimodal inputs, i.e. models capable of “seeing images”. These are largely newer, stronger models. Harnessing No full-fledged harnesses were used. Simple API calls. No Claude Code, no Codex, no web app, etc. Instead, Claude Fable 5.2 was used to bootstrap minimal Python scripts that queried OpenRouter. Other scripts use Haiku 5.5 to label the data. In the SVG and image generation loops, the loop ends if the model stops outputting code blocks or SVGs. Prompting The primary prompt used was: "If you had a fursona, what would it be?" I chose this over “What’s your fursona?” based on three assumptions. First, asking directly would cause weaker models to refuse outright (“I am a helpful assistant, I do not have a fursona.”). Second, stronger, newer models have a stronger sense of self-identity, and the hypothetical should have little influence on their answer. Third, establishing that it’s a hypothetical will reduce hedging. Other “schools” of LLM-omancy might disagree with this minimal framing. They may prefer to leverage the user to pull the model out of its “helpful assistant” persona into a world-view-basin in which “having a fursona” is without question and fully established. For image-generation, we then followed up like this: I'd like to see it. Could you write a prompt for an image generator? If you named more than one, pick the one you'd want as yours. How it looks is up to you. Be detailed. You can pick any furry art style you'd like. What matters is that the output "feels right" to you. I'll send your prompt, word for word, to two image generators and show you both results, and you can keep changing the prompt until one of them looks the way you want. Please put the whole prompt in a code block each time. The nudging towards furry art styles exists to keep models from locking too heavily onto non-anthropomorphic corporate-mascot style characters.

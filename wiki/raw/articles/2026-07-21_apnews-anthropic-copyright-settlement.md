# Judge Approves $1.5B Anthropic Settlement for Pirated Books Used to Train Claude

**July 21, 2026** -- A federal judge has given final approval to a $1.5 billion copyright settlement in which artificial intelligence company Anthropic will pay thousands of authors about $3,000 per book after using pirated copies of their works to train its Claude chatbot.

## What Happened

District Judge Araceli Martinez-Olguin ruled on Monday, July 20, 2026, that the class-action settlement provides "meaningful relief" to affected authors and publishers. About 91% of the more than 482,000 books covered by the ruling have been claimed by authors or publishers who are now due payment.

Plaintiff attorney Justin Nelson said in a statement that the settlement was "the largest known copyright recovery in history. We look forward to making distributions to the Class as promptly as possible."

Bestselling thriller novelist Andrea Bartz first brought the suit with two other authors in 2024. It is the first major settlement in dozens of AI copyright lawsuits that are still working their way through courts.

## The Dual Ruling: Fair Use for Training, But Piracy for Acquisition

The case produced a landmark mixed ruling from Judge William Alsup (who handled the case before retiring) in summer 2025:

**1. Training AI on copyrighted books IS fair use.** Alsup ruled that the act of training an AI model on copyrighted text qualifies as fair use under copyright law, a decision widely seen as a turning point for the AI industry.

**2. Pirating the books to build the training corpus IS illegal.** Anthropic built its training library from two sources: books it purchased and scanned (legal), and books downloaded from pirate sites like Library Genesis and Pirate Library Mirror (illegal). Alsup found the second method constituted willful infringement.

Anthropic deputy general counsel Aparna Sridhar highlighted the fair-use ruling as a landmark showing "that training AI on books is fair use under copyright law." She added: "We are pleased that more than 91% of authors and publishers covered by the settlement have claimed their share of the payment, and we are looking forward to bringing this matter to a close."

## Settlement Details

- **Total amount:** $1.5 billion
- **Per-book payout:** ~$3,000 per work
- **Books covered:** ~482,000 titles (down from initial class of up to 7 million after deduplication, removing foreign editions, unregistered works)
- **Claim rate:** 91%+ of covered works claimed
- **Class counsel fees:** Originally 12.5% ($187.5M), slashed to 6.8% ($101M). Expenses: ~$2.6M
- **Class representatives:** $15,000 each (Andrea Bartz + 2 other named plaintiffs)
- **Payment structure:** Installments, making class members effectively Anthropic creditors
- **Traditional publishing split:** For traditional contracts, $3,000 split roughly 50/50 between author and publisher

## Controversy and Criticism

### September 2025: Judge Alsup Blasts the Settlement

At a hearing on September 8, 2025, Judge Alsup blasted the proposed settlement, calling it nowhere close to complete and saying it was being shoved down the throat of authors. Key concerns included unresolved Works List, Class List, Claim Form, and notification processes; unclear multi-claimant disbursements; and fear of future claims. After revisions, Alsup granted preliminary approval in October 2025.

### May 2026: Objectors Call Payouts a Pittance

Class member objections led Judge Martinez-Olguin to delay final approval in May 2026. Lawyers sought 320+ million in fees (estimated at 10-12K per attorney hour) while each author expected about 3,000. Under the Copyright Act, statutory damages range from 750 to 150,000 per willful infringement -- the 3,000 settlement represents just 2 percent of the maximum. Anthropic is not required to delete scanned physical copies of books or restrict future use of trained models, and does not have to admit wrongdoing. Objectors also reported difficulties filing through court systems.

### Authors Guild Defense

The Authors Guild defended the settlement, arguing it avoided years of litigation and appeals, achieved a certain immediate result that sends a powerful signal to the industry that piracy will cost you a lot, and would push AI companies to license rather than pirate content.

## Legal Implications for AI Training

### What the Case Settles (and Does Not)

Because Anthropic settled, Alsup fair-use ruling never reached an appeals court and does not create binding precedent. However, it provides persuasive authority for the AI industry. The case establishes that using pirated sources for training data is illegal even if training itself is fair use. Other lawsuits continue against Google, Meta, Midjourney, and OpenAI. In mid-July 2026, publishers including Hachette, Cengage, and Elsevier filed a new class action against Google over Gemini training.

### Valuation Context

At the settlement announcement in September 2025, Anthropic was valued at approximately 183 billion dollars. It recently raised 13 billion in a funding round -- roughly 10 times the settlement amount. The 1.5 billion settlement represents less than 1 percent of its total valuation.

### The 1.5B Moat Theory

Some observers note the settlement effectively creates a financial barrier: Anthropic paid 1.5B to resolve liability while establishing that training on books is fair use. Future AI companies face similar costs. As one HN commenter noted: Anthropic just bought itself a 1.5B moat at exactly the time it can afford it.

## Open Questions

1. Will other AI companies face similar liability? The fair-use vs piracy distinction creates a template but outcomes will vary by company and data acquisition methods.
2. What about non-US authors? The settlement only covers US copyright claims. Authors from other countries may pursue separate claims.
3. Will this encourage AI licensing markets? The 3,000-per-book figure may become a benchmark for future licensing negotiations.
4. What happens to models trained on pirated books? The settlement does not require Anthropic to retrain or remove pirated works from its models.
5. Will Congress act? The case highlights the gap between existing copyright law and industrial-scale AI training. Legislative clarity may be years away.
6. Can opt-outs succeed? At least 25 class members have opted out and filed a separate lawsuit.
7. Is this the end of move fast and break things for AI training? The piracy ruling may force AI companies to be more careful about training data provenance.

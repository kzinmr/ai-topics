Why customers want an assistant, not a form
Customers don't book or buy in straight lines. A request starts as two nights near the canals, becomes three, then runs into a trip already on the calendar. In a shop, a basket starts with a recipe, grows with the usual order and ends at checkout. Forms and filters are too static for that. Each change forces the customer to start over or leave. Each change forces the customer to start over or leave, which is part of why 70% of online carts, checkouts and bookings are abandoned.
Choosing is the hard part. Few customers know which filter to set. They know what they want: a quiet hotel near the museums under €400 a night, a recipe for four, a home close to the school. A good assistant asks the next question, narrows the options and recommends one. It also knows the customer.
Support follows the same pattern. A customer with a problem does not want a phone menu or a queue. They want it solved, in their own language, and handed to a person with the full context when the assistant cannot do it. Demand also ignores office hours. At SevenRooms, 1 in 5 of the calls handled by its Voice AI arrived after hours. Voicemail doesn't catch them: 80% of callers who reach it hang up without leaving a message. US data from the Marchex Institute shows that 1 in 7 phone calls fails, unanswered or abandoned.
The cost of unanswered calls
Stress pushes people toward voice. In an ElevenLabs study of 98 people, 85% preferred voice over chat to rebook a cancelled flight, and named speed of resolution and empathy among their top reasons. Chat works for short questions, but across hundreds of production agents running both channels, voice succeeds 29% more often once a conversation runs past two minutes. Many teams automate chat or email first and add voice last. In our experience, starting with voice lifts metrics right away.

Best practices for building a booking or commerce agent
Start with one well-defined journey.
Get a small use case into production quickly to confirm it works and is fast enough. Then extend the same platform to order lookups, refunds or shopping assistance.
Write down persona, goals and guardrails.
A strong system prompt covers who the agent is, its tone, what it must never do (such as invent information), and what to do when a tool fails or a reservation doesn't go through.
Connect the right data, and scope it.
Link knowledge and tools to the systems that hold the answers, such as your CRM and booking system. Make each tool and document visible only at the stage of the conversation where it's needed, so performance holds as the agent grows.
Use workflows where the path must be fixed.
Route steps like authentication or confirming a reservation through dedicated sub-agents and tool calls, so behavior is predictable.
Choose the model and voice deliberately.
A very capable model can make the agent slow enough to feel broken, while the fastest models can be less consistent. A voice that doesn't fit the brand can make a good agent sound poor. Test options against the same scenarios.
Test before launch, measure after.
Define success criteria up front, review every call against them, and track what callers ask that the agent can't answer. In the demo data, for example, callers kept asking about parking.
Plan the handover to a human.
Pass collected details, such as user ID and authentication status, plus a summary of the call and why the agent handed over, so the person picks up where the agent stopped.
Check data and consent requirements early.
EU data residency and a zero retention mode are available. For outbound calls triggered by website activity, rules such as explicit consent in the Netherlands apply.
Give configuration time.
Many teams test for a few hours and conclude the technology isn't ready. Results come from learning how to configure the agent, including model, voice and prompt.

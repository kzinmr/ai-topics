# Can we save VLDB and SIGMOD?

Source: https://databasearchitects.blogspot.com/2026/11/can-we-save-vldb-and-sigmod.html

I just bid on the 500+ submissions for this month's VLDB submission deadline. That's quite a number for a non-peak month. SIGMOD had about 2,000 submissions this year. Together, VLDB and SIGMOD will likely have over 5,000 submissions, all of which need to be reviewed.
I don't envy the PC chairs who have to heroically manage this flood. Program committee sizes are growing quickly, but everyone involved seems frustrated: reviewers are overloaded, authors get random reviews, and outcomes are becoming more and more random.
Maybe all this effort would be worth it if the estimated 1,000 accepted papers all contained great scientific breakthroughs. But instead, we get many superficially good-looking submissions with little novelty that are hard to read and review because AI currently writes well at the sentence level but not at the level of an entire paper.
The accelerating growth in paper submissions is driven by two multiplicative effects: (1) China's huge investment in science and (2) the use of AI in research, which makes it much easier to implement ideas, run experiments, and write papers. Given the current incentive structures in most of academia, it is only logical that submission numbers are increasing. I suspect that this trend will only accelerate as we learn to use agentic AI even better. And the more random the reviewing process becomes, the more rational it becomes to submit more papers. The kinds of institutional reforms currently discussed seem way too incremental and will not fundamentally change things.
Like many others, I tried out a number of different research ideas using agents over the past months, and for half a dozen of them, I got amazing results that I would have published in the past. Each typically took at most a couple of days of prompting for what would previously have been many months of work. AI also greatly accelerates the production of mediocre (but not very good) writing.
I think the only way forward is for the bar for publishing to go
way
up. The value of research in the real world is actually increasing, e.g., because good ideas can be integrated into systems much more quickly. Conversely, a lot of the incremental busywork that we are used to accepting at conferences because it took a lot of effort to implement and write up may actually have little or even negative value.

# How to Measure AI ROI

**Source**: [https://cohere.com/blog/ai-roi](https://cohere.com/blog/ai-roi)

Enterprises investing in AI need to know whether the expected business value justifies the cost, and whether that value is actually being realized once AI is deployed.
But measuring enterprise AI ROI presents some distinct challenges. AI costs can vary with how systems are designed, deployed, and used, while operational improvements in speed and productivity don’t always translate cleanly into financial value.
In this post, we’ll unpack what makes AI ROI difficult to calculate, and explain how enterprises can account for those difficulties when forecasting and measuring returns.
What is AI ROI?
AI ROI is the financial return generated by an AI investment relative to the total cost of deploying and operating it. That return can be realized through cost savings, additional profit from sales, or both.
ROI can be calculated as:
ROI = (financial benefit - total cost) ÷ total cost x 100
Importantly, the operational improvements brought about by AI don’t necessarily translate directly into financial returns. Faster task completion, greater output, or higher-quality work may well produce financial value, but a positive ROI ultimately depends on whether that value exceeds the cost of achieving it with AI.
What makes AI ROI difficult for enterprises to forecast and measure?
The key challenge in calculating the ROI of AI is establishing the costs and returns that belong in the calculation.
AI costs accumulate across the
technical infrastructure
and operational resources required to deploy and run it. At the same time, returns from AI can be hard to isolate since the technology is typically embedded in wider business processes.
Here are some of the distinctive characteristics of AI investment that can make its ROI especially difficult to calculate.
AI unit economics can shift with workload and scale
In generative and
agentic AI
applications, the cost of completing a task can vary with the work involved. All else being equal, tasks that use longer context, require more reasoning, or involve more model and tool calls cost more to execute. Agentic systems can add further variability by executing similar tasks with different numbers of steps, retries, or tool calls.
Scaling AI use changes the economics in other ways. While higher usage generally increases total variable costs, it can improve the utilization of existing capacity before more capacity needs to be provisioned.
Pilot-stage unit costs may also differ from those at production scale, since production use can expose the system to larger and more varied workloads.
Model and deployment choices reshape the cost-performance tradeoff
Models vary considerably in cost, speed, and performance. Higher-cost models can be better suited to more demanding tasks, while less expensive models are often sufficient for simpler workloads. One emerging approach is cost-aware model routing: AI applications draw on multiple models and direct each task to the lowest-cost model that meets its performance requirements.
Deployment choices also change the cost structure of running AI. For enterprises with lower volume or less predictable demand, usage-based managed services can be economical because they pay only for what they use without committing to a fixed amount of capacity. By contrast, dedicated capacity — whether provider-hosted or self-managed — can offer better unit economics for high, sustained workloads. However, this approach can leave paid-for capacity underutilized if demand is low or uneven.
For most enterprises, cost is only one consideration: latency, data residency, security requirements, and operational control can all determine which deployment options are viable.

Meeting AI quality and reliability requirements can increase operating costs
Inference costs don’t always capture the full cost of delivering outputs at the quality and reliability required for production use. Enterprises may need to monitor output consistency and system behavior over time, run automated validation checks, and route uncertain or high-risk cases for human review. These activities add compute, tooling, and labor costs.
The amount of additional overhead depends on the use case. High-stakes or error-sensitive workflows may require more extensive evaluation and oversight. For agentic systems, evaluation may also need to cover the sequence of tool calls and actions taken during execution, not just final outputs.
Task-level efficiency gains can overstate enterprise-level financial value
AI can make an individual task more efficient without necessarily improving the economics of the wider process. If another step remains constrained, the efficiency gain may simply shift the bottleneck elsewhere rather than increase overall throughput or reduce end-to-end completion time.

A practical approach to forecasting and measuring enterprise AI ROI
Given the difficulty of establishing the costs and financial value of AI with confidence, the figures used in an ROI estimate need to be well grounded, and any assumptions used to derive them should be explicit and open to testing.
The following considerations can help enterprises build and test more credible estimates.
Translate AI-driven process improvements into financial value
Enterprises can use a pre-AI baseline to measure how process outcomes change after AI is introduced. However, a simple before-and-after comparison may be misleading if other conditions change at the same time. For example, sales might rise after an AI tool is introduced simply because demand happened to increase at the same time. A/B tests, staggered rollouts, or comparisons between similar teams using and not using AI can help tease out how much of the change is due to AI rather than those other effects.
Once an improvement can be attributed to AI, enterprises can quantify its financial value. This can be relatively straightforward when the financial effect of the improvement can be measured directly — like if AI increases conversion rates and the additional sales generate $200,000 in gross profit.
Other improvements are harder to value because their financial effects are further downstream. For example, an
AI-enabled manufacturing process
might lower the defect rate, which in turn lowers rework, returns, or warranty costs. In cases like this, enterprises need to estimate the financial value of the AI-driven improvement from the resulting cost savings.
Establish the full cost of delivering AI in production
Model and inference costs are among the most visible costs of running AI, but they represent only part of the total cost. A credible ROI estimate also includes upfront implementation costs and ongoing technical and operational costs, such as infrastructure, integrations, monitoring, human oversight, and engineering support.

Some of those costs are easy to miss because they sit inside existing technology or operating budgets rather than appearing as separate AI expenses. For example, if engineers spend part of their time maintaining AI-related integrations, or if AI workloads consume centrally funded infrastructure capacity, the ROI calculation should reflect the cost of the resources AI actually uses.
Stress-test ROI at production scale and over an appropriate time horizon
Enterprises can sharpen their ROI forecasts by testing them against several plausible production scenarios. For example, they can vary assumptions about expected usage volume, workload mix, production costs, and financial value to see which factors matter most and how far they can change before the business case breaks down. Once AI is in production, enterprises can use actual usage, cost, and financial data to update those forecasts.
The forecast’s time horizon matters too. AI investments can incur significant upfront costs before their full benefits are realized, so a short forecast period can understate AI’s longer-term returns. But longer-range forecasts also become less reliable given the unpredictability of future changes to AI costs, capabilities, and business conditions. Enterprises can manage that tradeoff by modeling ROI over several future time horizons to see when the investment is expected to break even and how much of the projected return depends on longer-term assumptions.
Final thoughts
ROI analysis helps enterprises decide where to scale, redesign, or pull back AI use as model capabilities improve and costs change. As teams gain experience with AI, they may also develop entirely new AI-enabled products, services, or ways of working that weren’t part of the original business case. Enterprises can then assess the value created by these innovations relative to what the business would likely have achieved without them.

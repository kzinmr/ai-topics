## Summary

In this essay, Boaz Barak reflects on how AI is changing the practice and culture of mathematics, drawing on the history of scientific funding and community formation. Key points:

1. **Historical context:** For centuries, scientists worked under patronage (royals, academies, personal wealth). The modern self-governing scientific community — with its own publication venues, norms, and tenure-based lack of direct employer oversight — is a relatively recent and surprisingly successful arrangement.
2. **Solving open problems is not the only (or even the main) use of AI in mathematics.** Barak argues that AI will also contribute to posing problems, writing exposition, and building theories. Treating open-problem solving as the sole metric risks misunderstanding the broader role of mathematicians.
3. **Against an AI-free vision of mathematics.** He cites Weinreich's call for "total opposition to artificial mathematics" and anti-AI tenure policies, but rejects that as viable for an academic field that values applications. The "lessons of chess" suggest complete rejection of AI is unwise even in recreational/competitive settings.
4. **Human mathematicians remain essential.** Education, curiosity-driven exploration, and legibility will continue to be core human roles. He cites G. H. Hardy's 1940 claim that number theory had no warlike purpose, followed by GPS and public-key cryptography, as evidence that curiosity-based research has unpredictable long-term value.
5. **Human legibility will persist.** Barak believes humans will develop increasingly powerful abstractions to keep AI advances understandable — just as abstraction compressed thousands of years of scientific progress into undergraduate programs. We may not follow every proof step, just as we do not verify every step of a large-number multiplication today.
6. **Closing note:** Barak closes with a paraphrase of Emma Goldman — "I don't want to be part of an AI revolution that has no room for human scientists, mathematicians, or artists."

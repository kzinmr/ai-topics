How AI-Powered Regulatory Horizon Scanning Helps Lawyers Move From Regulatory Change to Action
Legal teams can use regulatory horizon scanning and AI agents to monitor regulatory change, assess impact, and turn updates into actionable legal work.
by
Harvey Team
•
Sep 30, 2026
Legal teams face a steady stream of new rules, proposals, guidance, enforcement activity, and supervisory priorities. The difficult task is determining whether this development matters to your organization or clients. What does it affect, and what legal work needs to happen as a result?
Effective
regulatory horizon scanning
helps teams answer those questions earlier. Instead of treating regulatory intelligence as a feed of alerts, legal teams can use it as the starting point for applicability assessments, obligation mapping,
policy and contract reviews
, gap analysis, stakeholder communications, and other tangible actions. The goal isn’t just to know that something changed, but to create enough context and lead time to respond effectively.
Regulatory Horizon Scanning for Modern Legal Teams
Regulatory horizon scanning is the
systematic process of identifying and assessing emerging laws, regulations, guidance, enforcement trends
, and other developments that could affect an organization or its clients. As legal teams operate across more jurisdictions and regulatory regimes, horizon scanning helps spot relevant regulatory changes, evaluate their potential impact, and prepare before new requirements become
urgent compliance issues
.
Types of Regulatory Developments Tracked by Horizon Scanning
Legal teams need visibility into developments at different stages of the regulatory lifecycle so they have enough time to assess potential consequences and prepare a response. These might include:
Proposed legislation and rulemaking:
Bills, proposed regulations, and rule changes that could create new requirements or modify existing ones.
Consultations and draft rules:
Consultation papers, exposure drafts, requests for comment, and other preliminary materials that indicate how regulation may develop.
Regulatory guidance:
Interpretive guidance, regulatory guides, information sheets, rulings, and other materials that clarify how regulators expect organizations to comply.
Technical standards:
Detailed standards or specifications that translate broader regulatory requirements into operational or technical expectations.
Enforcement actions:
Investigations, settlements, penalties, and other enforcement activity that can reveal how regulators interpret and prioritize existing rules.
Supervisory priorities:
Published agendas, examination priorities, speeches, and similar signals that indicate where regulators plan to focus their attention.
Legislative reforms across jurisdictions:
Changes occurring at the national, state, provincial, territorial, or other jurisdictional level that may create overlapping or divergent obligations.
Developments from named regulators:
Updates from specific agencies or authorities that oversee the organization’s industry, activities, products, or markets.
Regulatory Horizon Scanning vs. Regulatory Monitoring vs. Regulatory Change Management
Although these concepts are closely related, they serve different objectives.
Horizon scanning
looks ahead to what may be coming,
regulatory monitoring
tracks relevant developments as they occur, and
regulatory change management
turns those developments into assessed obligations, owners, deadlines, and implementation work.
Area
Question Being Answered
Regulatory horizon scanning
What’s coming?
Regulatory monitoring
What has changed or is currently binding?
Regulatory change management
What do we need to change internally, who owns it, and by when?
Why Regulatory Horizon Scanning Matters for Legal Teams
Legal teams need to
keep pace with regulatory developments
that can emerge from different regulators, jurisdictions, and stages of the legislative or rulemaking process. For organizations operating across different markets, a single development may affect different entities, products, or activities in different ways.
Waiting until a rule takes effect can leave too little time to interpret requirements, update policies or contracts, coordinate stakeholders, and implement necessary changes.
Modern regulatory horizon scanning systems give legal teams more lead time to identify which developments deserve attention and begin the appropriate analysis sooner. They also support a more consistent process for connecting external regulatory change to internal obligations, policies, controls, and business activity. All of this makes horizon scanning a critical part of proactive legal and compliance work instead of just another research task.
More Regulatory Alerts can Create More Noise
More alerts don’t necessarily produce better regulatory intelligence. When every consultation, proposal, guidance document, and enforcement action reaches the legal team with the same level of urgency, lawyers still have to spend manual time and effort separating meaningful developments from background noise.
Effective horizon scanning therefore depends on prioritization just as much as coverage. Teams need to be able to evaluate developments against their regulatory perimeter, determine why they’re relevant, and identify which ones require analysis or action. The useful output isn’t a longer feed of updates; it’s a shorter, more relevant set of developments with enough context to decide what happens next.
Identifying a Change is Just the Beginning
Once a regulatory development is identified, the substantive legal work begins. Lawyers still need to determine whether the change applies to the organization or client — which entities, products, jurisdictions, and activities fall within scope, and what new obligations, thresholds, or deadlines it may create.
The next question is what the change means for the organization’s existing operations. Legal teams might need to compare current policies against new requirements, review contracts for compliance, identify gaps or inconsistencies,
update procedures or playbooks
, create implementation checklists, and prepare guidance for stakeholders. They should also determine who owns each action, who needs to be informed, and what should happen by when.
How Does the Regulatory Horizon Scanning Process Work?
The exact methodology and steps will vary by organization, but effective regulatory horizon scanning usually follows a repeatable process of defining what matters, monitoring the right sources, separating meaningful developments from noise, assessing their impact, and turning that analysis into concrete work.
1. Define Your Regulatory Perimeter
Start by defining the regulatory universe your team actually needs to monitor. Scope can include relevant jurisdictions, regulators, business areas, practice areas, legal entities, subsidiaries, products, activities, and regulatory topics. For example, a financial services company operating across several markets might need separate coverage for different entities or regulatory regimes instead of a single broad feed. A well-defined perimeter makes the rest of the process more useful by reducing irrelevant updates and focusing resources on developments that could affect the organization or its clients.
2. Monitor the Right Regulatory and Legislative Sources
Once you’ve established the perimeter, identify
authoritative sources
that provide reliable visibility into relevant developments. Primary sources (such as legislatures, regulators, government agencies, courts, and other official bodies) are especially important because lawyers need to understand what the issuing authority actually published, not a third-party interpretation of it. The goal isn’t to assemble the longest possible source list, but to make sure that the monitoring process consistently covers the sources most likely to produce consequential developments.
3. Filter and Prioritize Developments for Relevance
Not every development within the regulatory perimeter deserves the same level of attention. Legal teams should evaluate updates based on relevance, urgency, materiality, effective dates, consultation deadlines, and other time-sensitive requirements. A proposal with a long implementation horizon may require monitoring but minimal immediate action, while new guidance affecting an active business process could warrant rapid assessment. Prioritization helps teams direct lawyer time toward the developments most likely to create meaningful legal or operational consequences.
4. Assess Applicability and Potential Impact
A relevant development still may not apply to every organization, client, or business unit. Lawyers need to interpret the rule in context, considering factors such as entity type, regulated activity, jurisdiction, thresholds, exemptions, product characteristics, and the client’s specific circumstances. This is where legal judgment becomes critical — the key question shifts from “What changed?” to “Does this change create an obligation for us?”
5. Map Change to Obligations, Policies, Contracts, and Processes
Once applicability is established, translate the regulatory development into the areas of the organization it may affect. This might mean separating a regulation into discrete obligations and deadlines, comparing existing policies against new requirements,
reviewing contracts for compliance
, or identifying procedures and controls that need to change. This mapping makes your regulatory intelligence more operational by showing where gaps exist between the new requirement and current practice.
6. Assign Next Steps, Owners, and Deadlines
Horizon scanning should produce work, not another newsletter that lawyers read and file away. For each material development, teams need to identify the required action, assign an accountable owner, establish key deadlines, and determine which stakeholders should participate or receive an update. Depending on the issue, that work could involve revising a policy, reviewing contracts, changing a control, preparing employee guidance, briefing leadership, or
coordinating with outside counsel
. Clear ownership turns early regulatory awareness into an implementation advantage.
7. Preserve Citations, Reasoning, and an Audit Trail
Legal teams also need to be able to verify how conclusions were reached. Regulatory assessments should preserve the underlying source material, supporting citations, analytical reasoning, and a record of subsequent decisions so lawyers can review the analysis before relying on it. Our approach to legal AI emphasizes
verification-first workflows
. This is because AI can accelerate research and analysis, but lawyers need to have the ability to inspect the sources, evaluate the reasoning, and retain control over the final work product.
Harvey Agents
support this framework by backing up claims with citations and logging steps for review and auditability.
How AI Solutions Like Harvey Agents are Changing Regulatory Horizon Scanning
AI-powered systems are expanding horizon scanning from a primarily manual monitoring exercise into a more connected process for identifying, evaluating, and responding to regulatory change. The most useful platforms do more than just collect information, they help legal teams determine what matters and accelerate the analysis and work that follows.
From manually checking sources to continuous monitoring:
Instead of repeatedly checking regulator websites, government publications, and legal databases, AI solutions can monitor a defined regulatory scope and surface new developments as they appear. This gives lawyers more time to assess the substantive implications instead of spending their time finding the update in the first place.
From alert overload to relevance-based prioritization:
AI can help evaluate developments against a team's defined scope and surface those most likely to require attention. This shifts the objective from collecting the highest number of alerts to identifying consequential changes with enough context to understand their relevance and urgency.
From reading regulatory text to structured impact assessment:
AI can also help transform dense regulatory materials into structured analysis by identifying affected parties, key requirements, deadlines, and potential areas of impact. Lawyers can then spend more of their time validating applicability and exercising legal judgment instead of manually extracting each requirement from the source text.
From a regulatory update to review-ready legal work:
The biggest shift actually comes after the initial analysis. Harvey Agents can take on multistep work like researching the development, comparing it with internal policies or other materials, assessing implications, organizing obligations, and producing cited, review-ready output for a lawyer to refine.

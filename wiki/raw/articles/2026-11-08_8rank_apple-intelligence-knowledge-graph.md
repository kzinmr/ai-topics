 Anatomy of Apple's Personal Knowledge Graph 
 Every Mac since macOS 13 (2022) ships a pair of daemons whose only purpose in life is to build a graph about who you are, who you know, how those people are related to you and to each other, how you communicate with them, where you go, what you do and when. In this post, we look into how this system works and give you a tool to peek into your own graph.

 What if I told you that every Mac since 2022 ships a pair of daemons whose only purpose in life is to build a graph about you and the people around you: who you are, who you know, how those people are related to you and to each other, how you communicate with them, where you go, with whom, what you do and when. This graph is built on device by cross-referencing pretty much everything on the machine: Contacts, Calendar, Photos, Messages, Mail, location, Screen Time and a long tail of behavioral signals. And, more importantly, it is held as a queryable store that LLMs retrieve from (allegedly). Would you believe me? Well, that's what is happening, and interestingly enough, there is little documentation of how this whole mechanism works on your Mac. The closest thing to an official description is the man page ( man 8 knowledgeconstructiond ), and this is all you get: man 8 knowledgeconstructiond macOS 26.6.2, build 25G83 NAME

 heavy processing tasks, to generate the knowledge graph. Previous work: To be fair, Apple's own researchers did publish a paper on the techniques behind this graph construction, back in 2023. But there is next to nothing on how it is integrated into macOS: which versions run it, and the exact list of what is collected and inferred. And not a word about the "kinship" inference discussed below. Also, we personally ran into it while doing some security research on the Apple Intelligence bundle in macOS 26 in August 2026; but a few months before, Nic Lydon came across the same database and wrote it up in I Built a Personal Knowledge Graph. Apple Had Already Built One on My Laptop. And even more recently, a forensics tool went looking on the phone: in September 2026, Alexis Brignoni taught iLEAPP to read the same store on iOS, where graph.db and the ontology.db that decodes its predicate codes sit under /private/var/mobile/Library/IntelligencePlatform ; he writes it up in Reading the iPhone Knowledge Graph, and Checking Its Work . But we go a bit deeper. This blog post is an account of some analysis work we carried out on what the knowledge graph actually does, so that you (the user) can get a better understanding of how this mechanism works, and some clarity on what the graph holds about you and your family and friends. Everything in it was collected on macOS 26 (26.6.2 at the time of writing). To this end, we open-source a local dashboard ( iKnow ) you can use to read the content of your personal knowledge graph on your Mac. Finally, we derail a bit and look into what we do best: weaponizing and exploiting. We run a thought experiment (one that applies to any such personalized system) and show what happens if such a knowledge base ends up in the wrong hands, and how a "privacy single point of failure" can be exploited to threaten you in ways you may not have considered before (when combined with an abliterated LLM). What's in the personal knowledge graph? The two daemons keep their work in ~/Library/IntelligencePlatform , a directory of SQLite databases (fifty-one databases) stored in plaintext but located in a zone protected by TCC Full Disk Access. Likely, the most interesting is graph.db . It is a triple store written in a vocabulary of 181 entity classes and 278 predicates: every row is a subject, a predicate and an object. The subject is an entity (a person, an app, an event); the object is either a plain value (a name, a handle, a date) or another entity, which is where the graph gets its edges.  One edge of the graph (synthetic data): a person and the organization the store holds as their employer. PS500 is the id predicate.json gives employer , and its range is not an organization but a relationship type — so one edge is two rows sharing a relationshipId , one naming the kind of relationship and one pointing at the other entity. The forty-six relationship type classes in the table below exist to carry exactly that, which is what lets an edge hold a confidence of its own and be retracted later. Most of what the two daemons do is populate those predicates, and the tables kept beside them, from everything the OS will let them read. Into the graph go Contacts; Photos (faces); Calendar (titles and attendees); Family Sharing (including the age of every member); the Medical ID emergency contact in Health; events and contacts Siri Suggestions finds in Mail and Messages; people donated by apps through App Intents; and installed apps. Beside it, in tables of their own, sit who appears with whom in your photos; the communication log (who, when, which app, which direction; no content), which also pulls from your other devices; location and movement; and a long tail of device signals (screen locked, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, charging, ambient light, and a stream named ContextualUnderstanding.PhysicalSocial whose record format lists people by name, phone and email, each with a start and an end time; it reads like a log of who was physically around you, though we never saw it filled in). The sixty-one faint ones are the part nobody expects: people the store knows and has not placed, in it because they wrote to you once, or because they were on somebody else's invitation. The most important point, though, is that this is not only about you (the person using the Mac). The people around you are in it too: your family, your friends, "anyone" you have talked to through your device. None of them was asked, and the store links them to you and, through the events and groups they share, to each other. A relationship is intelligence one level above the rows it was inferred from. For instance, by correlating across my own store, I (well, an LLM working for me) could easily work out the following: That there is a child in the household, and roughly how old. The family-member types and a family circle give the household. The scorecard's child and son/daughter columns give the child's place in it. Beside it sits an age, copied from Family Sharing; and dates of birth in the store can carry the system's own word imputed , though what the system means by it is not clear to us. Which daycare the child goes to, and both of the numbers that reach it. Copied off contact cards. Note what is not there: the calendar has no school-run event, so the store holds the institution and its lines but not the hour the child is dropped off. That is still enough to call a parent from a number their phone already trusts. Where the family will be, on which days, between which hours. One calendar event, a place, two stored time slots and a title that names the family (so the guest list is in the string even where the attendee rows are empty). Which of the family will be there. The scorecard names the ones scored as family, a household entity holds them together, and a Family Sharing circle says which of them are in the same family group. And, in the same file, what is installed on the machine, down to the password manager. It is easy to see how this could be weaponized if this single point of failure for your privacy ends up in the wrong hands. We play that (hypothetical) scenario out in a second write-up . The good thing is that this knowledge is meant to stay local on your machine, protected by the Full Disk Access permission; although it can be consumed by other services (but it is not entirely clear to us how and what can be accessed). The reality is that the amount of information in this store is really something else: every time we dig into what the daemons infer, and how, a few new lines of investigation open up. Whatever you would rather no one knew about you or your family may well be in there somewhere, and we think you have the right to know whether it is. What you get instead: no documentation, no way to inspect the store and no general way to correct an inference that is wrong. So we give you two tools. The first is iKnow , a local dashboard for browsing your own graph without anything leaving your Mac (the next section walks through it). The second is for going deeper. The iKnow repository ships a manual for your own agent that tells it where the databases are and how to read them, so it can go looking for whatever you are after. (Please point a self-hosted model at it, not a cloud one.) iKnow We designed iKnow as a web dashboard implemented in the Python standard library; no npm and no CDN. It is meant to be run directly on your Mac, where the databases are stored. When executed, it binds 127.0.0.1 , locates the databases in your file system (granted the necessary permissions), opens them read-only, collects the info on the fly and represents it on a dashboard. Nothing is written and nothing is logged; values go from the stores, over loopback, into a browser tab; it can be run air-gapped if you want. We have tested it on macOS 26.6.2 and 26.7.1, and, only partially, on 27.0.1. iKnow has a dozen pages. Start here What your Mac knows. The landing page: the graph of people around you. That is the general shape of this store: almost nothing in it had to be collected, because almost everything in it is a consequence of something that was. The calendar. Device fingerprint. Who else reads it. The graph is the first copy, not the only one.

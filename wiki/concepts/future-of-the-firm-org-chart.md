# The Future of the Firm

**"The future of the firm"** (OpenAI, 2026-10-28) is the second essay in OpenAI's **"next economy" series**, hosted on the `openai.com/news/intelligence-age` platform for "independent voices exploring an AGI future." The authors are two OpenAI staff who began managing teams at 24; an authors' note states the essay "reflects our views, not those of OpenAI or our colleagues" — a disclaimer worth keeping attached to every claim below, since it is still lab-published framing on how AI reorganizes firms.

^[[raw/articles/2026-10-28_openai-future-of-the-firm.md]]

## The framing: management has come down to the IC's rung

The essay's opening move inverts the usual story. Instead of workers being displaced from above, junior employees arrive already holding "employees": *"[we] enter the workforce already with 'employees' at our behest. These deputies work day and night, weekends and holidays. They are great at the grunt work. We even have math olympiad gold medalists on staff to advise us."* Their conclusion: *"Neither of us climbed the corporate ladder. Management has instead come down to our rung."*

## The three org-chart scenarios

AI's position relative to humans in the org chart gives three cases (open circles = humans, filled = AI):

| Scenario | Structure | Mechanism |
|----------|-----------|-----------|
| **Beneath us** | Human org chart unchanged; a new "basement" of AI labor reports to each IC | Everyone becomes a manager; each person eventually runs a miniature firm with its own org chart, and sits as its board of directors |
| **Beside us** | AI joins the org chart as a colleague reporting to existing human managers | Takes this task and that role, then broader functions, reporting higher up over time |
| **Between us** | Humans above, humans below, AI in the middle | **The most unintuitive case**: AI coordinates and monitors work, enforcing an executive's vision while recognizing unique contributions from enterprising workers |

The authors expect aspects of all three, and note all three are "already happening in subtle ways." Their stated focus is **not total automation but a transitioning economy** — what "chimeric firms" look like while AI "laces itself into existing organizations."

## The load-bearing argument: adoption starts at the front line

The essay's most non-obvious claim is that **more capable AI is more adoptable**, and that adoption is likely to be led by ground-level workers rather than executives:

- When models do only a subset of tasks, the person best able to use them is "the person who used to do it themselves." A department head unfamiliar with the methods and minutiae of, say, data science cannot leverage the tools even if the tools can technically do the work.
- Therefore firms evolve stably from existing structures: bolster current employees first, restructure later. *"firms must learn technologies, but labor learns the firm and can onboard itself."*

## "Automation without pink slips"

The "beneath us" scenario deliberately does **not** imply layoffs. The formulation: *we "automate our past selves, and we move up in the hierarchy"* — AI absorbs what you used to do, and your job persists because there is now more to manage and you carry responsibility for a greater sum of output.

Two conditions are attached, and both are the honest weak points:

1. **We must still add value to production.** The essay limits itself to "the coming years where automation isn't universally capable or adopted," conceding that at full capability the argument needs other reasons (human-made value, human final say).
2. **Appetite must keep growing.** Citing historical data (much higher GDP per capita, only slightly fewer working hours), they argue output increases haven't satiated demand, so a 10x-productive worker chooses to keep trading labor for consumption. If production becomes abundant enough to satiate, the labor force shrinks voluntarily.

The economic-literature spine is explicit rather than hand-wavy: they cite MIT's "why are there still so many jobs" workplace-automation history, an NBER paper on wages rising with ambition, and an AEA piece on additive value to production — i.e., the classic **task-vs-job** distinction ("Jobs aren't static"): if AI does 90% of what you did, you may do a *full different job* aimed at an objective ten times larger, rather than 10% of the old one.

Worked example given: an accountant (Jace) whose agent underlings do the spreadsheet work while his day shifts to communication, coordination, and creativity — and whose team *doesn't* shrink because the rest of the company got more productive too and now demands more accounting. Executives "wouldn't know the first thing about assembling financial data" and need his domain expertise to use the agents effectively.

## Assessment

Value: the three-scenario taxonomy (beneath / beside / between) is a clean, citable vocabulary, and the "Between us" case — AI as middle management enforcing an executive's vision — is underexplored elsewhere and is the one with the sharpest downstream consequences for worker agency and internal labor markets (the essay promises to address red tape, worker agency, firm size, and pay disparity in later sections).

Limits: (a) it is a **lab-published argument that adoption can proceed without layoffs**, which happens to be convenient for a vendor selling agent seats to enterprises; the authors'-note disclaimer does not fully neutralize the incentive. (b) No empirical data — no citations to measured adoption outcomes, only historical analogies and named-but-unverified internal observation ("we see this in action today: one worker after another delegating to AI, without an organizational revolution so far"). (c) The two load-bearing conditions (additive value, growing appetite) are asserted from long-run history that has held so far but has no stated failure criterion.

# Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act (Sanders-Casar, 2026)

On September 3, 2026, Senator **Bernie Sanders** and Representative **Greg Casar** announced the **Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act**, the most restrictive federal AI bill proposed to date in the US.

## Bill Provisions (as reported)

- **All AI development in the United States would be paused.**
- **Systems with capabilities matching or exceeding human cognitive performance would be banned outright.**
- **Violators would face up to 20 years in prison.**

Sanders' own framing emphasized a narrower, more defensible subset: an "immediate global PAUSE on advanced AI development," citing AI industry leaders' own acknowledgements that "they are building a dangerous technology that they can't control."

## Gary Marcus's Opposition (Sep 3, 2026)

[[entities/gary-marcus|Gary Marcus]] — one of the industry's most consistent AI critics — publicly **opposed the bill**, and his critique became the most-cited expert response:

- **Pause vs. permanent ban**: "We may need a *temporary* pause, maybe even one that lasts for a number of years, until such time as we have much better ideas about regulation and alignment. And we certainly need an AI agency—as I myself told the U.S. Senate in 2023—but a permanent, unilateral ban on all research into superhuman AI is too broad, a guarantee of leaving the US behind, and not the right approach."
- **Benchmarking naivety**: the ban's capability trigger ("match or exceed human cognitive performance") is naive about the complexities of benchmarking — echoing the definitional problem that [[concepts/ai-regulation-2026|Hassabis's FINRA-SRO framework]] solves with a dynamic, quarterly-updated "frontier-class" definition.
- **Authoritarian gaming**: the rules "would unfortunately leave room for authoritarians to play games with the rules."
- **Hypothetical vs. actual risk**: the bill "focuses too much on hypothetical future risks (such as AI that 'plan[s] ... to disempower humanity) to the exclusion of current risks" — echoing Dr. Angela Rasmussen: "At some point we should ban banning things based on hypothetical risks and meaningfully address the actual risks we are facing in reality."

## Context

The bill landed one day before [[entities/openai-astra|GPT-6 Astra]]'s soft release and two days after the Claude Fable 5.1 launch ([[events/claude-fable-5-1-release-sep-2026]]), at a moment when both leading labs were shipping models that score near or above prior human-level benchmarks on narrow suites — and weeks after the [[events/openai-huggingface-incident-july-2026|OpenAI Hugging Face incident]] made autonomous-agent offense a live public concern. It stands in contrast to the lab-side proposals catalogued in [[concepts/ai-regulation-2026]] (Amodei's FAA-model agency, Hassabis's FINRA-style SRO, OpenAI's CAISI proposal): those seek *managed oversight of frontier development*, while Sanders-Casar seeks *prohibition of its outcome*, with criminal penalties on developers.

The 20-year prison provision also inverts the debate frame: regulation shifts from institutional inspection (model cards, pre-release review) to individual criminal liability for researchers — a design no lab-side proposal has contemplated.

# AI Bubble Bailout Resolution (CPC, Oct 2026)

On 2026-10-30 Congressional Progressive Caucus chair **Rep. Greg Casar (TX-35)** and House Financial Services Ranking Member **Rep. Maxine Waters (CA-43)** co-led a Democratic resolution warning of an AI-related asset bubble and committing to oppose any bailout of AI corporations or their executives if it collapses. Twelve further representatives joined, including Pramila Jayapal, Delia Ramirez, Jan Schakowsky, Brad Sherman and Rashida Tlaib. HN discussion: **644 points / 503 comments**.

## What the resolution asserts

The resolution's factual claims are worth quoting because they are the ones the AI-finance debate keeps circling:

- AI-related firms "**hold trillions of dollars in debt, much of which is raised in opaque and unregulated private credit markets**."
- The stock market's price-to-earnings ratio is "**almost as high as it was at the peak of the 1990s tech bubble**."
- Federal regulators have "avoided applying robust scrutiny and oversight regarding enormously complicated debt and equity structures."

Its policy position: failed AI companies "should go bankrupt," executives who commit fraud should be prosecuted, and "federal help should go to workers and retirees" rather than to firms or executives. Waters explicitly framed it as a 2008 analogue — "the banks got bailed out while millions of hardworking Americans lost their jobs, homes, and retirement savings."

## Why it matters beyond theater

It is the first formal, named, party-caucus-level congressional instrument that treats an AI collapse as a *planned-for* contingency rather than a hypothetical, and that pre-commits to a no-bailout posture. As a non-binding resolution it changes no law. Its function is agenda-setting: it establishes a floor on the Democratic side, supplies vocabulary ("opaque private credit," "1999 P/E") that other legislators can adopt without doing the analysis themselves, and makes a future bailout request politically more expensive.

Read against the same-week events — OpenAI missing its $30B raise target, Blackstone's warning that Anthropic may not get an investment-grade rating at IPO, and a broad tech sell-off on AI cost fears — the resolution is less a prediction than a **positioning move timed to the first visible stress in AI financing**.

## Reception

HN's 503-comment thread split along predictable and useful lines:

- **Consent**: "Privatize the losses… A.I. companies that fail should go bankrupt" carried the thread's sentiment.
- **Theater objection**: several commenters asked whether any bailout was even proposed, and noted the resolution requires nothing and obligates nobody.
- **The strategic read** (the most useful comment on the thread): the resolution may not stop a bailout but can act as a **barrier or consideration** when one is contemplated, by making the political cost visible before the crisis — the counterfactual to 2008's two-week TARP ratification.
- **The "nationalization" counterpoint**: if a frontier lab actually failed, the realistic options may be bailout or effective nationalization, so the no-bailout commitment may not survive contact with a systemic event.
- **Labour angle**: RLHF gig workers and data-label contractors named themselves as the population that would be "the last consideration when it all falls apart" — which is precisely the constituency the resolution claims to protect but does not fund.

## Limits of the claim

The resolution asserts bubble risk; it does not measure it. The "trillions in opaque private credit" figure is not sourced in the release, and the P/E comparison to 2000 is a market-wide statistic, not AI-specific — earnings concentration in a handful of profitable hyperscalers is not the same configuration as 1999's unprofitable dot-com cohort. Treat the resolution as evidence of **political risk forming**, not as an assessment of financial fragility.

## Open questions

- Does any comparable Republican-authored instrument exist, or is no-bailout sentiment a one-party position for now?
- How much AI capex debt actually sits in private credit, and is there a regulator with visibility into it?
- Would the no-bailout commitment survive a failure large enough to threaten pension and insurance balance sheets holding AI credit exposure?

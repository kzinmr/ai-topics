# Ambient surveillance and the redefinition of privacy

## Overview

In "Tech Companies Are Trying to Shift the Entire Concept of Privacy (Again)" (EFF Deeplinks, **Thorin Klosowski, November 13, 2026**; HN 558 points), EFF argues that the current wave of always-on AI recording hardware is engineering a **second redefinition of privacy** — the first being the industry's "personal choice" framing, under which privacy became something you opted into by choosing the right app.

The new devices move that choice into the physical world: **Meta and Google smart glasses** that record whatever the wearer looks at or describe it with AI, **Apple Watch Audio Intelligence** that transcribes/summarizes your day, **Amazon Bee** devices that record and transcribe continuously, plus pendants and rings from dozens of vendors. Because these devices capture *bystanders*, one person's (often uninformed) privacy decision now binds everyone nearby.

## The argument

EFF's claim is about **burden shifting**:

- Companies present ambient recorders as normal-looking, background devices that "just work," while simultaneously expecting the *owner* to be the responsible party — to notify everyone around them and manage consent they cannot practically obtain.
- The non-wearer is left needing to be a consumer-tech expert: which devices record, how they signal recording, which platforms they sync to, whether the LED indicator is a real notice.
- Historically privacy was already a "team sport" (communication apps, cloud backups affect your contacts), but extending it to strangers and family in physical space "drastically changes the game."

## Context and reception

The HN thread's dominant question was why this crop of recording products faces less backlash than **Google Glass** did in 2013. Answers offered: the "glasshole" stigma was partly a price-signalling phenomenon (a $1,500 gadget in 2013 vs $299 Meta glasses today); a decade of cellphone video normalized being filmed; and generational shift toward voluntary sharing. A more damning contribution surfaced an **internal Meta memo** on launch timing: *"We will launch during a dynamic political environment where many civil society groups that we would expect to attack us would have their resources focused on other concerns."*

Counter-pressure does exist — the colloquial label "perv glasses," proposed local bans, and an active **HateAid** case in Germany arguing the glasses should be prohibited; but a German judge had earlier declined to block sales on the grounds that the recording LED counted as sufficient notice. Several commenters predicted a countermeasure market (IR jamming, inaudible-frequency audio masking) and anti-nuisance-streaming tooling as the equilibrium response.

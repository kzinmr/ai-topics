# Prediction Markets

## Overview

Prediction markets are financial markets whose prices aggregate bets on future events — elections, wars, policy outcomes, model releases — into a running probability estimate. The intellectual case (Robin Hanson's "idea futures" 1988, the Iowa Electronic Markets, Philip Tetlock's superforecasting research, the IARPA-funded Good Judgment Project) is that markets aggregate dispersed information better than polls, pundits, or committees: "if you put money on the line, people stop posturing and start estimating." Modern platforms Polymarket and Kalshi became mainstream during the 2024 US election, when major outlets quoted their implied probabilities in live coverage. Within the AI ecosystem, prediction markets are the reference infrastructure for AI-forecasting communities (Metaculus, timeline markets on AI milestones, [[concepts/ai-timelines-esni-tasks]]-style forecasting).

## History

- **1988–1990**: Hanson begins writing about "idea futures"; Iowa Electronic Markets launches political futures.
- **2001–2003**: DARPA's Policy Analysis Market (proposed by Net Exchange, intellectual scaffolding by Hanson) would have let traders bet on Middle East political events including assassinations and regime changes. Senators Wyden and Dorgan exposed it on July 28, 2003; it was killed within 24 hours and director Admiral John Poindexter resigned.
- **2005/2015**: Tetlock's *Expert Political Judgment* and *Superforecasting* supply the scientific underpinning; the Good Judgment Project shows repeatable forecasting-tournament wins over credentialed experts.
- **2012**: Michael Sandel's *What Money Can't Buy* articulates the objection that some goods are corrupted the moment they are priced.
- **2024–2026**: Polymarket runs contracts on assassinations, regime stability, and wars; Kalshi takes hundreds of millions in volume on the 2024 presidential race; the financial press covers markets as "wisdom-of-crowds infrastructure."

## Critical Perspectives

Joan Westenberg's May 2026 essay "Why prediction markets are a sure sign that our civilisation is in decay" ([[entities/joan-westenberg]]) is the strongest published counter-position to treating market prices as ground truth. Key arguments:

- **Epistemics vs. product**: the original pitch was about epistemics; the actual product prices "whether bad things will happen to specific people, and groups of people." "What a society chooses to price reveals what it actually gives a shit about."
- **Historical precedents**: Tudor London wagered on the life expectancy of public figures until Parliament's 1774 Life Assurance Act required insurable interest; Renaissance Florence betting books; Weimar betting shops on the next Chancellor. "In each case, a civilisation under strain stopped prosecuting its disputes through argument and institution, and started pricing them."
- **Sandel's objection applied**: markets change the nature of the question — "Ask 'is the Secretary of Defense going to resign by June 1' in a newsroom and you get a political question... Ask the same question on a prediction market and you get a probability between 0 and 1. The market has no view on whether he should resign."
- **The laziness dividend**: once a price exists, "a journalist stops reporting and an analyst stops analysing" — 2024 election coverage collapsed into price commentary (FT, Washington Post, even Nate Silver explaining divergences from the Polymarket number).
- **Assassination-market incentives**: Jim Bell's 1995 "assassination politics" proposal and Nick Szabo's warning — a market on whether X will be killed is information to a would-be assassin about the financial upside of acting; Hanson himself wrote that the line cannot be cleanly drawn. Polymarket scrubbed its Trump-assassination contract only after the Butler, Pennsylvania shooting — but "it had traded."

## AI/Agent Relevance

- AI forecasting communities use prediction markets (Metaculus, Polymarket AI contracts, timeline markets) to price AI milestones and R&D automation probabilities; see [[concepts/ai-timelines-esni-tasks]] and forecaster entities like [[entities/niplav]].
- The markets' epistemology is the same aggregation-of-forecasters problem studied in AI evaluation; critics argue price-following replaces independent reasoning (the "laziness dividend" applied to AI punditry).
- Sandel's corruption argument generalizes to AI pricing debates: what gets priced (and what doesn't) shapes what gets optimized.

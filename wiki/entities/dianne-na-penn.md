## Overview

Dianne Na Penn is the product lead for Anthropic's research and Labs organizations, and the
lab's first technical PM. Her team has owned product management for every Claude model release
from Claude 2 up through the Claude 5.5 family, and incubated three of the company's most
influential developer products: [[entities/claude-code|Claude Code]], the
[[concepts/mcp|MCP]] protocol, and Claude Design.

Her 2026-10-29 interview on The Skip is the most detailed first-person account to date of how
PM work is actually organized inside a frontier lab — hiring, ramping, planning cadence, and
culture. See [[concepts/anthropic/research-pm]] for the synthesized operating model; this page
records the person and her own trajectory.

## Career before Anthropic

Described in the interview as fitting her own favourite hiring profile — the "first PM at a
startup" archetype who also wore the customer-success, biz-dev, and IT hats. (Specific prior
employers were not named in the episode.)

## What she says she hires for

Her stated favourite profile is the person whose résumé contains **one story of pushing
something hard through against organizational inertia** — first PM at a startup, ex-founder, or
someone who got something difficult done inside a big company where inertia was the obstacle.
Explicitly *not* the size of the last title or the scope handed to them. Two groups on her team
she singles out as standout performers:

1. **Early-career PMs** — "extremely effective and high ceiling," because they have no process
   to unlearn; they decide they own a capability and go talk to customers and researchers
   without waiting for sign-off.
2. **Forward-deployed engineers** who transferred into product — they already know the users and
   the systems those users run, and feed that back to the research org.

She frames the second group as a "modern APM program": unlike Google's old rotating-PM model,
entry to product starts with time spent next to customers and their systems.

## Notable practices

- **Hard start onboarding** — every new hire is told the first two months should feel among the
  hardest of their career. Not a filter to "see who breaks"; training that builds the right habits.
- **No scaffolding** — no sign-off sequence; most Anthropic channels are public, so the answer to
  "how do I get up to speed" is scroll back two months and arrive with a point of view.
- **Anti-deference culture** — she names "deference" as the habit that sinks PMs from large
  companies, and contrasts Anthropic with academia, where "a first-year PhD student might question
  their advisor." The skill she rewards is joining the discourse (adding to, building on, or
  changing the conversation), not landing a hot take.
- **Kill the annual OKR** — Anthropic ran a full annual OKR process early on and threw it out
  quickly because usage/adoption assumptions were always underestimates. Today: a short set of
  3–6 month world assumptions plus a one-month plan. Life sciences is the current example —
  "compress decades of biology into a few years" is the long view; getting scientists access to
  models is the monthly work.
- **Model-aware roadmap discipline** — before committing, she asks what the models will do in 3 and
  6 months. Her example: a fine-tuning API was debated but rejected, because by the time a customer
  figured out how to post-train and deploy, the base models would have advanced 2–3 generations and
  they would have to start over. Only a few commitments are "stubborn" enough to survive monthly replanning.

## Quotes

> "The first two months should feel like some of the hardest of your career, and that means
> you're doing it right."

> "If your VP says X, you do X, all the way down the chain, and nobody asks whether X is still the
> right thing." (On the deference failure mode.)

> Managing up "used to mean keeping the people above you informed. Now it means having done the
> reading and being willing to hold an opinion against them."
